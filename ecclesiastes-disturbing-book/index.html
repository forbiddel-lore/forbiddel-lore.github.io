Full script

In Jerusalem, under Ptolemaic rule, a young apprentice sat beside a lamp and copied a scroll.

The lamp burned low on a stand near the wall. The apprentice bent over the writing surface and followed the lines set before him. His teacher had shown him how to prepare the pen, how to keep the letters even, how to leave enough space between words for another reader to distinguish them. A mistake could spoil a line. A damaged sheet could cost time and material. The work demanded attention.

At the top of the scroll stood the opening words of Ecclesiastes.

The King James Version later rendered them: "Vanity of vanities, saith the Preacher, vanity of vanities; all is vanity." The Hebrew word behind "vanity" was hebel. It could suggest breath, vapor, mist, or something fleeting and insubstantial. The apprentice copied the phrase, letter by letter, while the meaning gathered around it.

All was hebel.

The statement did not sound like the familiar language of reward. It did not promise that wisdom would always prosper, that the righteous would visibly triumph, or that the world would display a clear moral order. The scroll spoke of labor, wealth, knowledge, pleasure, justice, and death. It examined each one and found that none could secure a lasting advantage.

The generations came and went, but the earth remained.

The sun rose and hurried toward its setting, only to rise again. The wind moved south and turned north, circling and returning. Rivers ran into the sea, yet the sea did not fill. The eye was not satisfied with seeing. The ear was not filled with hearing. What had been would be again. What had been done would be done again.

There was nothing new under the sun.

The apprentice had been taught that wisdom was precious. Ecclesiastes agreed, at first. Wisdom surpassed folly as light surpassed darkness. The wise person had eyes in the head, while the fool walked in darkness. Yet both reached the same end. The wise died. The fool died. Memory faded. The work of one person passed to another who had not labored for it. The righteous and the wicked could meet the same fate.

The text did not resolve the difficulty by denying death. It placed death at the center.

It also refused to offer certain knowledge about what came afterward. The dead went to Sheol, the realm of the dead. The living knew that they would die. The dead, in the language of the book, knew nothing of what happened under the sun. The boundary could be named, but it could not be crossed by human observation.

The imagined apprentice might have looked toward his teacher. He might have wondered whether the words were meant to be accepted, argued with, or simply preserved. Ecclesiastes entered the scriptural tradition while sounding unlike some other biblical writings about wisdom, justice, and the fate of the righteous.

Its speaker called himself Qoheleth, a Hebrew title often understood as "one who gathers" or "the assembler." The Greek Septuagint translated the title as Ecclesiastes, a name connected with an assembly. Later readers associated the speaker with Solomon because the book opens by describing him as a son of David and king in Jerusalem. The voice speaks as a ruler with extraordinary wealth, building projects, servants, gardens, possessions, and access to every form of pleasure.

Yet the book never names Solomon directly.

Instead, a literary king speaks from a position of almost unlimited experience. He tests wisdom. He tests pleasure. He builds. He gathers wealth. He observes courts, laborers, fools, mourners, lovers, and rulers. He watches the seasons turn and sees every human project pressed against the fact of death.

Ecclesiastes is not a single argument stated once. It is a journey through competing judgments. Work is futile, yet work can be enjoyed. Wisdom is better than folly, yet wisdom cannot prevent death. God has ordered time, yet human beings cannot discover the whole pattern. Justice exists as an expectation, yet injustice stands openly in the courts. The book's final words urge fear of God and obedience to commandments, but the voice that leads there has spent twelve chapters exposing the limits of human certainty.

That tension helps explain the book's long afterlife.

Jewish readers preserved it, debated its place among sacred writings, and eventually read it alongside other books in the Hebrew Bible. Christian translators carried it into Greek and Latin. Jerome included it in the Vulgate. Martin Luther translated and commented on it. Leo Tolstoy placed its questions beside the crisis described in A Confession. Modern scholars continue to disagree about its unity, its date, its speaker, and the relation between its skeptical observations and its closing judgment.

The book has no surviving author's biography. No contemporary inscription identifies Qoheleth. No ancient witness explains the precise circumstances of composition. The evidence comes from the Hebrew text, its language, its manuscript fragments, its ancient translations, and the history of how communities received it.

What does Ecclesiastes actually say from its first line to its last? Who wrote it, and when? Why did a work so sharply at odds with easy claims about reward and justice enter the Bible? Why has it unsettled readers for two thousand years?

To answer, we must go back to the manuscripts, the language, and the voice called Qoheleth.

The oldest physical witnesses do not begin with a complete scroll.

They begin with fragments.

Among the Dead Sea Scrolls found at Qumran, fragments of Ecclesiastes were recovered from Cave Four. The pieces are damaged, separated, and incomplete, but they establish that the book circulated in Hebrew during the period when the Qumran communities preserved and copied Jewish writings. The fragments belong among the older biblical materials from the caves, though their precise dates and the history of each manuscript remain matters for specialist study.

A fragment does not tell its reader who first composed a book. It tells something narrower and firmer. Ecclesiastes existed as a Hebrew text before the surviving medieval manuscripts by many centuries. Its words had entered the stream of Jewish textual transmission by the late Second Temple period.

The cave preserved no author's signature.

The Hebrew text itself carries the next evidence. Its language contains features that most scholars regard as relatively late within biblical Hebrew. It also includes Persian loanwords. Such terms do not provide a single exact year, because words can travel across regions and remain in use after political circumstances change. They do place the composition in a broad historical setting.

Most scholars date Ecclesiastes somewhere between the fifth and the third centuries before the common era. Some place it closer to the later part of that range, often in the Hellenistic period. The book's linguistic character, its intellectual setting, and its relation to other wisdom traditions all enter the discussion.

The opening line offers a different kind of clue.

It presents the speaker as "the words of the Preacher, the son of David, king in Jerusalem." In the Hebrew, the title is Qoheleth. The Septuagint rendered it with the Greek name Ecclesiastes, and that name passed through later traditions. Qoheleth may mean one who gathers, one who addresses an assembly, or an assembler. Its exact force is debated, but the title describes a public speaker rather than a private diarist.

The son of David and king in Jerusalem appears to be Solomon. The book does not name him. It uses the royal figure as a voice through which the inquiry can reach its furthest extent. A king who possesses wisdom, wealth, servants, buildings, gardens, music, and sexual access can test the possibilities of human fulfillment more completely than an ordinary laborer could.

That is why most scholars read the Solomonic setting as a literary persona rather than a record written by Solomon himself. Solomon is said to have ruled in the tenth century before the common era. The language of Ecclesiastes points much later. The Persian loanwords and late features do not fit a straightforward tenth-century date, and the book's concerns belong to a world in which older Israelite traditions had encountered Persian and Hellenistic cultural environments.

The distinction matters.

The text says that the speaker is a son of David and king in Jerusalem. The historical evidence does not establish that Solomon composed the book. The literary voice borrows the authority of the ideal wise king. It then uses that authority to examine the limits of wisdom itself.

The book moved into Greek.

The Septuagint was not one single translation produced at one moment by one known team. It was a collection of Greek translations made over time for Jewish communities, especially in the eastern Mediterranean. Ecclesiastes entered that Greek stream under the name Ecclesiastes. Its Greek wording influenced later readers, although the Hebrew remains central for reconstructing the book's original phrasing.

Centuries later, Jerome translated the Hebrew Scriptures into Latin. His work became the Vulgate. Jerome's Latin title, Ecclesiastes, helped fix the name in Christian textual tradition. The Vulgate did not erase the earlier history. It became another layer through which the text was copied, read, interpreted, and transmitted.

Between the Hebrew, Greek, and Latin witnesses, small differences appear. Translators make choices. A Hebrew image can become more abstract in another language. A word that suggests breath or vapor can be rendered as vanity, futility, emptiness, or meaninglessness. Each choice directs the reader toward a different emotional center.

The manuscript evidence is also uneven.

The fragments from Qumran do not preserve every line. The medieval Hebrew manuscripts are much later than the composition of the book, although they transmit a substantially coherent text. Ancient translations sometimes assist comparison, but a translation is not a transparent duplicate of its Hebrew source. It reflects interpretation as well as language.

The people who preserved Ecclesiastes did not leave a statement explaining why they valued it. Its survival is visible in objects: fragments from the Judean desert, later Hebrew codices, Greek copies, Latin manuscripts, and printed editions. Each witness carries the book forward without resolving its authorial mystery.

The book's place among Jewish sacred writings was not always treated as self-evident. Later rabbinic discussions record debate about books that could appear to conflict with themselves or with other teachings. Ecclesiastes was read in relation to the Mishnah and to traditions associated with figures such as Hillel, Shammai, and Rabbi Akiva, though these later sources do not provide a direct report from the period of composition. They show that questions about the book's status and meaning continued after its language had become ancient.

Some Jewish readers associated the book with Solomon's final years. That tradition supplied a moral frame: a king who had pursued wisdom, pleasure, and power could look back and pronounce on their limits. The tradition is later than the composition of the Hebrew text. It is not evidence that Solomon wrote it.

The historical record leaves a narrow but durable outline.

A Hebrew work called Qoheleth circulated in the Second Temple period. Its language places it late in the history of biblical Hebrew. Its title became Ecclesiastes in Greek and Latin traditions. Its speaker adopted the shape of a Davidic king, while the author remained unnamed. Later communities preserved the work even as they debated its tone and purpose.

Michael V. Fox argues that the book's repeated claim about hebel does not simply mean that everything is meaningless. In his reading, the term points toward the elusive, frustrating, and transient character of human experience. Choon-Leong Seow likewise treats the book as a complex work whose tensions should not be flattened into a single doctrine.

Before those modern arguments, the text itself must be heard.

It begins with a declaration about vapor, breath, and repetition. From there, the sun rises, the wind turns, and the question of human gain enters the room.

The first line came without preparation.

"The words of the Preacher, the son of David, king in Jerusalem."

Then the judgment followed: "Vanity of vanities, saith the Preacher, vanity of vanities; all is vanity."

The King James Version used "vanity." The Hebrew word was hebel. It could name a breath that disappears as soon as it leaves the mouth. It could suggest mist over water, vapor above the ground, or something too insubstantial to hold. The word did not necessarily mean that nothing existed. It described the instability and elusiveness of what human beings tried to grasp.

The speaker placed the word over the entire world.

What profit had a person from all the labor performed under the sun?

The question moved outward from one life to the generations. A generation went, and another came, but the earth remained. The human sequence changed while the ground endured. The words did not celebrate continuity. They pressed against the scale of human effort. Each person labored inside a brief interval, while the earth outlasted the name and work of that person.

The sun rose.

It moved across the sky and hurried toward the place where it would set. Then it returned to rise again. The movement seemed immense, but it also seemed repetitive. Morning followed night. Night followed morning. The sun's journey had no visible conclusion.

The wind turned next. It traveled toward the south and circled toward the north. It turned and turned again. The same motion returned upon itself, as though the world had movement without arrival.

The rivers ran toward the sea.

They poured into it, but the sea was not full. The streams continued toward the place where water gathered, and from there the cycle began again. The language of Ecclesiastes made nature active but not progressive. The rivers moved, yet nothing was completed. The sun traveled, yet did not reach a final destination. The wind circled, yet did not escape its course.

Human beings entered the same pattern.

The eye was not satisfied with seeing. The ear was not filled with hearing. Sight and sound could accumulate without producing completion. More experience did not create rest. The appetite for perception remained open.

The speaker then made the claim that would govern the first chapter.

That which had been was what would be. That which had been done was what would be done. There was nothing new under the sun.

The sentence did not deny that objects could be invented or that events could differ in their details. It spoke about human patterns. Ambition, labor, pleasure, conflict, and death returned in new forms. The names changed. The underlying conditions did not.

The speaker considered fame.

There was no remembrance of former people, and those who came later would not be remembered by those who followed them. Memory, which seemed to offer a defense against death, was itself temporary. A person could build a name, but the name depended on other minds, and those minds would also disappear.

The opening declaration had become an investigation.

Qoheleth identified himself as having been king over Israel in Jerusalem. He set his heart to seek and search out by wisdom all things done under heaven. The task was difficult. God had given human beings an occupation, or burden, to be exercised in the world.

The speaker looked at what was done under the sun.

All the works could be seen, but the total pattern could not be repaired or completed by human understanding. What was crooked could not simply be made straight. What was lacking could not be counted as though counting would supply it.

The investigation turned inward. The speaker said that he had enlarged and increased wisdom beyond those who had been before him in Jerusalem. His heart had experienced great wisdom and knowledge. He had applied himself to know wisdom and madness and folly.

The result was not triumph.

He learned that this too was a striving after wind. The King James Version rendered the phrase "vexation of spirit." The image was difficult. It could suggest chasing wind, feeding on wind, or grasping at wind. In every case, the pursuit ended with something that could not be held.

The reason came next.

In much wisdom was much grief. The more knowledge increased, the more sorrow increased.

The first chapter therefore moved in a widening circle. It began with all things and returned to the person who tried to understand them. The earth remained. The sun repeated its course. The wind turned. The rivers flowed. Human beings sought satisfaction through sight, hearing, memory, and knowledge. None of these abolished the instability named by hebel.

The word has shaped the book's entire afterlife.

"Vanity" gives the opening a moral and psychological tone. It can sound like pride, emptiness, or the hollowness of worldly ambition. "Meaningless" makes the claim more absolute, as though the book denies significance itself. "Breath" preserves the physical image but can understate the frustration. "Vapor" suggests something visible and real that cannot be grasped.

Michael V. Fox argues that hebel should not be reduced to "meaningless." For Fox, the word marks what is fleeting, enigmatic, and resistant to control. Choon-Leong Seow also emphasizes that the book's repeated term cannot be separated from the situations in which it appears. Labor is hebel because its results pass to another. Pleasure is hebel because death interrupts it. Wisdom is hebel because wisdom cannot prevent death or explain every event.

The first chapter does not yet offer the command to enjoy food, drink, and work. It has not reached the royal experiment or the poem about time. It has only established the field in which those later judgments will operate.

The sun rose, crossed the sky, and returned.

The earth received the rivers without filling.

The human mind reached toward wisdom and found the wind.

The royal experiment began with pleasure.

The speaker said that he would test mirth and discover what was good. Laughter appeared as madness. Pleasure appeared as a possible answer, but the speaker did not accept it before examining it.

He turned to wine.

His heart guided him with wisdom, he said, while he held himself back from folly. He wanted to know what was good for human beings to do during the few days of life. Wine could be tested as part of the experiment, but it could not be allowed to govern the investigator. Pleasure was placed under observation.

Then the building began.

The speaker made great works. He built houses for himself. He planted vineyards. He made gardens and orchards. He planted trees of every kind of fruit. He constructed pools of water to irrigate the trees. The royal persona became a landscape of controlled abundance: buildings, cultivated ground, water, and living things arranged by command.

The work required people.

The speaker acquired male and female servants. He had servants born within his house. He possessed great herds and flocks, more than anyone who had been before him in Jerusalem. The scale of the experiment grew with every addition. Wealth was not considered in the abstract. It was accumulated as property, labor, animals, land, and buildings.

Silver and gold came next.

The treasures of kings and provinces entered the royal store. Male and female singers were present. The text also mentions the delights of the sons of men, including a large number of concubines in the King James Version. The Hebrew wording at this point is difficult, and translations differ in how they represent the final measure. The scene remains clear in its purpose: the speaker had access to forms of pleasure, status, music, sexuality, and possession that ordinary lives could not command.

He became great.

He exceeded all who had been before him in Jerusalem. His wisdom remained with him. The royal voice did not describe a collapse into uncontrolled appetite. It claimed that the experiment had been planned and observed by an intellect that wanted to know whether pleasure could produce lasting gain.

The eyes received everything they desired.

The speaker did not withhold pleasure from them. His heart took pleasure in all his labor, and this was the portion received from that labor. For a moment, the royal experiment seemed to produce an answer. Work created possessions. Possessions created pleasure. Pleasure could be experienced in the present.

Then the speaker looked again.

He considered all that his hands had done and the labor he had taken to perform it. Everything was hebel and a striving after wind. There was no lasting profit under the sun.

The word returned like the breath at the opening.

The speaker did not deny that the houses existed, that the gardens bore trees, that the pools held water, or that pleasure had occurred. The judgment concerned what those achievements could finally secure. They could produce experience. They could not defeat time.

The inquiry shifted from pleasure to wisdom.

Wisdom surpassed folly as light surpassed darkness. The wise person had eyes in the head. The fool walked in darkness. The speaker recognized an advantage. Wisdom could perceive what folly could not. It could guide decisions, expose danger, and make the world more intelligible.

Yet one event happened to the wise and the fool alike.

The speaker asked himself why he had become so wise. What advantage could wisdom finally provide if the same fate awaited the wise person and the fool? The memory of both would fade. In days to come, all would be forgotten. How did the wise person die? Like the fool.

The royal voice reached a sharper conclusion.

Therefore he hated life.

The hatred did not arise because life contained no pleasure. It arose because the work done under the sun was grievous to him. Everything was hebel and a striving after wind. The work could be great, intricate, and beautiful, but death made its final ownership unstable.

The speaker hated all his labor because he would leave it to the person who came after him. That successor might be wise or foolish. The person who had labored could not control the judgment of the one who inherited the result.

The inheritance problem disturbed the king more than failure would have done.

A person could work with wisdom, knowledge, and skill, then leave the result to someone who had done nothing to earn it. The successor would possess the fields, houses, treasures, or authority. The original laborer would be gone.

This too was hebel.

The speaker asked what a person received from all the labor and striving of the heart performed under the sun. The days contained sorrow. The occupation brought grief. At night the heart did not rest.

Then the language turned.

There was nothing better for a person than to eat, drink, and let the soul enjoy good in labor. The speaker said that this also came from the hand of God. He asked who could eat or enjoy apart from God. To the person who pleased God, God gave wisdom, knowledge, and joy. To the sinner, God gave the task of gathering and collecting so that the result might be handed to the one who pleased God.

The conclusion repeated the governing term.

This too was hebel and a striving after wind.

The book held the judgments together without smoothing them into one mood. Pleasure failed as a permanent answer, but pleasure in the present could still be received. Labor could not secure the future, but labor could contain a portion of joy. Wisdom surpassed folly, but wisdom did not escape death. The king hated life because its gains could not be retained, yet he could still name eating, drinking, and work as gifts from God.

The Solomon persona served this experiment well.

The text never says that Solomon performed these acts. It presents a speaker whose identity resembles the great Davidic king and whose resources exceed ordinary human limits. Tremper Longman III argues that the royal voice functions as a literary device, allowing Ecclesiastes to examine the search for meaning through the figure of a king who can try everything. After the second chapter, the explicit royal frame recedes. The speaker continues to judge wisdom, work, pleasure, oppression, and death, but the narrative no longer depends on a detailed account of Solomon's court.

The persona had done its work.

A king had tested laughter, wine, buildings, vineyards, gardens, water, servants, herds, silver, gold, singers, and sexual pleasure. A king had compared wisdom with folly. A king had faced the death shared by both.

The experiment did not produce a single answer. It produced a boundary.

Human beings could enjoy what arrived in the present. They could not command what happened after their labor ended.

The third chapter opened with a poem about time.

For everything there was a season, and a time for every purpose under heaven. The lines moved through paired opposites, one after another, as though human life were held inside a calendar too large to read.

A time to be born, and a time to die.

A time to plant, and a time to uproot what had been planted.

A time to kill, and a time to heal.

A time to break down, and a time to build up.

A time to weep, and a time to laugh.

A time to mourn, and a time to dance.

A time to cast away stones, and a time to gather stones together.

A time to embrace, and a time to refrain from embracing.

A time to gain, and a time to lose.

A time to keep, and a time to cast away.

A time to tear, and a time to sew.

A time to keep silence, and a time to speak.

A time to love, and a time to hate.

A time of war, and a time of peace.

The poem did not assign each moment to a named person. It did not say that every event was morally equal. It set human actions and conditions inside the larger movement of time. Birth and death stood at the edges. Planting and uprooting belonged to the earth. Speech, silence, love, hatred, war, and peace belonged to the life of communities.

The speaker then asked what profit workers had from their labor. He had seen the occupation given to human beings. God had made everything beautiful in its time. Yet God had also placed a sense of eternity in the human heart, so that no person could find out the work God had done from beginning to end.

The poem's order did not grant human beings access to the whole order.

The seasons could be named. The pattern could be felt. The complete design remained beyond discovery. Human beings sensed duration greater than their own lives, but they could not survey divine activity from its beginning to its end.

The speaker returned to the present.

He knew that there was nothing better for people than to rejoice and do good while they lived. He knew that every person should eat, drink, and enjoy the good of labor. This was the gift of God. The work of God would endure. Nothing could be added to it or taken from it. God acted so that people would fear before him.

What had been was present already. What was to be had already been. God sought what had passed by.

The language then entered the courts.

The speaker saw injustice in the place of judgment. Wickedness stood where justice should have been. He said in his heart that God would judge the righteous and the wicked, because there was a time there for every purpose and every work.

The poem had described time as ordered. The observation of the courts revealed disorder within that order. The speaker did not explain the contradiction. He recorded both: a world with appointed seasons and a world in which judgment itself could contain wickedness.

He considered human beings.

God had shown them that they were like animals. The fate of human beings and the fate of animals was the same. As one died, so died the other. All had one breath. Human beings had no advantage over animals, for all was hebel.

The claim was physical and stark.

All went to one place. All came from dust, and all returned to dust. The sentence reached back to the creation language of Genesis, but Ecclesiastes used it to press human beings into the same material cycle as other creatures.

The speaker then asked who knew whether the spirit of human beings went upward and whether the spirit of animals went downward into the earth. The King James Version rendered the line as, "Who knoweth the spirit of man that goeth upward, and the spirit of the beast that goeth downward to the earth?" The text did not provide an observation that settled the question. It placed the uncertainty inside the argument.

The speaker saw that there was nothing better than for a person to rejoice in works, because that was the person's portion. Who could bring anyone to see what would come after?

The chapter therefore held two movements together. Time had an order associated with God, yet the whole order could not be known. Human beings could enjoy their labor, yet they shared death with animals. The world could contain moments appointed for war and peace, planting and uprooting, weeping and laughter, while the courts still displayed injustice.

The poem later traveled far beyond its original setting.

Its sequence of paired times became one of the most recognizable passages in Ecclesiastes. Jewish and Christian readers used it in sermons, commentaries, and liturgical reflection. In the twentieth century, the lines entered popular song through Pete Seeger's composition "Turn! Turn! Turn!", recorded by The Byrds in nineteen sixty five. That later song rearranged the passage and added a refrain, but the order of the biblical poem remained recognizable.

The poem's afterlife often softened its setting. Detached from the surrounding discussion, it could sound like a calm statement that every event had its proper place. Within Ecclesiastes, the poem is less settled. It is followed by the inability to know the complete work of God, the sight of injustice, and the claim that human and animal mortality converge.

Robert Alter emphasizes the power of the poem's formal balance while retaining its place within the book's larger meditation on human limitation. The repeated structure gives each pair weight. Birth stands beside death. Building stands beside breaking. War stands beside peace. The form suggests order, but the surrounding prose refuses to turn that order into a simple promise that every human question will be answered.

The text's wording is known through the Hebrew manuscript tradition, compared where possible with ancient translations and the fragments from Qumran. The poem is not a modern insertion, and its central sequence is stable across the major textual witnesses. Translation still affects its sound. "A time to kill" can be heard as a general description of mortal history, while another translation may choose "a time to tear down" or a related phrase where the Hebrew permits different nuance.

The chapter ended not with a solution to death but with a portion assigned to the living.

A person could rejoice in work. A person could eat, drink, and receive what labor offered. The future remained beyond sight.

The poem had named every season, but it had not opened the whole design.

That is where Part Three ends.

The next words turn from the solitary search for wisdom toward the people standing beneath power. Ecclesiastes says that oppression exists under the sun. Tears fall, and there is no comforter. The hand of the oppressor is stronger than the hand of the oppressed.

Qoheleth says that he looked upon the dead and judged them happier than the living, because the dead had already passed beyond the injuries of the world. Then he judged the unborn happier than both, because they had not yet seen the evil done under the sun.

The statement is severe, but the text does not soften it. It does not present death as a reward in a later world. It describes release from observation. The unborn have not entered the field in which violence, ambition, and grief can be witnessed.

Then Qoheleth saw another kind of misery. A person worked alone. There was no second person beside him, no child to inherit the result, and no end to his labor. His eyes did not rest from wealth, yet he never asked whom the wealth would serve. The text gives his imagined speech: he had no companion, no child, and still he denied himself the good of life.

The lonely worker is not condemned for laziness. His fault is that work has become detached from relationship. The hand remains busy, but the labor has lost its answer.

Qoheleth states the principle in a line that later readers often remembered: two are better than one, because they have a good reward for their labor. If one falls, the other can help him up. If two lie together, they have warmth. A single person may be overcome, but two can resist, and a threefold cord is not quickly broken.

The passage moves through practical images rather than abstract doctrine. A companion raises the fallen. Companions share warmth. Companions withstand an attacker. The strength lies in mutual presence.

Then the scene shifts to a kingdom. Qoheleth describes a poor and wise youth who rises from prison to reign, while an old king remains in power but has ceased to receive correction. The younger figure is poor, yet his wisdom gives him movement. The older figure is wealthy and established, yet his refusal to listen makes his position fragile.

The text does not name either ruler. It does not attach the story to a known dynasty. The point lies in the reversal: a youth can rise, and an old king can lose the capacity to govern because he will not accept advice.

The people who follow the new ruler are said to be without number. Yet the text immediately places a limit on popularity. Those who come later will not rejoice in him. The crowd gathers around one ruler, then turns toward another. Even political success is subject to the passing of generations.

From the palace, the text enters the temple. Qoheleth warns against approaching God carelessly. A person should guard the steps when going to the house of God and should draw near to hear rather than to offer the sacrifice of fools. The fool does not know that he is doing evil.

The warning concerns speech. The text says that a person should not be quick with the mouth or hasty in the heart to utter words before God. God is in heaven, and the speaker is on earth. Therefore words should be few.

A dream can come through much activity, and a fool's voice can be recognized through many words. The text does not describe silence as a ritual achievement. It describes restraint as an answer to the distance between human beings and God.

Then Qoheleth turns to vows. When a person makes a vow, he should not delay in fulfilling it. God has no pleasure in fools. A vow should be paid. Better not to vow than to vow and fail to pay.

The passage does not give a procedure for making a vow. It gives a warning about speech that becomes obligation. A careless promise cannot be repaired by multiplying explanations. The words have already created a demand.

Qoheleth also warns against allowing the mouth to cause the flesh to sin, and against telling a messenger that the vow was a mistake. The text asks why God should be angered by the speaker's voice and why the work of his hands should be destroyed.

The argument then returns to dreams and many words. In the multitude of dreams and words there are also many vanities. The final command is simple: fear God.

This fear is not explained as a complete system of doctrine. It is the posture that remains after speech has been reduced. Qoheleth has questioned labor, power, wealth, and even the value of life. Here he questions religious performance when it becomes verbal excess.

The text then returns to wealth. If a person sees the oppression of the poor and the violent denial of judgment and justice in a province, he should not be surprised. A high official watches another official, and higher officials watch them both. Above the officials stands the interest of the land and the benefit of cultivation.

The wording gives no portrait of a just bureaucracy. It describes a chain of authority in which one level watches another, while the system remains tied to agricultural production. The poor are not protected by the existence of offices. The structure of offices can itself become part of the problem.

Qoheleth says that the increase of a land belongs to all, and that a king is served by the field. The line is compressed, and translations differ in how they connect the clauses. Its setting remains agricultural: political power rests on land and on the labor extracted from it.

Then comes another observation. Whoever loves silver will not be satisfied with silver, nor whoever loves abundance with increase. This too is hebel. When goods increase, those who consume them also increase. The owner sees the wealth, but the wealth itself does not become nourishment.

The sleep of a working person is sweet, whether the worker eats little or much. The abundance of the rich does not permit rest. Riches can be kept to the harm of their owner, and can disappear through bad business. A child may be born, yet there may be nothing left to place in that child's hand.

Qoheleth describes a person who comes from the womb naked and departs as he came. Nothing from the labor can be carried away. This is a direct echo of the book's earlier insistence that death leaves the worker without possession of the results.

The person is said to eat in darkness, with much sorrow, sickness, and anger. The image is not merely of poverty. It is of wealth that fails to become enjoyment. The possession remains external to the possessor.

Yet the text also states a more positive judgment. Qoheleth says that it is good and fitting for a person to eat, drink, and enjoy the good of all labor under the sun during the few days of life given by God. This is a gift. If God gives wealth, possessions, and the power to enjoy them, then the person can accept the allotted portion and rejoice in the work.

The tension is deliberate. Wealth itself is not the answer. Wealth together with the power to enjoy may be. The same material condition can become either a gift or a burden, depending on whether pleasure is possible.

Qoheleth then describes a person to whom God gives riches, possessions, and honor, so that nothing is lacking. Yet God does not give that person the power to eat from them. A stranger consumes the goods. This is hebel and a grievous evil.

The text does not say that every wealthy person suffers this fate. It presents a case in which possession and enjoyment have been separated. The person has the visible signs of success but cannot inhabit them.

The final image in this section concerns a stillborn child. Qoheleth says that the child comes in hebel and departs in darkness, and that its name is covered. Though the child has not seen the sun or known anything, it has more rest than the wealthy person who cannot enjoy life.

The statement belongs to the book's harshest reflections. It is not a description of a funeral custom, and it is not a promise about the condition of unborn children. It is Qoheleth's comparison between an existence untouched by the world's frustrations and a life burdened by abundance without pleasure.

The text concludes that even if a person lives two thousand years but does not see good, both lives go to the same place. All labor is for the mouth, yet the appetite is not filled. The wise have no lasting advantage over fools in this respect, and the poor person who knows how to walk before the living may have no better answer.

The eye sees, but is not satisfied. The desire wanders. Better is what the eyes see than what desire roams after. This too is hebel and a chasing of wind.

The ancient readers who preserved these lines did not erase their roughness. The book entered Jewish scripture, but its presence remained a problem. The Mishnah later records arguments over whether Ecclesiastes rendered the hands sacred, the technical language used for recognized scripture. Its statements about the dead, pleasure, wisdom, and contradiction demanded interpretation.

Michael V. Fox argues that the tensions are not accidental damage around a simpler teaching. For Fox, Qoheleth's repeated reversals are part of the book's method. The reader is forced to watch every human advantage lose its stability. Choon-Leong Seow gives more weight to the book's literary setting and its inherited wisdom forms, while still treating its tensions as deliberate features of the composition.

Physical evidence places the work within the world of ancient Judea, but it cannot recover the private response of the first readers. Fragments of Ecclesiastes among the Dead Sea Scrolls show that parts of the Hebrew text circulated at Qumran. They do not preserve a record of a debate in which a named reader explained the stillborn child or the lonely worker.

The words remain, and their discomfort is part of their history.

A new sequence begins with a house of mourning. Qoheleth says that it is better to go there than to the house of feasting, because death is the end of every person and the living can take the matter to heart.

The statement does not condemn every meal or every celebration. Earlier passages have allowed eating, drinking, and joy. Here the house of mourning has a different value. It places an ending before the living person and makes evasion harder.

Sorrow can be better than laughter, the text says, because the sadness of the face may improve the heart. The heart of the wise is in the house of mourning, while the heart of fools is in the house of mirth. Better is the rebuke of the wise than the song of fools.

The images are social. A rebuke is spoken by another person. A song can fill the air without correcting anything. Qoheleth does not claim that every sorrow produces wisdom. He says that the encounter with sorrow can become more truthful than amusement that asks nothing.

Then comes a darker comparison. The crackling of thorns under a pot resembles the laughter of fools. It flares quickly and gives little lasting heat. This too is hebel.

Oppression appears again. A gift can corrupt the heart. The end of a matter is better than its beginning, and patience is better than pride. Anger rests in the bosom of fools. Qoheleth asks the living not to say that earlier days were better, because such a judgment does not arise from wisdom.

The passage moves through maxims rather than a single story. Wisdom is good with an inheritance and is profitable to those who see the sun. Wisdom is a protection, as money is a protection, but the advantage of knowledge is that wisdom preserves the life of its possessor.

Yet wisdom does not control events. Consider the work of God, Qoheleth says. Who can make straight what has been made crooked? In the day of prosperity, let a person be joyful. In the day of adversity, let the person consider that God has made one as well as the other, so that human beings cannot discover what will come after them.

The text then reaches one of its most quoted commands: be not righteous over much, neither make thyself over wise. Why should a person destroy himself? Do not be overly wicked, neither be foolish. Why should a person die before his time?

The King James Version preserves the phrase in that form. The Hebrew does not present a clear endorsement of moral indifference. It warns against a posture that turns righteousness or wisdom into a form of self-destruction. The adjoining warning against excessive wickedness has produced different readings.

One reading treats the lines as practical counsel against extremes. Another sees irony: Qoheleth may be exposing the danger of using wisdom sayings as if they could guarantee survival. The text itself refuses to resolve every tension.

Wisdom is a strength, Qoheleth says, but one sinner can destroy much good. There is no righteous person on earth who does good and never sins. The statement places a limit on moral confidence.

The next command concerns speech. The living should not take every word spoken to heart, lest a servant curse them. The speaker's own heart knows that it has cursed others many times. Judgment of another person must therefore pass through memory of one's own speech.

Qoheleth says that wisdom remains beyond reach. Whatever has been is far off and exceedingly deep. Who can find it? He turns his mind to know, search, and seek wisdom and the reason of things, and he finds one thing more bitter than death: the woman whose heart is snares and nets, whose hands are bonds.

A person who pleases God escapes, but the sinner is taken. The wording reflects an ancient literary stereotype of the dangerous woman, a figure also present in some wisdom traditions. It should not be turned into a biography of women or a description of ordinary relationships. Within this passage, it is an image of entrapment attached to a gendered wisdom convention.

Qoheleth says that he found one person among a thousand, but a woman among all those he had not found. The line is difficult and has received sharply different interpretations. Some readers see the residue of an ancient male author's suspicion. Others see an exaggerated rhetorical search for a fully reliable human being, whether male or female, rather than a statistical claim.

The next words widen the judgment. God made human beings upright, but they have sought out many schemes. The text places the origin of disorder in human striving rather than in a defect in creation.

Qoheleth then considers the relation between wisdom and authority. Wisdom makes the wise person stronger than ten rulers in a city. Yet there is no person on earth who does only good and never sins. Wisdom gives an advantage, but it does not make the wise invulnerable.

He tells the listener not to curse the king, even in thought, or the rich in a bedroom, because a bird of the air may carry the voice and a winged creature report the matter. The image is proverbial. It does not describe an espionage system. It describes how speech can escape its speaker.

Chapter eight begins with praise for wisdom. Who is like the wise person? Wisdom changes the face and makes severity less hard. The text then gives advice about obedience to a king, because of an oath before God. The king's command has authority, and the person who keeps it will know no evil thing.

Yet the passage immediately recognizes the limits of power. The heart of a wise person knows the time and judgment, but human misery is heavy. No one knows what will be, and no one can tell another when it will happen. A person has no power over the spirit to retain it, and no power over the day of death.

The text says that wickedness will not rescue its practitioners. It also records a frustration: there are righteous people who receive what seems appropriate for the wicked, and wicked people who receive what seems appropriate for the righteous. Qoheleth says that this too is hebel.

Because judgment is not promptly executed, human hearts are set toward evil. A sinner may do wrong repeatedly and still live long. Yet Qoheleth knows that those who fear God will fare better, and that the wicked will not have the same enduring good. The passage holds both observations together without making them fit neatly.

The text moves toward joy again. It praises the living because they know they will die, while the dead know nothing. The dead have no further reward, and the memory of them is forgotten. Their love, hatred, and envy have perished. They have no more portion in anything done under the sun.

This is one of Ecclesiastes' clearest statements about death. It does not describe an immortal soul receiving a reward in heaven. It places the dead outside the activities, emotions, and competitions of the living world. The word Sheol appears later in the book as the place where there is no work, thought, knowledge, or wisdom.

The conclusion is striking. Go thy way, eat thy bread with joy, and drink thy wine with a merry heart, for God now accepteth thy works. Let the garments be always white, and let the head lack no ointment. Live joyfully with the wife whom thou lovest all the days of the life of thy vanity, which God has given under the sun.

The King James Version's phrase "all the days of the life of thy vanity" preserves the collision between pleasure and hebel. Joy is not presented as a cure for mortality. It is the good available within mortality.

Whatever the hand finds to do, Qoheleth says, should be done with strength, because there is no work, device, knowledge, or wisdom in Sheol, where the person goes. This is not a command to a single profession. It is a statement about the limited time of action.

The next scene is a race. The swift do not always win. The strong do not always win the battle. The wise do not always receive bread, the understanding do not always receive riches, and those with knowledge do not always receive favor. Time and chance happen to all.

The language is not a theory of randomness in the modern mathematical sense. It names the unevenness of outcomes. The fastest can lose. The strongest can fall. Skill and effort matter, but they do not govern the result completely.

Qoheleth illustrates the point with a small city and a powerful king who comes against it. A poor wise person saves the city, yet no one remembers that poor person. Wisdom is better than weapons of war, but one sinner can destroy much good.

The same movement from wisdom to oblivion appears again. The saving act has occurred, but memory has not preserved the name. Ecclesiastes does not merely ask whether wisdom works. It asks what happens when wisdom works and is then forgotten.

The advice to enjoy bread, wine, clothing, oil, and marriage has often been compared with the counsel given by Siduri in the Mesopotamian Epic of Gilgamesh. In that poem, the alewife Siduri tells Gilgamesh to eat, drink, celebrate, and cherish the woman placed in his arms, because death is part of human existence.

The resemblance is real at the level of theme and sequence. Both passages join mortality to ordinary pleasures. Both turn away from a quest for permanent escape and toward the goods available in the present world.

Scholars disagree about the relationship. Some see a common ancient Near Eastern wisdom pattern rather than direct borrowing. Others allow that traditions about mortality and enjoyment could have circulated across languages and cultures, though no surviving document proves that the author of Ecclesiastes read Gilgamesh. Michael V. Fox treats the comparison cautiously, while other interpreters use it to place Qoheleth within a wider conversation about death.

The available Hebrew manuscripts do not reveal the route by which such ideas traveled. The Dead Sea Scrolls preserve fragments of the book, but no fragment identifies an earlier source for the advice. The parallel is historical evidence of shared human questions, not proof of direct literary dependence.

The chapter closes with a memorable image of wisdom that is present, effective, and then overlooked. The poor wise person saves the city, and the city forgets him. In Ecclesiastes, even a successful act can be swallowed by time.

The final movement begins with youth. Rejoice, young person, in youth. Let the heart enjoy the days of early life, and walk in the ways of the heart and in the sight of the eyes. Yet judgment will come for all these things.

The command carries both permission and limit. Youth is not condemned. Pleasure is not erased. But the text refuses to separate enjoyment from accountability. The young person may follow the heart, yet the book says that God will bring the whole matter into judgment.

Therefore remove sorrow from the heart and put away evil from the flesh, because childhood and youth are hebel. The phrase does not mean that youth is worthless. It means that youth passes, as every condition under the sun passes.

Chapter ten returns to short observations about folly. Dead flies make the perfumer's ointment give off a foul smell, and a little folly outweighs wisdom and honor. The image is material and exact: something small can spoil something carefully prepared.

The wise person's heart turns toward the right, while the fool's heart turns toward the left. Even when the fool walks along the road, understanding fails him, and he tells everyone that he is a fool. Folly announces itself.

If the ruler's anger rises against a person, the text says, do not leave the place. Calmness can prevent great offenses. The passage then describes a disorderly court: a ruler may be a child, and officials may feast in the morning. Blessed is the land whose king is noble and whose princes eat at the proper time, for strength rather than drunkenness.

The text does not identify a particular kingdom. It presents political order as a matter of character, discipline, and timing. When a ruler is foolish, officials consume resources while the structure itself decays.

Through laziness the building decays, and through idle hands the house leaks. A feast is made for laughter, and wine makes life glad, but money answers everything. The saying is compact and ambiguous. It may describe money's practical power, or it may expose a world in which every matter is forced into economic terms.

The next verses warn against cursing a king in private or a rich person in the bedroom. A bird of the air might carry the voice, and something with wings might tell the matter. The image repeats an earlier warning. Speech travels beyond the room in which it is spoken.

Chapter eleven opens with a command that has invited many interpretations: cast thy bread upon the waters, for thou shalt find it after many days. Give a portion to seven, and also to eight, because no one knows what evil may come upon the earth.

The bread may be an image of commerce, generosity, or risk. The passage does not explain it. The following image concerns clouds filled with rain and trees falling toward the south or north. Wherever the tree falls, there it remains.

The person who watches the wind will not sow, and the person who looks at the clouds will not reap. As the path of the spirit and the growth of a child in the womb remain mysterious, so the work of God cannot be fully known. In the morning the seed should be sown, and in the evening the hand should not be withheld, because no one knows which effort will prosper, or whether both will do equally well.

The text favors action without pretending to control outcomes. Waiting for perfect knowledge would end in paralysis. Acting does not guarantee success.

Light is sweet, and it is pleasant for the eyes to see the sun. If a person lives many years, let the person rejoice in them all, while remembering the days of darkness, which will be many. Everything that comes is hebel.

Again, joy and mortality stand together. The darkness is not described in detail here. Its length is enough. The person should rejoice because the days of life are limited, not because death has been removed.

The young person is told to rejoice in youth, to let the heart cheer during the days of youth, and to walk in the ways of the heart and the sight of the eyes. Then comes the returning limit: know that for all these things God will bring judgment.

The book's language does not produce a simple system of reward and punishment. Earlier, the righteous and the wicked received the same event. Here judgment is affirmed, but its timing and operation are not explained. The statement functions as a boundary around pleasure rather than as a map of the afterlife.

Chapter twelve begins with the words "Remember now thy Creator in the days of thy youth." The King James Version capitalizes Creator, while the Hebrew phrase can also be understood as a plural or intensive form referring to the creator. The central command is to remember before the days of trouble arrive.

The passage describes old age through a chain of images. The sun, light, moon, and stars grow dark, and clouds return after rain. The keepers of the house tremble. The strong men bow. The grinders cease because they are few. Those looking through the windows grow dim.

The doors are shut in the streets. The sound of grinding is low. A person rises at the sound of a bird, while the voices of song are brought low. The text then names fear on the road, terror connected with heights, and the almond tree flowering. The grasshopper becomes a burden, and desire fails.

The exact referents of the images have been debated since antiquity. The keepers of the house may suggest arms. The grinders may suggest teeth. Those looking through the windows may suggest eyes. The almond tree may evoke white hair. The grasshopper may represent a bent or weakened body, though no single interpretation commands universal agreement.

What remains clear is the direction of the passage. The body that once worked, saw, heard, moved, and desired becomes unreliable. The old person approaches the house that will be left behind.

Then the silver cord is loosed, the golden bowl is broken, the pitcher is shattered at the fountain, and the wheel is broken at the cistern. These are images of collapse. A valuable object and a useful mechanism alike reach the point at which they cannot perform their function.

The sequence ends with dust returning to the earth as it was, and the spirit returning to God who gave it. The line is often read as a statement about the destiny of the human spirit. In its immediate setting, it contrasts the body's return to dust with the return of breath or spirit to its giver. The text does not develop a complete account of personal existence after death.

The final poetic description is followed by the familiar phrase: vanity of vanities, saith the preacher; all is vanity. It closes the body of Qoheleth's speech by returning to the refrain that has governed the book from its opening line.

Then another voice appears.

The epilogue speaks of the preacher in the third person. It says that Qoheleth was wise, taught the people knowledge, and weighed, searched out, and arranged many proverbs. The words are described as acceptable, like goads and firmly fixed nails given by the masters of assemblies from one shepherd.

The passage then warns against making many books. Of making many books there is no end, and much study is a weariness of the flesh. The statement is not a condemnation of every written work. It is an epilogue's reminder that accumulation of words does not remove the burden of interpretation.

The closing judgment is concise: fear God and keep his commandments, for this is the whole duty of man. God will bring every work into judgment, including every hidden thing, whether it is good or whether it is evil.

The change in voice has shaped modern scholarship. Many scholars think the epilogue was added by an editor or later frame narrator. Its third-person description of Qoheleth differs from the first-person speech that fills most of the book. Its final command also appears to contain the tension by placing the book's radical observations inside a more conventional frame.

Tremper Longman III argues that the frame narrator guides the reader in how to hear Qoheleth. On this reading, the epilogue is not merely an accidental appendix. It presents Qoheleth as a wise teacher whose claims must be measured against the conclusion about fearing God and keeping commandments.

Michael V. Fox gives greater emphasis to the book's internal argument. For Fox, the epilogue may preserve a response to Qoheleth, but it does not cancel the force of the speech that precedes it. The reader still has to pass through the claims that the dead know nothing, that the righteous and wicked share one event, and that wisdom cannot secure outcomes.

Choon-Leong Seow treats the epilogue as a literary frame that helps explain the book's reception. Its presence matters because it may have made Ecclesiastes more acceptable within a scriptural collection. Yet the frame does not erase the body. It preserves the disturbance by enclosing it.

No surviving manuscript tells when the epilogue was attached. The Hebrew text transmitted in the medieval Masoretic tradition includes it. Fragments from Qumran show that Ecclesiastes circulated in the Second Temple period, but the surviving evidence is too incomplete to establish whether every early copy had the same ending.

The last line sounds settled. The path to it was not.

The argument over Ecclesiastes did not end when scribes copied the book. It entered rabbinic memory as a dispute about whether the work belonged among sacred writings.

The Mishnah, in the tractate Yadayim, records disagreement between the schools of Shammai and Hillel over whether Ecclesiastes defiles the hands. In rabbinic legal language, a recognized sacred scroll makes the hands ritually unclean. The question therefore concerned the book's status as scripture.

The report does not give a verbatim transcript of a council deciding the matter. It preserves a later account of disagreement. Ecclesiastes was treated as a work whose presence required explanation.

Rabbinic reports also say that sages wanted to withdraw the book because its words contradicted one another. The problem was visible in the text itself. One passage praises wisdom, another says that wisdom increases sorrow. One passage urges enjoyment, another calls all things hebel. One passage says that the dead know nothing, while the ending speaks of judgment.

The epilogue offered a way to contain the problem. It presented Qoheleth as a wise teacher and ended with fear of God and obedience to commandments. The association with Solomon also helped. The book's opening persona is identified as the son of David and king in Jerusalem, and later readers connected that royal figure with Solomon.

The identification is part of the book's literary presentation, not a secure record of authorship. A writer could speak through a Solomonic persona in order to place difficult reflections within the tradition of Israel's wisdom king.

The Greek Septuagint translated Ecclesiastes as a work associated with the assembly, and the Latin tradition carried the title into Jerome's Vulgate. Jerome read the book within a Christian scriptural collection and helped establish the terminology through which Latin readers encountered it. His interpretation did not remove the language of mortality, but it placed the text within a larger theological world.

Centuries later, Martin Luther treated Ecclesiastes as a serious wisdom book while reading its warnings through Christian doctrine. His approach belonged to a different setting from the rabbinic dispute, but the same problem remained: the text's statements about death, pleasure, and judgment could not simply be passed over.

Leo Tolstoy encountered Ecclesiastes during the crisis described in A Confession. He read its reflections on labor, wealth, death, and the apparent futility of human activity as part of his own search for an answer. His use of the book belongs to modern autobiography, not to evidence about the original author. It shows how the text continued to provide language for a reader who found ordinary explanations insufficient.

The book's survival was therefore not the result of a single uncontested decision. It was copied, translated, argued over, framed, and repeatedly interpreted. The Hebrew text remained available because communities judged it worthy of preservation, even while some readers judged parts of it dangerous or contradictory.

Modern scholars disagree about how the tensions should be understood.

Michael V. Fox argues that contradiction is part of the point. Qoheleth examines the world from different angles and refuses to let one formula settle the matter. The apparent conflicts are not necessarily errors repaired by the epilogue. They make the reader confront the instability of every human scheme.

Choon-Leong Seow emphasizes the book's literary and historical setting. For Seow, Qoheleth belongs to the wisdom tradition but reshapes it through a voice that tests conventional claims. The work's repeated use of hebel does not mean that every experience is simply meaningless. It points toward what is elusive, fleeting, or resistant to human control.

Tremper Longman III gives special weight to the frame narrator. In his reading, the epilogue tells readers that Qoheleth's observations must be heard within a larger theological judgment. The book is not reduced to skepticism, because its closing voice offers a final standard by which the speeches are assessed.

These positions overlap in some places, but they are not identical. Fox stresses the unresolved force of the book. Seow stresses its literary world and semantic range. Longman stresses the interpretive authority of the frame.

Robert Alter's translation and discussion of the wisdom books draw attention to the texture of the Hebrew poetry and prose. The repetitions, abrupt turns, and compressed images carry much of the work's effect. The book is not merely a collection of propositions. Its form makes instability audible.

Thomas Kruger places Qoheleth within the intellectual world of the Hellenistic period and examines how the work responds to older wisdom traditions. The precise date remains disputed, but the book's language and concerns fit a period in which Judean writers engaged wider cultures while continuing to write within Hebrew traditions.

Most historians place Ecclesiastes somewhere in the Second Temple period, often in the later centuries before the common era. The exact date cannot be recovered from the text alone. The Solomon persona gives the book a legendary past, while its Hebrew vocabulary and intellectual setting point toward a later composition.

The canon debate also cannot be turned into a simple contest between believers and skeptics. Ancient Jewish authorities preserved the book while arguing about its status. Christian translators and theologians retained it while interpreting its darkest claims. Modern scholars continue to disagree because the text itself gives them reasons to disagree.

He was an invented figure, not a person in the historical record.

What can be said about his city is more solid. Jerusalem contained scribes, religious institutions, laborers, officials, and people whose lives depended on harvests and political decisions.
