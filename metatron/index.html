Metatron: How Enoch of Genesis Became an Angel in Jewish Texts

How the single verse about Enoch in Genesis grew into the angel Metatron of Third Enoch, the Talmud and Kabbalah.

Full script

Genesis five, verse twenty-four, says of Enoch, "And Enoch walked with God: and he was not; for God took him." The Bible says very little about Enoch, yet later Jewish literature expands him into a heavenly scribe, an angelic prince, and, in Third Enoch, Metatron, called the lesser Yahweh.

The distance between those two descriptions is the subject here. Genesis gives Enoch only a brief place in a genealogy. Later texts make him a visionary, a traveler through heaven, a transformed angel, and an intermediary who stands close to the divine throne. The surviving sources do not describe one continuous doctrine. They preserve layers of interpretation, written at different times, in different languages, and for different religious purposes.

The transformation begins with an absence.

In the genealogy of Adam through Seth, Enoch appears as the son of Jared and the father of Methuselah. Genesis gives his age as three hundred and sixty-five years. Then it says that he walked with God and was no more, because God took him.

The wording differs from the formula used for many other figures in the genealogy. The text does not simply say that Enoch died. It says that God took him. Genesis does not explain where he went, whether he remained human, or what his relationship with God involved. It does not call him an angel, a prophet, a scribe, or a heavenly prince. Those identifications belong to later interpretation.

The unusual wording created room for that interpretation. Ancient readers often treated unexpected details in scripture as signs that invited explanation. Enoch's disappearance could be read as an ascent rather than an ordinary death. His statement in Genesis that he walked with God could be expanded into a history of intimacy with the divine realm. His age, three hundred and sixty-five years, could be associated with the solar year and with the ordered movements of the heavens.

The Bible itself does not make those connections. Later authors did.

The earliest surviving Enochic literature emerged during the Second Temple period, the era between the rebuilding of the Jerusalem Temple in the late sixth century before the Common Era and its destruction in the first century of the Common Era. The Enochic works were not produced all at once, and they do not form a single book by one author. They are a collection of apocalyptic, astronomical, visionary, and instructional writings attached to Enoch's name.

Apocalyptic literature is writing that presents hidden heavenly revelations, often through visions of judgment, cosmic conflict, resurrection, or the end of an age. Such texts commonly place an ancient figure in contact with angels and divine mysteries. By speaking through Enoch, their authors could present new teaching as wisdom from the primeval past.

The historical setting helps explain the appeal. Jewish communities lived under Persian, Hellenistic, and Roman rule. Jerusalem had been conquered, its Temple destroyed and rebuilt, and its religious institutions drawn repeatedly into political conflict. Questions about evil, judgment, angels, impurity, calendars, and divine justice were ways of interpreting a world that appeared unstable.

The Enochic writings looked upward for an explanation.

The Book of the Watchers is the opening major section of First Enoch. First Enoch is an anthology, meaning a collection of works composed at different times and later transmitted together. The Book of the Watchers is generally dated to the third or second century before the Common Era, although the composition and editing of the larger collection are complex. It is not identical with the Astronomical Book, the Dream Visions, the Book of Parables, or the Epistle of Enoch, which make up other portions of First Enoch.

The Book of the Watchers begins with angels called the Watchers. They descend to earth and take human women as partners. Their union produces gigantic offspring, usually associated with the giants. The Watchers also reveal knowledge connected with metalworking, adornment, enchantment, astrology, and other human arts.

The text presents this knowledge as part of a larger moral and cosmic violation. The Watchers have crossed a boundary between heaven and earth. Their actions contribute to violence and corruption, and the resulting disorder becomes a reason for judgment.

Enoch is chosen as an intermediary. He carries messages between God and the rebellious angels. The Watchers ask him to plead for them, but the judgment against them remains firm. Enoch becomes a witness and a scribe of heavenly events. He travels through regions associated with the dead, observes the movements of the sun and stars, and receives revelations about judgment.

In the Book of the Watchers, Enoch remains recognizably human, even when he receives extraordinary access. He is a visionary, messenger, and recorder. He does not yet become Metatron.

That distinction is essential. First Enoch supplies a profile that later writers can expand. Enoch is already linked with heaven, judgment, writing, cosmic order, and knowledge that ordinary human beings cannot see. Later authors inherit those features rather than creating the entire pattern from nothing.

The astronomical material in First Enoch strengthens the connection between Enoch and cosmic order. The sun, moon, and stars move according to rules revealed to him. Time is not random. It follows a divinely established structure, and Enoch becomes the witness who records that structure.

He is also presented as an author. The Enochic books claim to preserve revelations that the ancient patriarch received and transmitted to later generations. This is a literary attribution, not evidence that the historical author was Enoch. The texts were composed long after the period in which Genesis places him, but the attribution gives them a voice from before the Flood, before Moses, and before Israel's kings.

Enoch is not a priest in Jerusalem, a royal prophet, or a court official. He is a primeval figure outside ordinary political history, brought into heaven before the Flood. That position makes him unusually adaptable. Different communities can use his name to discuss angels, calendars, the origins of evil, resurrection, judgment, and the structure of the universe.

The Enochic tradition was therefore not one fixed book at the beginning. It was a growing body of visions and teachings attached to a single ancient authority.

The oldest surviving physical evidence for several Enochic works comes from the Dead Sea Scrolls, discovered at sites near Qumran. Fragments of Enochic writings survive in Aramaic among those scrolls, showing that the tradition circulated in Jewish communities before the rise of Christianity. The complete text of First Enoch survives most fully in Ge'ez, the classical language of Ethiopia, through the Ethiopian Orthodox tradition.

The evidence of manuscripts also complicates the apparent unity of First Enoch. Its sections differ in date, style, vocabulary, and subject. The Book of the Watchers is an apocalyptic account of angels and judgment. The Astronomical Book concerns the heavenly calendar. The Dream Visions recount symbolic history. The Book of Parables develops visions of judgment and an exalted figure often called the Son of Man. These works were gathered under Enoch's name, but they do not necessarily come from one author or one community.

Across them, however, a recognizable Enoch emerges: a human being permitted to see too much.

Enoch's reputation did not stay inside these books.

The Book of Jubilees, a Jewish work written in the second century before the Common Era, retells Genesis and gives him a sharper profile. It calls him the first human being to learn writing, says angels showed him the affairs of heaven, and has him record his testimony against the Watchers. After that, Jubilees says, he was taken from among humankind and set in the garden of Eden, where he writes down the judgments of the world. Here Enoch is a scribe by office, long before anyone calls him Metatron.

Around the same period, the Jerusalem sage Ben Sira listed Enoch among the heroes of Israel. He wrote that Enoch pleased the Lord and was taken up, an example of repentance to all generations.

The New Testament knows the tradition as well. The Epistle to the Hebrews says, in the King James Version, "By faith Enoch was translated that he should not see death; and was not found, because God had translated him," says Hebrews, chapter eleven, verse five. And the short letter of Jude quotes a prophecy of "Enoch also, the seventh from Adam," that matches a passage in First Enoch: "Behold, the Lord cometh with ten thousands of his saints," in Jude, verse fourteen.

For Jewish and early Christian readers alike, then, Enoch was already a figure taken seriously long before any text called him Metatron.

The biblical sentence still leaves a question open. If God took Enoch, what became of him after he vanished?

Second Enoch, often called the Slavonic Book of Enoch, offers one answer. The work is preserved primarily in medieval manuscripts written in Church Slavonic. Its original language was probably Greek, although the exact route by which it reached its Slavonic form remains debated. Scholars commonly place its composition around the late first or early second century of the Common Era, but the date and textual history are not certain.

Second Enoch begins with Enoch already in a heavenly setting. Two immense beings take him on a journey through the heavens. He sees angels, cosmic regions, and the divine presence. The work resembles earlier Enochic literature, but its structure places greater emphasis on education and transformation.

Enoch is not merely shown the universe. He is instructed within it.

He passes through several heavens and encounters different classes of angels, regions associated with punishment and reward, and the divine throne. He receives teaching about creation, time, and the order of the cosmos. He is brought before God and later returns to earth to teach and prophesy before being taken permanently.

At one point, Enoch's appearance changes before the heavenly beings. The exact wording varies across the surviving manuscript tradition, but the general movement is clear. He becomes like the glorious beings around him. His face is illuminated, and his status approaches that of the angels.

Second Enoch therefore develops several features that later converge in Metatron. Enoch is the human taken into heaven. He is changed in appearance. He receives heavenly knowledge. He is associated with books and writing. He can return to humanity as a teacher, even after being admitted to the celestial world.

The work does not call him Metatron. That name is absent from the surviving text. The absence prevents a simple claim that Second Enoch directly tells the same story as Third Enoch. The texts share themes, but their exact relationship is uncertain.

Some scholars have proposed direct connections among Enochic writings and later Jewish mystical traditions. Others argue that similar ideas could develop independently from common biblical and apocalyptic themes. The evidence does not establish one uninterrupted genealogy. What it does show is a recurring interpretive movement: Enoch's disappearance is explained through ascent, revelation, and increasing transformation.

By late antiquity, that movement entered a rabbinic environment concerned with the limits of heavenly speculation.

The Second Temple was destroyed by Rome in the year seventy of the Common Era. Jewish religious life continued under Roman rule, but the institutions of the Temple no longer occupied the same place. The rabbinic movement developed forms of authority centered on study, legal interpretation, prayer, and communal memory. Heavenly order remained important, but discussions of the divine throne had to be fitted into a religious world without the Temple.

At the same time, Jewish writers lived amid wider debates about angels, divine intermediaries, heavenly ascent, and the relationship between God and exalted figures. Christian thinkers discussed the Logos and other heavenly agents. Gnostic texts described complex divine hierarchies. Iranian, Greek, and local traditions contributed to the religious environment of late antiquity.

Jewish writers could explore a populated heaven, but they had to maintain the unity of God.

Rabbinic literature sometimes describes an idea called "two powers in heaven." The phrase refers to interpretations that appear to place a second authority alongside the God of Israel. Scholars disagree about the precise historical targets of the phrase. It may have been used against Christian claims, against other Jewish movements, or more broadly against doctrines that divided heavenly authority. Its application changed across contexts.

Metatron occupies the space where those concerns become concrete. He is close enough to God to serve as an intermediary and powerful enough to govern heavenly affairs. That closeness gives him a purpose, but it also creates a theological problem.

The Babylonian Talmud preserves one of the clearest versions of that problem in tractate Hagigah. The Babylonian Talmud is a large collection of rabbinic legal discussions, stories, interpretations, and debates. Its material accumulated over several centuries, and its final redaction is usually placed around the fifth to seventh centuries of the Common Era, although individual passages may be earlier or later.

The passage in Hagigah concerns the interpretation of Ezekiel's chariot vision and the dangers of entering what the text calls the orchard. The orchard is a metaphor for esoteric knowledge, meaning knowledge treated as restricted, difficult, or unsafe to transmit without discipline.

Four entered the orchard: Ben Azzai, Ben Zoma, Aher, and Rabbi Akiva. Ben Azzai looked and died. Ben Zoma looked and was harmed. Aher cut down the shoots, which the rabbis read as becoming an apostate. Rabbi Akiva entered in peace and departed in peace.

Aher means "the other." The name stands for Elisha ben Abuyah, a learned rabbi whom later rabbinic memory treated as the great example of a scholar who went wrong. Historians can reconstruct little of the real man apart from these traditions. What matters here is the reason the Talmud gives for his fall.

Elisha, it says, saw Metatron sitting.

Heaven, as the sages imagined it, had no seats for servants. A tradition quoted in the passage says that above there is no sitting, no rivalry, and no weariness. Elisha looked at a figure who sat in the presence of God, and he reasoned from what he saw. Perhaps, God forbid, there are two powers in heaven.

The court of heaven answers at once. Metatron is brought out and struck with sixty lashes of fire, and he is asked why he did not stand when Elisha appeared. Most readers take the punishment as the argument. A being who can be whipped by the court is not a god.

The Talmud then explains the seat. Metatron had been given permission to sit, because he was the scribe who wrote down the merits of Israel.

The explanation is almost comic in its modesty. A being so exalted that a great scholar mistook him for a rival to God turns out to be a clerk, seated because his work requires it. The passage keeps Metatron's high rank and fixes it to a duty, so that his place near the throne can be admitted without being misread.

Scholars disagree about what the story was aimed at. The phrase two powers in heaven may have targeted Christian ideas, or other Jewish groups, or any doctrine that divided heavenly authority. What the passage does show is that by the time of the Babylonian Talmud, Metatron's nearness to God had become something that needed careful explaining.

A later Hebrew work makes Enoch's identity with Metatron explicit.

The work is called Sefer Hekhalot, "Book of the Palaces," and is commonly known as Third Enoch. It is a Jewish mystical text concerned with heavenly ascent, angels, divine names, and the structure of the celestial palaces. Hekhalot means "palaces" or "temples" in Hebrew and refers to a body of Jewish mystical writings that describe journeys toward the divine throne.

Third Enoch is not simply the third volume in a continuous series. It is a Hebrew compilation with a complicated history of composition and transmission. Scholars often place its final form somewhere between the fifth and eighth centuries of the Common Era, while also arguing that some of its material may be earlier. The exact date and relationship of its sources remain debated.

The work begins with Rabbi Ishmael, a prominent rabbinic figure, ascending into heaven and meeting Metatron. The text is structured as a dialogue between an earthly sage and a heavenly guide. Metatron explains his identity and his position within the celestial order.

He identifies himself as Enoch, son of Jared.

This is the decisive identification. The tradition no longer merely places Enoch in heaven or gives him angelic features. It states that the man in Genesis became the angel Metatron.

According to Third Enoch, heaven did not welcome him. When Enoch was raised before the generation of the Flood, the angels objected. What was a being of flesh and blood doing among creatures of fire and holiness, and why had he been placed above them?

God's answer is not to defend Enoch as he was. It is to remake him.

The text piles up the changes. Enoch is enlarged until he matches the length and breadth of the world. He is given seventy-two wings, and a multitude of eyes, each as bright as a great luminary. His flesh becomes flame, his bones coals of juniper wood, his eyelashes flashes of lightning, his eyeballs torches of fire. A throne modeled on God's own throne is made for him, and heralds announce him as a prince and ruler over the angels of the heights.

The excess is the point. Enoch is not promoted. He is rebuilt for the heavenly court, and the human being becomes a heavenly person.

Third Enoch gives Metatron a large collection of titles. He is Prince of the Divine Presence, Prince of the Torah, Prince of Wisdom, Prince of Kings, and Prince of Princes. He is the heavenly scribe, a guide to souls, and a revealer of celestial knowledge.

Most provocatively, he is called the lesser Yahweh.

The phrase does not mean that Third Enoch openly presents two equal gods. Within the work, Metatron is an authorized representative, a bearer of the divine name, and a chief minister of the heavenly court. The title expresses delegated authority. Metatron can represent God because God has granted him authority, but he remains subordinate to the divine source.

That distinction does not remove the tension. A representative who bears the divine name can appear to possess independent authority. The title is therefore both an elevation and a limit. Metatron is associated with Yahweh's name, but he is the lesser bearer of that name.

The phrase depends partly on a biblical pattern in which an angel carries or represents the divine name. In Exodus, God tells Israel that an angel will go before them and that the divine name is in him. The passage does not identify that angel as Metatron. Later interpreters could nevertheless use the idea of a name-bearing angel to explain how Metatron could speak with divine authority without being identical with God.

Other scholars connect Metatron with the angel of the Lord, the Prince of the Presence, the heavenly youth, or the archangel Michael. These proposals are not equivalent. They reflect an attempt to explain why Metatron possesses titles and functions associated with several older figures.

Metatron is best understood as a composite figure: a character formed from multiple traditions that were brought together over time. Some features come from Enoch's transformation. Some resemble older ideas about God's presence. Some recall principal angels such as Michael. Some may reflect traditions about a heavenly youth or a bearer of the divine name. The surviving texts do not reveal one moment when a single author created the complete figure.

Third Enoch preserves the result of a long process of theological accumulation.

Rabbi Ishmael's encounter with Metatron presents that result through celestial administration. Angels have ranks, names, duties, and liturgical functions. Heavenly palaces have guardians. Songs of praise rise from angelic hosts. Records of human conduct are kept. The throne is surrounded by powers whose appearance overwhelms the visionary.

Metatron makes this universe legible.

He knows the names of angels. He understands the structure of the heavens. He records human deeds. He guides visionaries and explains the organization of the divine court. If heaven is imagined as a royal administration, Metatron is its chief minister, archivist, and interpreter.

This administrative quality is one of the most distinctive features of the figure. Ancient and late antique religious texts often represent heaven as a court. God is enthroned. Angels attend. Books are opened. Deeds are recorded. Judgments are issued. The heavenly world mirrors earthly kingship, but it also provides an ideal form of order.

Metatron belongs to that political imagination. He manages the distance between God and creation.

The transformation of Enoch solves several narrative problems at once. It explains where Enoch went. It provides a human witness who can be trusted with heavenly revelation. It gives mystical ascent a guide who has already crossed the boundary. It creates an angelic figure distinct from God but unusually close to God. It also allows a tradition to claim access to ancient wisdom without placing all religious authority within a Temple priesthood, a royal court, or a later institution.

The solution creates a new difficulty. If Enoch becomes an angel, does he remain human? If Metatron bears the divine name, does he share the divine nature? If he can sit, judge, teach, write, and command, what prevents him from becoming a second deity?

The literature answers through repeated acts of containment.

Metatron is elevated, but subordinated. He is called the youth, a term that can indicate proximity and status while also suggesting dependence. He is a servant. He may sit for a task, but not as an independent ruler. His authority is received rather than innate. His appearance resembles the divine presence, but he is not the source of that presence.

The tradition wants the benefits of a heavenly vice-regent without conceding divine plurality.

A vice-regent is a representative who exercises the authority of a ruler without being the ruler himself. The term is modern, but it describes the political logic of Metatron's role. He is powerful because he acts for God, and subordinate because his authority is derived.

The Merkabah tradition provides the setting for this heavenly court.

Merkabah is Hebrew for "chariot," especially the chariot in Ezekiel chapter one. Ezekiel sees a storm wind, living creatures, wheels, a firmament, and a throne resembling sapphire. Above the throne appears a humanlike figure surrounded by radiance. The passage became one of the foundational sources for later Jewish speculation about the divine throne and heavenly ascent.

In Hekhalot literature, visionaries approach the throne through a series of heavenly palaces. Each palace has angels, guards, names, hymns, and displays of power. The writings are not uniform, and they preserve materials from different periods and communities. Their descriptions are often repetitive, cryptic, and difficult to align into one system.

Metatron appears within this world as both inhabitant and guide.

He is not merely one angel among many. He knows the route through the palaces. He can explain the names and functions of the guardians. He can present the visionary before the throne. He can interpret what the visionary sees.

This role develops naturally from Enoch's earlier literary profile. In First Enoch, Enoch sees cosmic mysteries. In Second Enoch, he travels through the heavens and receives instruction. In Third Enoch, he guides Rabbi Ishmael through the celestial order.

The transformation is therefore not only a change in rank. It is a change in narrative function. The former traveler becomes a permanent resident and guide.

Metatron is also connected in some traditions with the heavenly youth, or na'ar. The Hebrew word na'ar can mean a youth, attendant, or servant. Its meaning in angelic traditions is not fixed. Some passages associate the heavenly youth with Metatron. Others distinguish the two figures. This instability is one reason scholars resist treating Metatron as a single, unchanging personality from the beginning.

Names and titles in ancient mystical texts can move between figures. A title can become a personal name. A being associated with one task can inherit another being's functions. Metatron may represent precisely this kind of convergence.

The origin of the name Metatron is uncertain. Proposals have connected it with Greek words for a guide or measurer, Latin expressions for one who stands near or behind a throne, names from magical and mystical traditions, and transformations of other angelic names. None of these explanations has universal acceptance.

The uncertainty affects the historical reconstruction. If the name's origin cannot be securely established, the figure must be traced through usage, titles, stories, and functions rather than through an agreed etymology.

Metatron's meaning is cumulative.

In Third Enoch, he is Enoch transformed. In the Talmud, he is a heavenly official whose seat requires explanation. In Hekhalot literature, he is a guide to the palaces. In later mystical traditions, he becomes a mediator, protector, and channel of celestial knowledge.

The Kabbalah inherited this figure and placed him within new systems of symbolism.

Kabbalah is a collection of medieval Jewish mystical traditions that developed in several communities, especially from the twelfth century onward. A central Kabbalistic concept is the sefirot, the attributes or emanations through which the infinite divine reality is described as manifesting in the created world. Different Kabbalistic works organize the sefirot differently, and no single Kabbalistic doctrine defines Metatron.

Some texts link Metatron with Tiferet, the sefirah associated with beauty and harmony. Others connect him with Yesod, associated with foundation, or with the angelic realm more generally. These associations vary by author, period, and symbolic system.

Metatron may appear as Prince of the World, Angel of the Divine Face, or celestial counterpart of humanity. He can represent the axis joining upper and lower realms. He can embody the perfected human transformed into an angelic being. He can carry prayers upward or divine influence downward.

The older problem remains. Metatron is close to God but cannot be God. Kabbalistic texts can describe him as a channel or configuration of divine power while preserving a distinction between the infinite source and the created intermediary.

His earlier identity as Enoch is important here because it gives the angel a human origin. An angel belongs to heaven by nature, while Enoch enters heaven through divine choice. His authority rests partly on what he was. He can speak to humanity because he once belonged to humanity. He can stand among angels because he has been remade.

This helps explain why Metatron appears in traditions of sacred writing and esoteric transmission.

The Sefer Raziel tradition offers a later example. Sefer Raziel HaMalakh, "The Book of the Angel Raziel," is a medieval Jewish compendium associated with the angel Raziel and with the transmission of heavenly knowledge to Adam after the expulsion from Eden. Its familiar form is not an ancient book written by a biblical angel. It is a compilation of materials from different Jewish esoteric traditions, probably assembled in medieval Ashkenaz, the Jewish cultural region of northern Europe.

Its manuscript history is complex, and not every version presents the same contents. Some passages concern angelic names. Others address cosmology, calendars, astrology, divine names, or protective practices. The work should not be treated as one uniform text from one period.

Metatron belongs to the same conceptual world of transmission. In this tradition, heavenly knowledge reaches humanity through a chain of angelic and human authorities. Adam receives wisdom after leaving paradise. That knowledge passes through primeval figures and later custodians. Enoch or Metatron can occupy a place within this chain as a mediator of celestial teaching.

The point is not that every copy of Sefer Raziel contains an identical account of Metatron. The point is that Metatron makes a particular kind of authority possible. He can serve as a guarantor of antiquity and heavenly origin.

A book claiming to preserve primordial wisdom needs a route by which the wisdom reached humanity. Adam receives it in Eden. Angels guard it. Enoch records it. Metatron carries it through heaven. Later sages preserve it in manuscripts.

Such claims are not historical evidence that an angel authored the book. They are literary and religious strategies that give knowledge authority by placing it outside ordinary time.

Metatron is suited to this role because he connects several ancient names at once. He is Enoch, the biblical man who vanished. He is the angelic scribe who records the order of heaven. He is Prince of the Presence, who stands near God. He is a transmitter who can carry celestial wisdom to human beings.

The transformation is therefore textual before it is theological. No surviving document records a historical person named Enoch changing into an angel before witnesses. The change takes place in interpretation, narrative, compilation, and manuscript transmission.

That does not make the transformation unreal within the religious worlds that preserved it. A community could regard the stories as accounts of an actual heavenly reality. Modern history, however, can establish the sequence of surviving texts and the development of their imagery. It cannot determine whether a heavenly transformation occurred outside the texts.

The evidence is best handled in layers.

The first layer is Genesis. Genesis describes Enoch's unusual removal from the world. It does not call him an angel and does not mention Metatron. The idea of transformation is an interpretation of the text's wording and silence.

The second layer is early Enochic literature. First Enoch presents Enoch as a visionary, messenger, scribe, and witness of heavenly mysteries. It establishes qualities later needed for an angelic transformation, but it does not identify him as Metatron.

The third layer is Second Enoch. Enoch travels through several heavens, receives instruction, and undergoes a change toward angelic appearance. The name Metatron is absent from the surviving work, but the concept of human elevation is present.

The fourth layer is rabbinic literature. The Babylonian Talmud knows Metatron as an exalted heavenly figure and preserves the story of Elisha ben Abuyah seeing him seated. The passage shows concern about Metatron's proximity to divine authority.

The fifth layer is Third Enoch. Here the identification becomes explicit. Metatron tells Rabbi Ishmael that he is Enoch, son of Jared, transformed into a heavenly being.

The sixth layer is Hekhalot and medieval mystical literature. Metatron becomes a guide, cosmic prince, mediator, protector, and participant in systems of divine manifestation.

The seventh layer is the Sefer Raziel tradition and related esoteric manuscripts. Metatron joins a network through which heavenly wisdom is imagined to reach human readers.

This sequence does not prove that each stage directly caused the next. Texts can preserve older motifs without copying a specific earlier passage. Authors can develop similar ideas independently. Several works survive only in manuscripts later than their presumed composition, and the dates of their final forms remain disputed.

The pattern is nevertheless coherent. Enoch moves from righteous ancestor to visionary seer, from seer to heavenly traveler, from traveler toward transformed angel, and from transformed angel to chief mediator.

The title lesser Yahweh marks the highest point of that ascent and exposes its limit. Metatron can approach God because he is not God. He can bear the name because he is not its source. He can sit because he has been assigned a task. He can judge because he acts within the heavenly court. He can teach because wisdom has been entrusted to him.

The distinction is fragile, and that fragility is central to the tradition.

A figure too distant from humanity cannot explain how human beings encounter heaven. A figure too close to God threatens monotheism. Metatron occupies the narrow space between those problems.

Scholars also debate whether Enoch and Metatron were originally connected. The name Metatron may have circulated independently before it was attached to Enoch. Some traditions may have imagined Metatron as an angelic title and only later identified him with the patriarch. Others may have combined the figures because Enoch's ascent offered a compelling explanation for Metatron's unusual rank.

The surviving evidence does not reveal the first moment of the identification.

It does show that Third Enoch preserves the identification as an established claim. The work does not present Enoch's transformation as a tentative possibility. It expects the reader to understand that the patriarch of Genesis and the highest angel are the same being.

He also exposes a difference between apocalyptic and rabbinic forms of authority. Enochic literature presents revelation as knowledge received from heaven and recorded for later generations. Rabbinic literature places greater emphasis on interpretation, debate, and the regulated transmission of esoteric teachings.

Metatron belongs to both worlds, but uneasily.

As Enoch, he comes from the ancient visionary tradition. In Talmud and Hekhalot literature, he becomes part of arguments about who may know what and how the celestial world should be described. In Kabbalah, he enters systems of divine presence and emanation. In Sefer Raziel traditions, he helps authorize a chain of transmission from heaven to manuscript.

His identity changes because the needs of the texts change.

The Enochic books need an ancient witness. The Hekhalot texts need a guide to the palaces. The Talmud needs an example of dangerous interpretation. Kabbalistic literature needs a mediator between divine and created realms. Medieval esoteric compilations need a celestial transmitter of primordial wisdom.

Metatron performs all of these roles without being reduced to one of them.

His cultural influence is therefore a history of adaptation rather than simple survival.

In medieval Jewish sources, he remains a powerful angel of the divine presence. In other works, he is connected with Torah, heavenly study, and the celestial counterpart of humanity. In mystical speculation, he may be associated with the pattern through which divine energy reaches the lower worlds.

Later Christian occult writers encountered Metatron through Jewish and Kabbalistic materials and placed him into angelic hierarchies of their own. Those later systems should not be projected backward onto ancient texts. The Metatron of a Renaissance magical diagram is not automatically the Metatron of the Babylonian Talmud or Third Enoch.

The same caution applies to modern spiritual literature. Contemporary books and websites may present Metatron as an archangel of healing, geometry, or personal guidance. Those images belong to the later reception of the name. They are not straightforward descriptions of ancient Jewish sources.

The historical Metatron is more unstable than the simplified modern angel. He is the human transformed into fire. He is the servant mistaken for a second god. He is the scribe who records merits. He is the guide through palaces where even angels are overwhelmed.

His association with measurement and cosmic geometry also has older roots. Enoch's astronomical revelations and Metatron's role as heavenly organizer create a path toward later images of proportion and cosmic structure. But the object commonly called Metatron's Cube belongs to much later symbolic developments. It should not be treated as an ancient object described in Third Enoch.

The distinction between ancient text and modern reception is essential. A name can outlive the context that created it. Once Metatron entered Kabbalistic and esoteric culture, later readers could assign new meanings to him. Those meanings may be historically significant without being ancient.

One text supplies Enoch's disappearance. Another supplies his visions. Another supplies his movement through the heavens. Another supplies his transformation. Another supplies his dangerous proximity to the throne. Another supplies his role in mystical ascent. Another supplies his place in the transmission of primordial wisdom.

The angel is assembled through manuscripts.

The transmission of Metatron's identity depends on scribes, translators, compilers, and interpreters. First Enoch survives most fully in Ethiopic, with earlier Aramaic fragments among the Dead Sea Scrolls. Second Enoch survives in Church Slavonic manuscripts. Third Enoch survives in Hebrew manuscripts with a complicated textual history. Rabbinic traditions are preserved in later compilations of oral and written material. Kabbalistic works circulate through medieval manuscripts and commentaries. Sefer Raziel exists in varying forms and editions.

There is no single original Metatron manuscript.

There are fragments, witnesses, recensions, quotations, and reinterpretations.

That instability reveals how religious ideas travel. A tradition is copied, translated, expanded, corrected, challenged, and made useful in new settings. Enoch became Metatron through that process.

The transformation also depended on a particular view of scripture. Genesis leaves Enoch's destination unexplained. Later authors could claim that they were not inventing a new figure but explaining what the biblical wording implied.

Absence becomes productive. Genesis supplies the unexplained taking. Apocalyptic authors fill the gap with heavenly journeys. Mystical authors fill it with angelic transformation. Rabbinic authors fill it with theological warning. Kabbalists fill it with cosmic symbolism.

Each filling of the gap creates a new Enoch.

The patriarch of Genesis is quiet, genealogical, and brief. Metatron is radiant, named, ranked, seated, punished, and surrounded by books. Yet both remain connected by the same sentence: God took him.

The historical record can establish the broad movement. Genesis supplied the unexplained taking. First Enoch made Enoch a visionary and heavenly scribe. Second Enoch brought him through the heavens and described a movement toward angelic status. Rabbinic literature recognized Metatron as a figure of extraordinary rank whose position could be misunderstood. Third Enoch made the identification with Enoch explicit and called him the lesser Yahweh. Hekhalot literature placed him within the heavenly palaces. Medieval Kabbalah and esoteric traditions expanded his symbolic reach.

Historians cannot establish that a historical man named Enoch became an angel. They can establish that later Jewish texts interpreted Genesis in that direction, that those texts differ in date and form, and that scholars disagree about the precise relationships among them. The dates of several works, the origin of the name Metatron, and the first moment when Enoch was identified with Metatron remain uncertain.

What survives is a documented history of interpretation. A brief biblical ancestor became a visionary, a heavenly traveler, a transformed angel, a scribe, a guide, and a bearer of delegated divine authority. The phrase "God took him" became the foundation for a figure who stood close to the throne while remaining, in the texts that preserved him, subordinate to the one God.
