Full script

The Picatrix is the largest handbook of star magic the Middle Ages produced. It is four books that claim to teach how the power of the planets can be drawn down into an object made by human hands. A ring, a stone, a figure cut in metal, a small image buried at a doorway: each, the book says, can be made to hold a portion of the sky, and to go on acting after its maker has gone home.

Here is one item from inside it, so that you know the kind of book this is. In its second book, the first ten degrees of the Ram, the opening stretch of the zodiac, are given a face, and the face is a person. The book describes a tall black man with red eyes, wrapped about the body in a white cloth, strong and quick to anger. It says this is how the Indians pictured that stretch of the sky. Thirty-six such figures follow, one for every ten degrees of the heavens, and each is meant to be carved, painted or cast.

It is a strange thing to find in a book written in tenth-century Cordoba by a man steeped in Arabic philosophy. It is stranger still to find it copied by Latin scribes in Toledo and in Florence. The Picatrix is not the voice of one tradition. It is a gathering, and it draws together Greek astrology, Indian star lore, the temple religion of the pagans of Harran, alchemy, Neoplatonic philosophy and farming lore from Iraq. One compiler stitched them together, and he tells us how many books he read to do it.

The road ahead is simple. The Picatrix has four books, and we will take them in order, as the compiler arranged them. Book One lays down the theory and the first worked examples. Book Two gives the figures of the sky. Book Three, the part most used, turns to the seven planets and to how each was addressed. Book Four reaches toward spirit, prayer and marvel. By the end you will understand what a talisman was, how one was made, how a planet was addressed in the book's rites, and why the Moon stands at the start of every operation the book describes.

Two things I am keeping for last, because the book keeps them for last. The first is its own answer to the question of where a sage's power really comes from. It is a doctrine it calls Perfect Nature, and it makes much of what comes before look like preparation. The second is a city called Adocentyn, which the book says Hermes built in Egypt, with four gates, guardian images and a tower whose light changed with the days of the week.

Everything between here and there is the staircase. Where the book's own words come up, I give them in paraphrase, because the English translations belong to their modern translators. I will also say plainly what the book claims and what it only reports, and leave the rest to history.

So we begin where the book begins: with the man who made it, and what he said he was doing.

In the middle of the tenth century Cordoba was the capital of a caliphate that took learning seriously. Its rulers collected books the way other courts collected soldiers. The chroniclers speak of a royal library running to hundreds of thousands of volumes, a figure to be held loosely but not dismissed. They speak also of agents sent east to Baghdad, Cairo and Damascus to buy or copy whatever was worth having. Al-Andalus, the Muslim-ruled part of the Iberian peninsula, was at that moment one of the places where a man could read nearly everything the ancient and eastern worlds had written about the stars.

One man set out to read it all, and to arrange it. He called his work the Ghayat al-Hakim, which means The Goal of the Sage. The word hakim is wide. It can mean a sage, a philosopher, or a physician, and it names someone who knows how the world is put together and acts on that knowledge. The Latin world would know the book by another name, the Picatrix, and we will come to that name in its place.

The author tells us a good deal about how he worked. He says the Goal took him about six years, in the nine fifties of our era, and that he wrote it after finishing a companion book on alchemy called the Rutbat al-Hakim, whose title gives the sage a rank or degree. The alchemy was the step, and the Goal was to be where the step led. He also says he drew the book from two hundred and twenty-four works.

That is a compiler's boast and a compiler's confession in one breath. He is not claiming to have invented anything. He is claiming to have read widely and sifted carefully. His method is what makes the Picatrix a book you can learn from, because he usually tells you what school an idea comes from and sometimes where he thinks it went wrong.

Who was he? For centuries the answer was Maslama al-Majriti, the astronomer of Madrid. His name stands in many manuscripts, and the Latin readers inherited it. Hellmut Ritter and Martin Plessner, whose German translation of nineteen sixty-two is the great modern landmark, already carried a doubt in their title, which calls the author a pseudo-Majriti.

In nineteen ninety-six Maribel Fierro argued for a different man: Maslama al-Qurtubi, Maslama of Cordoba, who died in nine sixty-four. Her case runs through the Rutbat al-Hakim. The Goal names that alchemical book as its companion, and the biographical record ties the Rutbat to the Cordoban Maslama. If the companion is his, the Goal is his. His dates also fit the nine fifties. The title of her study speaks of Batinism, the habit of reading an inner meaning beneath outer forms, which suggests the sort of mind that could join astrology, philosophy and religion into one book.

Now to what the book says in its first pages. It begins by saying what magic is. The author's definition is broad and philosophical: magic is whatever acts upon minds and souls by causes the senses cannot perceive. By that measure the art is not a trick of the hands. It concerns forces that pass between the sky, the material world and the human soul, and the book assumes those forces are real and orderly.

Next it divides the art in two. There is a theory, which is knowledge of the heavens and of the natures of things. There is a practice, which is the making of images, the burning of incense and the speaking of prayers. The book insists the two belong together. An operation done without the theory is, in its view, merely a gesture, and it fills much of its first book with theory for exactly that reason.

Then it explains a word you will hear many times in this episode: talisman. The compiler explains it as meaning something like a thing that dominates, a figure that holds sway over whatever it is set upon. A talisman, in his sense, is not a lucky charm. It is an object made at a chosen moment so that the force of a heavenly body rules over some part of the world, a harvest, a household, a body, a city.

Then come the sources, and here you should note how mixed the company is. There are the Sabians of Harran, star-worshipping pagans whose temples we will visit later. There is the Nabatean Agriculture of Ibn Wahshiyya, a work put into Arabic in the early tenth century and presented as a translation of ancient Nabatean lore, full of plant wonders. There are the Brethren of Purity, a fellowship of philosophers who wrote an encyclopedia of epistles. There is Jabir ibn Hayyan, the great name of Arabic alchemy.

There are books carrying the names of Hermes, Aristotle and Plato, and there is Indian astrology, which reached the author by way of Abu Ma'shar, the ninth-century astrologer of Baghdad.

With that library behind him, the compiler opens his first book not with a ring or an incense but with the shape of the cosmos.

A practitioner following the Picatrix would not have begun with metal. He would have begun with a table of numbers. Before any figure was cut or any incense kindled, he needed to know where the Moon stood, which sign she was passing through, which planets looked at her, and what hour of what day it was. The book's first great lesson is that an act performed at the wrong moment is simply the wrong act. Timing is not a refinement of the art. It is the art.

To see why, you need the single idea on which the book rests. Everything below is moved by what is above. The world under the Moon is made of the four elements, fire, air, water and earth, and it is the world of change, where things are born, mix, decay and die. Above the Moon lie the heavens: seven planets, counting the Sun and the Moon, twelve signs of the zodiac, and the fixed stars beyond them. The book treats these as ordered, living powers.

From them, it says, a spiritual force pours continually downward, and it is this force that shapes what happens on earth. The planets differ in their natures. Saturn is cold and dry, Jupiter temperate and fortunate, Mars hot and fierce, the Sun royal and warming, Venus gentle and moist, Mercury quick and changeable, the Moon cool and receptive. The signs add their own tints, and so do the fixed stars.

Now the second half of the idea. This force falls on everything, but it does not take hold everywhere equally. A thing receives it according to its own fitness. The teacher's analogy is a seal pressed into wax: the same seal leaves a clear mark in warm wax and none in stone. The book's claim is that a figure made of the right material, with the right form, at the moment when the right force is pouring down, becomes such wax. It takes the impression and keeps it.

Hence the importance of the hour. The Arabic astrologers divided each day and each night into twelve hours, each ruled in turn by a planet in a fixed order: Saturn, Jupiter, Mars, the Sun, Venus, Mercury, the Moon, and then round again. The first hour of a day belongs to the planet that gives the day its name, so Saturday opens with Saturn and Sunday with the Sun. When the book later says a rite is to be done on a certain day and in a certain hour, this is the system it means.

But above all planets stands the Moon, and this is the part of the theory the book returns to most often. She is the nearest heavenly body to the earth and the swiftest of the planets, completing her round of the zodiac in about a month. In the cosmos of the Picatrix she is the last gate before the world of change. As she travels she meets each planet in turn, joins it, looks upon it, and takes up its influence. Then, being closest to us, she passes it down.

Every other planet's force reaches the earthly world, the book teaches, through her. That is why every operation in the book is dated by the Moon's place first. It is also why the book gives so much space to the path she travels, which brings us to the mansions.

The Moon takes about twenty-seven and a third days to circle the sky, so the Arabic astronomers divided her road into twenty-eight stations, one for each night's lodging. These are the mansions of the Moon. Each covers a little under thirteen degrees of the zodiac, and each is named for a star or cluster marking its place. The first lies at the horns of the Ram. The third is the Pleiades, which the Arabs called al-Thurayya. The fourth is the Eye of the Bull, the bright star we call Aldebaran.

Book One lists all twenty-eight, and for each it says what the Moon's stay there was good for, what it was bad for, and what kinds of image could be made in it. I will not recite the full table, because the entries run to a line or two each and a recitation would drown you. Listen instead to the pattern.

Some mansions the book treats as favourable for founding and building, for raising a house, laying out a city, planting and sowing. Others it treats as suited to separating, and in them it says the operator who wanted to sow discord between friends, or between husband and wife, would do his work. Others are named for journeys, for the endurance of a besieged place, for the recovery of what has been lost or buried. Some it calls plainly unfortunate, and an astrologer was to leave them alone for any good purpose.

How did a working astrologer use the list? He found the Moon's longitude from astronomical tables or from an instrument, matched it to its mansion, and read the entry. Then he checked who aspected her and whether any planet was unkindly placed. If the stage looked right and the aim matched, he marked the hour on which the Moon would reach it and began his preparations.

The book is even-handed about the dark side of the list. It does not hide the mansions that serve harm, and it does not recommend them. It simply says what each is for. That is how a compiler writes when he is trying to be complete.

With the sky mapped and the Moon's road laid out, the book turns from the theory to the making.

Picture a roof in al-Andalus, a clear night, and a man waiting with a plate of metal in his lap and a tool beside him. He has been watching a point on the horizon for an hour. He is waiting not for a hand on a clock but for the Moon to enter a particular sign while a particular planet is well placed. That is the scene the first book teaches, and it comes down to a few elements that the book describes in each of its worked examples.

First, an aim. Second, a planetary condition: the hour, the day, the sign where the Moon stands, and the planets that favour or oppose her. Third, a material, usually a metal or stone, chosen because it belongs to the ruling planet. Fourth, an image, the form that gives the force something to take shape in. Fifth, a fumigation, the burning of the incense proper to the planet. Sixth, words, spoken to the planet's spirit. Seventh, a placing: the finished object was buried, hung, worn, or hidden in a particular spot.

Take the love image, which is one of the most familiar of the book's operations. The book describes an image made for friendship or love between two people. The operator waited until the Moon and Venus stood in a favourable relation, and made two figures, in a pairing or embrace that shows the bond the work was meant to create. He then burned incense belonging to the planet of love, named the two persons, and asked the spirit of the planet to join them. The finished image was kept or placed where it would remain undisturbed.

The harmful operations run the other way, and the book treats them as part of the art. For the ruin of an enemy or of a city, it describes images made when the Moon stood in an unfortunate place, in the company of the harmful planets Saturn and Mars. The figure represented what was to be harmed. It was then buried or concealed in the enemy's territory or at the site chosen for ruin. The book reports the method without apology, and you will notice that the same ordered theory serves both blessing and cursing. What changes is only the condition of the sky.

The protective images are gentler, and here the book is closest to a country almanac. A figure made for driving scorpions or mice from a house was timed to a sign or a mansion connected with the creature or with its banishment. It was made in the proper material and buried at the threshold or at the corners of the place to be cleared. The reasoning is the book's usual one. A figure shaped like the creature takes the impression of the sky's force against it, and the force repels the creatures it resembles.

The physician's image is of the same family. It was meant to bring success to a doctor, so that his cures took and his patients trusted him. The book ties it to the planets that govern healing and to a moment when the Moon was well placed. The physician carried it or kept it in his house.

The image for an abundant harvest follows the same logic from the other side. It was made when the Moon stood in a sign fitted to the earth and the fortunate planets looked kindly on her. It was buried in the field or kept in the granary. In a land where everything depended on the rains and the crops, the planting calendars and the talismans were probably not so far apart in the minds of the people who used them.

Having described what is done, the book turns to the person who does it, and here it is surprisingly demanding. The operator, it says, needs a single-minded will. A divided mind will not impress its intention on the work. He needs belief: a man who doubts the art does not receive its effects. He needs secrecy: the book repeatedly says that the work should not be shown to fools, or boasted of.

Behind these three conditions sits a doctrine you should hold onto, because it explains the others. In the book's philosophy, man is a small world, a microcosm, and the heavens are the great world. Everything that lies in the heavens has its counterpart in the human being: spirit, soul, humours, powers. A man who fixes his will, his imagination and his belief on a single end is, in the book's reading, acting as the sky acts. His soul imprints the form, the sky supplies the force, and the material provides the wax.

A talisman, then, comes down to three things working together: a moment, a material and a mind. That is the heart of what the first book teaches, and the rest of the Picatrix is built to supply each of the three in greater detail.

The second book takes up the first of them in earnest, with the pictures that the sky itself is said to contain.

The second book of the Picatrix opens onto a sky full of figures. The zodiac is a belt of twelve signs, each thirty degrees wide. Each sign is divided into three stretches of ten degrees. The Arabic and Latin astrologers called each such stretch a face, or a decan, and there are thirty-six in all. Each face has a planet as its lord, and the lords follow a fixed order that begins with Mars at the head of the Ram. Mars rules the first face of the Ram, the Sun the second, Venus the third, and the sequence continues around the whole circle.

What makes the faces interesting is that each one also has a figure, a human or animal form said to stand for the quality of that stretch of heaven. The book gives these in detail, and as I said at the start, the first of them is the tall black man with red eyes in his white cloth. The book credits many of these descriptions to the Indians, which is its way of citing Abu Ma'shar and the Indian astrology that reached the Arabic world through him.

If you listen to the descriptions as a whole, you hear a whole cast. There are armed men and women, kings and servants, men holding tools and women holding children, and creatures of every kind. Each carries a mood and a destiny. The book says each figure shows the character of the people born under that face, and, for the maker of talismans, shows what shape to give an image when that face is rising over the horizon.

Why should a picture have force? Here the book uses the same principle it used before: like acts on like. A figure in the likeness of the sky's own figure resembles it, and what resembles a heavenly power can receive that power. The pictures are not decoration. They are the means by which an image becomes a vessel.

Alongside the thirty-six faces, the book tells of the forty-eight constellations that the Greek astronomers had set out, the Bull, the Scorpion, the Archer and the rest, now pictured as living forms. They are not abstractions to the author. They are shapes in the sky that a craftsman could reproduce in stone or metal, and the maker was to follow the picture that the heavens themselves offered.

Then the book turns to the planets. For each of the seven it gives an image to be engraved on a stone, and it assigns each planet its metal and its stones. The assignments follow the order that most readers of astrology know. Gold goes with the Sun, silver with the Moon, lead with Saturn, tin with Jupiter, iron with Mars, copper with Venus and quicksilver with Mercury. When the book wants an image to bear a planet's force, it asks the maker to cut it in the planet's own substance, so that the sympathy between the planet and the material helps the work.

The planetary figures themselves are vivid, and they are the ones the book gives in the shape that the planet was imagined to wear. Saturn, in the form the book reports, is a man with a raven's face and the feet of a camel, seated on a throne. You should notice how unlike a modern diagram this is. Nothing here is geometric. The book thinks in pictures, and it expects its reader to think in them too.

Now a story that carries these pictures out of the Arabic world and into the Renaissance. In nineteen twelve the German art historian Aby Warburg gave a lecture at an international congress in Rome. He had been studying a room in Ferrara, in the Palazzo Schifanoia, the pleasure palace of the Este family, whose Hall of the Months was painted in the fourteen sixties for Borso d'Este. Each wall is divided into bands. At the top ride the gods, and in the lowest band the duke's court goes hunting and judging. In the middle band, between them, stand the signs of the zodiac, each attended by three strange figures. Nobody had been able to say who they were.

Warburg identified them. They were the faces, the decans, in the forms the Arabic astrologers had handed down from Indian sources, and he traced them step by step: from India through Persia and the Arabic world, through Spain and the Latin translations, to the painters of Ferrara. The painters of the fourteen sixties, working for a Christian duke, were illustrating Indian star lore that had passed through Cordoba's world. The Picatrix is one of the books in which those descriptions were carried, and it is a witness to the same tradition he followed.

When you look at the Ferrara frescoes now, what you are looking at is the visible end of a long road. The figures that the compiler in Cordoba listed in his Book Two are walking across a wall in Italy five centuries later.

The planets that stand behind those faces are where the book's third part begins, and it is the part the Latin readers used most.

Book Three is where the Picatrix stops teaching the heavens and begins addressing them. Most of what its readers copied, quoted and feared comes from this book. It is organised around the seven planets, taking each in turn, and for each it first sets out a dominion: everything on earth that belongs to that planet.

Let me tell you the shape of a dominion. Saturn rules the old and the slow, labourers and those who work the earth, and things dark, heavy and cold. His colour is black and his metal is lead. Jupiter is the planet of judges, scholars and the fortunate, and his metal is tin. Mars rules soldiers, butchers and those who work with fire and iron; his colour is red, and the book gives him dominion over blood. The Sun is the king's planet, golden in colour and gold in metal, and rules the heart. Venus belongs to women, music, perfumes and pleasure. Mercury rules scribes, merchants and those who live by wit, and his metal is quicksilver. The Moon rules messengers and travellers, the colour white, and the metal silver.

Each dominion also extends to cloths, inks, incenses and the parts of the body. So a rite to Saturn, for example, would use black cloth, black ink and incense proper to Saturn, and everything involved in the rite would speak the planet's language. What the book is building is a consistent world in which a petitioner can speak to a planet in its own terms.

The book then tells how it was done. For each planet it gives a rite of address, and I will tell you two of them as the book describes them, because they show the pattern.

Take Saturn. A petitioner following the book waited for Saturn's day and Saturn's hour. He dressed in black, wore a ring on his hand, and burned on the coals the incense that belonged to the planet. He stood facing the planet, in a clean place. The prayer he spoke, in close paraphrase, addressed Saturn by his own qualities. It called him the lord of a great and distant sphere, cold, dry and barren, solitary and sorrowful. It begged him, by those very attributes, to look kindly on the petitioner and grant what was asked. The strangeness of this address is deliberate. You flatter a planet by naming what it is.

Now Mars. The rite fell on Mars's day and Mars's hour, and the petitioner dressed in red and came armed, as a soldier would. The incense was Mars's own. The prayer called him the warrior of the heavens, hot and bitter, a lord of battle and of blood, and asked him for victory and strength. A king or a soldier, one imagines, would have found this the most natural of the planetary rites. The book was not narrow in its audience.

For the Moon the frame is the same, with garments and a ring of her own. The prayer addresses her as the lady of the night, the swift, the messenger who carries the other planets' forces, and, since she is the carrier of all influences, it asks that she bring down to the petitioner the gift of whichever planet he has in mind. It is the gentlest of the rites and, as the book's whole theory would lead you to expect, the one most often used together with the others.

Each rite follows the same frame: a day, an hour, a garment, a ring, an incense, a posture, an address. The book then says what the petitioner might ask for and promises that the planet will answer. That promise is the book's, not ours, and it is the book's users who staked their hopes on it.

Where did all this come from? The book is clear. It credits these rites to the Sabians of Harran. Harran was an ancient city in upper Mesopotamia, in what is now south-eastern Turkey, and for centuries it had been a centre of planetary religion. Its temples were dedicated to the planets, and above all to the Moon. The people of Harran survived into the Islamic period, and the Arabic sources tell a famous story about how.

The story goes that a caliph, passing through Harran on campaign, asked the townspeople what religion they followed. They were not Christians, Jews or Muslims, and so, under the law, they might be forced to convert or be punished. Someone suggested they call themselves Sabians, a name that the Quran lists among the peoples who receive protection. They took the name and kept their temples. From then on the Sabians of Harran appear in Arabic writings as a learned, mysterious community, said to venerate Hermes as a prophet and to know the correct ways of addressing the planets.

Harran also produced astronomers and translators who worked in Baghdad. So when the compiler of the Picatrix writes about Sabian rites, he is not describing a pure fantasy. He is reporting a tradition he knew from books, whether or not he ever saw a Sabian temple. The authority of the Sabians for him was that they had been priests of the planets, and that they knew how a man should stand before a star.

All of this, the dominions and the rites, is the outer side of Book Three. The inner side is stranger, and it is the doctrine I promised to keep for last.

Now we reach the thing I have been holding back. Somewhere in the middle of Book Three, after the planetary rites, the Picatrix stops describing what a magician can do and begins to say what a magician is. The chapter is called by the name Perfect Nature, and I think it is the most important chapter in the whole book. That is my reading, and it goes further than scholars would sign. All the talismans and prayers, I would argue, are the exercises. This chapter names what the exercises are for.

Here is the teaching. The book says, giving it under the names of Hermes, Socrates and Aristotle, that every sage has a personal spirit joined to his own star. The spirit is not a visitor from outside. It is the spiritual form of the sage himself, the perfected shape of his nature, and it stands behind him like a companion. When the sage comes to know it, the spirit opens knowledge to him: it teaches him the sciences, shows him what to do, and guards him from harm. The one who has found his Perfect Nature has reached the goal the book's title names.

The book tells a story about how this was learned, and it makes Hermes the hero. Hermes, it says, set out to find the hidden cause of the sages' mastery, and sought it through long effort. At the end a figure appeared to him, and the figure instructed him. It explained that it was his Perfect Nature, the spirit joined to his star, and it taught him how a sage comes to reach it and to live with it. The book offers this as an account of the first discovery, and everything the later sages knew, it implies, they learned by the same road.

There is a quiet but radical idea inside this story. Ordinary astral magic puts the power in the sky and in the material. Perfect Nature puts the power in the sage. The planets, the incense, the images are all means by which the sage's own spirit is awakened and brought into harmony with his star. The talismans, on this reading, are not so much machines as aids to attunement.

The book gives the spirit four names, and the sage was to call it by them. The details of the names belong to the book and to its manuscripts, and I will not try to render them, because each copyist handled them differently. What matters is that the spirit has names, and that naming it was part of how a sage entered into relation with it.

The book also describes how the sage honoured it. According to the chapter, he prepared a ritual meal for the spirit. Food and drink were set out in a clean place, incense was burned, and the sage addressed the spirit and thanked it. The book says that the spirit takes pleasure in this, and that the sage who honours it faithfully finds it present. Here we see something close to a household cult, a guardian spirit fed and revered. I would put it more boldly: it reads very like a guardian-angel doctrine, set in Neoplatonic and astrological dress.

Now the rest of Book Three's inner material, which surrounds this centre. The book gives the names of the planets' spirits, the servants and ruling powers each planet commands. These names are strange to the eye, because they have passed through Greek, Syriac, Arabic and Latin, and every copyist has altered them a little. I will not read them out, partly because I cannot be sure of the forms and partly because a list of names would tell you nothing. What matters is that the book treats the planets as populated by spirits, which a petitioner could address as well as the planet itself.

It also gives confections: compound incenses and preparations of plants and other substances, made at the right planetary hours and said to sharpen the mind, steady the memory, or attune the operator to a planet. These recipes are what the later Latin readers found most practical and most suspect, since they seemed to turn the book from philosophy into pharmacy. Their ingredients vary, and I will not guess at them.

And the book gives cautions. The sage must keep the work from the unworthy. He must be pure in body and steady in faith. A mistake in the hour, the book warns, could spoil the whole operation. A man who revealed the teaching to fools would lose its fruit. The compiler wants his reader to feel that this is a serious art, and that carelessness is not just wasteful but costly.

Notice what this does to the rest of the book. The first three books have taught a sage to read the sky, shape the material, and address the planets. Perfect Nature says that all of this is a way toward being what the sage already potentially is. The goal the title names is not a power over objects. It is the meeting of the sage with his own spirit.

One book remains, and with it the second thing I promised.

Book Four begins with philosophy, as if the compiler wanted to steady the reader after the planetary rites. Its opening chapters deal with spirit, soul and intellect. The book describes the human being as made of three levels. The spirit is a fine substance, a bridge between the body and the soul. The soul is what wills and imagines. The intellect is the highest part, which can rise toward the divine order. The magician's will works through the spirit, which carries the soul's intention outward. This is the same microcosm doctrine you met in Part Three, now set out as a full psychology.

Then Book Four does something no other part of the Picatrix does. It gives prayers addressed to the Moon as she passes through each of the twelve signs. By now you understand why the Moon: she is the carrier, the gate through which every planetary force descends. The prayers treat her accordingly. In each sign she is addressed in terms suited to the sign and asked to bring down what that sign can give. It is the gentlest part of the book, a sequence of petitions to the one body that stands between heaven and earth.

After the prayers come the marvels. The book gathers wonders from the Nabatean Agriculture of Ibn Wahshiyya, which was a book about farming and plants that had grown into a book about hidden properties. The wonders are plants and substances with strange effects, remedies and protections, and they show the compiler treating agriculture and magic as one field. The book does not stand back from these marvels. It reports them as natural facts that the wise should know.

Then a collection of sayings. The compiler gathers the sayings of the sages, Hermes among them, short remarks on wisdom, the soul, the stars and the ways of the philosopher. This is a common habit in Arabic books of learning. After the practical and the marvellous, the reader is left with the voice of the ancient sages.

And then the second of the two things held back: the city.

The book tells of Adocentyn. It says that Hermes built this city in Egypt, and the account is the closest the Picatrix comes to a story. The city is a place built entirely under the stars, and it is a talisman writ large. The book says Hermes chose the site and the time by the heavens and raised the city with the sage's full knowledge of the planets.

The book gives its measure: a city twelve miles long, in the east of Egypt, and inside it a castle with four gates, one facing each quarter of the world. At each gate stood a guardian image. Over the eastern gate Hermes set the form of an eagle, over the western a bull, over the southern a lion, and over the northern a dog. These were not statues for show. The book says he brought spirits into them, so that the images spoke with voices, and no one could pass through a gate of the city without their leave.

Within the walls he planted trees, and in the midst of them one great tree that bore the fruit of every kind. Near the city were waters full of fish of many sorts.

On the summit of the castle rose a tower thirty cubits high, and the tower is the part of the account that listeners remember. At its top Hermes set a beacon, and the book says its light changed colour with each day of the week, returning to the first colour when seven days had passed. On the day of Saturn it shone with Saturn's colour, on the day of the Sun with the Sun's, and so on around the seven. The city was literally lit by the planetary week. The dominions that Book Three described so carefully, the black of Saturn and the red of Mars and the white of the Moon, here stand as a single great tower, colouring the whole place in turn.

Around the city Hermes set further images. The book says they were made to keep the people virtuous and safe. They guarded the inhabitants against disease and harm, and they kept them from vice and from quarrelling with one another. The city's laws, in effect, were written into its images. The book describes a place where the stars, the stones and the people worked together, so that the good order of the heavens became the good order of a community.

I would call Adocentyn the largest talisman in the book. That is a second bold reading, and I offer it as a reading. Every earlier operation had made a small object serve a small aim. Here the same method is applied to a whole city, and the people who live in it are inside the image. The compiler seems to want to end by showing what astral magic would look like if it were done on the grandest scale and for the highest end: a city governed by the sky, where virtue was not commanded but built in.

Whether the compiler thought such a city had ever existed, we cannot say. He reports it as a tradition, attributing it to Hermes, and he does not claim to have seen it. For the later readers, though, it was one of the book's most haunting images, because it presented magic as the foundation of civilisation instead of an exception to it.

With the city we have walked the whole length of the Picatrix. What remains is its journey, from Cordoba, to the Spanish court, and on to the studies of Renaissance Europe.

In the year twelve fifty-six, at the court of Alfonso the Tenth of Castile, the king we call Alfonso the Wise, an order was given. The Arabic Ghayat al-Hakim was to be translated into Spanish. Alfonso's court at Toledo was one of the great translating centres of the age. It turned books on astronomy, astrology, games and law from Arabic into the language of Castile, and he took a personal interest in the work. Of that Spanish version only fragments survive.

But from the Spanish a Latin version was made, and in that version the book acquired a new name: the Picatrix. Where the name came from is still not settled. Some have thought it a corruption of an Arabic name, some have thought it a Latin invention, and the scribes themselves seem to have treated it as the name of the author, a wise man called Picatrix. The original Arabic compiler of Cordoba vanishes behind it. The Latin Picatrix is the book the Renaissance knew.

It is worth saying that the book was never printed. It did not appear in the great waves of fifteenth- and sixteenth-century printing. It travelled only in manuscript, copied by hand, passed from one reader to another. That fact shaped its reputation. A book that is not printed is a book that must be sought out, and the people who sought out the Picatrix were people who wanted it.

One of them was Marsilio Ficino, the Florentine priest and philosopher who translated Plato and the works attributed to Hermes Trismegistus. In fourteen eighty-nine he published a book on life, in three parts, which discussed how to live well under the stars and how images and substances could draw down celestial benefits. Frances Yates, in nineteen sixty-four, showed how much of the magic in Ficino's book belonged to the tradition that the Picatrix carried. Liana Saif, in twenty fifteen, argued for the Arabic sources behind early modern occult philosophy. Ficino drew on the book's ideas cautiously. He kept his distance from its rites and insisted that celestial images should serve health. He was a Christian priest, writing in a world that watched such books closely.

A generation later came Cornelius Agrippa. His Occult Philosophy, which appeared in the fifteen-thirties, is a great catalogue of magic in three books, and large parts of it can be traced to the Picatrix. He took over the twenty-eight mansions of the Moon with their uses, and he took over many of the planetary and decan images. Agrippa had once served Maximilian the First, the Holy Roman Emperor, and the emperor himself is recorded as owning a copy of the Picatrix, a reminder that the book was not only in the hands of obscure magicians. It sat on the shelves of a court.

Then comes the joke. In the fifteen-forties François Rabelais, writing his comic cycle about the giants, has a character speak of the reverend father in the devil Picatrix, rector of the diabolical faculty, whom he met at Toledo. It is a delighted piece of mockery. Rabelais's readers apparently knew enough about Toledo, devils and the Picatrix to see the joke. By the middle of the sixteenth century, then, the name of the book had passed into French comedy as a byword for the black arts of Spain.

Why did readers keep it out of sight? The reasons are plain enough. The Church had long condemned the making of images to attract spirits, and the Picatrix addressed planets as powers and described rites close to prayer. Anyone who owned it risked suspicion. So its readers copied it privately, kept it in private libraries, borrowed its material without naming it, and let it circulate among a small circle of the learned. That is part of why the book's influence is so hard to measure: much of it was deliberately unspoken.

And that is the paradox of its reception. A book that was never printed and rarely named nonetheless worked its way into Ficino's philosophy, Agrippa's encyclopaedia, a painted hall in Ferrara and a joke in Rabelais. The Picatrix was an underground classic before the term existed.

It would be another four centuries before anyone edited it as a scholar edits a text, and the story of that recovery brings us to the end.

The modern recovery of the Picatrix is a story of libraries and patient scholars. Its first chapter belongs to the Warburg circle, the group of researchers gathered around the library that Aby Warburg built in Hamburg. They were interested in how the images of ancient astrology travelled across cultures, from India and Greece to the Arabic world and then to the Renaissance, and the Picatrix was an obvious witness.

In nineteen thirty-three Hellmut Ritter published the Arabic text of the Ghayat al-Hakim, the first scholarly edition of the book in its original language. It was the foundation. Until then, the Latin was a translation of a text no one had properly read.

In nineteen sixty-two, Ritter and Martin Plessner published their German translation, with the notes that still guide every reader. Then in nineteen eighty-six David Pingree edited the Latin version, the Picatrix itself, the text that the Renaissance actually read. Pingree's edition made it possible to compare the Arabic and the Latin line by line and to see what the translators had kept, softened or dropped.

English readers had to wait. The first English translations appeared only in the twenty-first century, and in twenty nineteen Dan Attrell and David Porreca published a full scholarly translation of the Latin. After more than a thousand years, a book that had travelled from Cordoba to Toledo to Florence finally had a standard English text.

What was the Picatrix, taken whole? Not one tradition but a gathering of many. In its pages you have heard Greek astrology, Indian decan figures, the temple religion of Harran, the philosophy of the Brethren of Purity, alchemy under the name of Jabir ibn Hayyan, agricultural wonders from the Nabatean Agriculture and a hundred voices under the names of Hermes, Plato and Aristotle. They were carried from Harran and Baghdad to Cordoba, then to Toledo and Florence, and they were copied by hand all the way.

You have also seen what it asked of those who followed it. It asked the astrologer to read the sky with exactness. It asked the maker to choose his material and shape his image. It asked the petitioner to address each planet in its own language. And it asked all of them to believe that man is a small world, answering to the great one.

Now return to the doctrine I held back. The book teaches that the sage's power is not finally in the metals, the images or the incense. It is in the spirit joined to his star, the Perfect Nature that opens knowledge to him and guards him. The planets, the Moon and her mansions, the faces, the confections and the prayers are all ways of approaching that meeting.

The title tells you so. The Goal of the Sage is not a talisman. It is the sage's own other self, found at last.

And that is where the road ends.
