The Anunnaki: What the Sumerian Tablets Actually Say

The Anunna in the Sumerian and Babylonian texts: children of An, the gods who dug the rivers in Atrahasis, humanity made from clay and a god's blood, the judges of Ereshkigal's underworld, Marduk's assignment of the gods in Enuma Elish, and how Zecharia Sitchin turned them into astronauts from Nibiru.

Full script

In Nippur, around eighteen hundred before the common era, a boy sat in a scribal house with a lump of wet clay before him.

The clay was not paper. It held its shape under pressure, and every sign had to be made before the surface stiffened. The boy held a cut reed in his fingers. Its end was triangular. A light push made a wedge. A turn made another. Several wedges joined into a sign that could carry a sound, a word, or an idea.

The teacher had placed an older tablet nearby. The boy copied from it one line at a time.

The story concerned the great gods called the Anunna. Before grain grew, before sheep had wool, before the work of fields and flocks had been assigned, the gods had known hunger. They had no bread. They had no clothing. Their world lacked the things that later made life possible.

The boy pressed the reed too deeply.

The teacher touched the clay beside the damaged sign. The correction did not require anger. A sign was either formed correctly or it was not. The boy smoothed the mistake with his thumb and waited for the surface to become even. Then he began again.

The tablet told of a time before the familiar order of the world. The Anunna were present, but grain and sheep were not. The gods had been brought forth before the food that sustained them. Their hunger belonged to the shape of the tale. The story did not ask whether the gods had descended from another star. It placed them inside a divine family and inside an economy of labour.

The boy copied the signs that named them.

The word was not yet the word known from modern books. Sumerian writing could be read in more than one way, and later Babylonian scribes inherited older names while changing their forms and meanings. A name could move across languages without remaining fixed. A group of gods could occupy one place in a Sumerian poem and another in a Babylonian epic.

Outside the school, Nippur was a city of temples, houses, courtyards, workrooms, and canals. Its scribes served institutions that measured grain, recorded animals, assigned labour, and preserved hymns and myths. The tablets made in such places were not all religious in the modern sense. Some counted barley. Some listed professions. Some trained beginners in signs. Others preserved stories of gods, kings, floods, journeys, quarrels, births, and death.

The boy's tablet belonged to that world.

He copied the Anunna as beings who stood near the beginning of order. In some texts they were the senior gods. In some they gathered in a divine assembly. In some they decreed fates. In others their name could refer more broadly to a company of gods. The texts did not give one stable description that applied in every century.

That variation matters because a modern image has gathered around the name.

Four thousand years after that lesson in Nippur, the Anunnaki appear in bestselling books, online videos, and illustrated accounts of ancient history as visitors from another world. In that version, the gods are astronauts. Their stories are treated as damaged reports of technology. Their descent from heaven becomes a flight from space. Their divine authority becomes evidence of advanced science.

The modern transformation has a traceable author. Zecharia Sitchin presented it most fully in The Twelfth Planet, published in nineteen seventy six. He argued that the Anunnaki came from a planet called Nibiru and had reached Earth in the distant past. He read Sumerian signs through a system that professional Assyriologists rejected, and he connected ancient gods with a planetary history not found in the Sumerian and Babylonian texts as those texts are translated by specialists.

The disagreement is therefore not between two equally supported readings of one sentence. It concerns the texts themselves, the languages in which they were written, and the methods used to translate them.

The ancient sources speak of gods who were born, quarrelled, judged, laboured, ruled cities, received offerings, and belonged to divine families. They speak of An, the sky, and Ki, the earth. They speak of Enlil, Enki, Ea, Ninmah, Nintu, and Mami. They speak of the Igigi, of human beings formed from clay, and of work imposed upon the human race.

They do not present one complete account of who made the Anunnaki. Sumerian traditions place the great gods within a family headed by An, while other traditions assign creation and kingship different roles. In the Babylonian Enuma Elish, Marduk rises to supremacy after the battle with Tiamat, and the creation of humans follows a different pattern from the one in Atrahasis.

The stories changed because scribes copied, adapted, combined, and recast them.

The question is not only what the name Anunnaki means. It is also what the gods do in each text, how their ranks are arranged, why the junior Igigi rebel, why humans are created, and how a modern writer turned a divine population into an extraterrestrial one.

Who were the Anunnaki in the texts themselves, who made them, and what did the scribes believe they were?

To answer, we must go back to the clay tablets of Mesopotamia.

The surviving stories began as marks pressed into clay, but the modern history of reading them began with broken pieces.

In the eighteen nineties, expeditions from the University of Pennsylvania worked at Nippur in southern Mesopotamia. The excavations recovered thousands of tablets and fragments from buildings associated with the ancient city. Many were administrative records. Others belonged to the scribal tradition that preserved Sumerian literary compositions.

The tablets had not been arranged for modern study. Some were broken in antiquity. Some had been discarded. Some belonged to different periods and had been deposited together only after long use. A story might survive in several copies, with gaps between them and differences in spelling or wording.

The recovery of Nippur changed the study of Sumerian literature because the city had been a major centre of scribal education. The tablets carried exercises, lexical lists, hymns, royal compositions, myths, and disputations. They showed that many texts known from later copies had passed through Old Babylonian schools around eighteen hundred before the common era.

That date is crucial. The Sumerian city-states had already existed for centuries. A tablet copied in an Old Babylonian school could preserve a Sumerian composition older than the copy, but the surviving object was still a later witness. The hand on the clay and the age of the story were not necessarily the same.

The first great discoveries came from Nineveh rather than Nippur.

In the nineteenth century, Austen Henry Layard excavated at Nineveh, the former capital of the Assyrian empire. Hormuzd Rassam later continued work there. Among the finds were tablets from the library of Ashurbanipal, the Assyrian king who ruled in the seventh century before the common era.

Ashurbanipal had been trained as a scribe. His library gathered copies of older works in Sumerian and Babylonian, along with lexical lists, rituals, omens, royal inscriptions, medical texts, and scholarly compositions. The tablets came from a much later period than the Old Babylonian school copies, but they preserved versions of stories whose history reached back centuries.

A broken tablet from that library carried a flood narrative.

George Smith, working in the British Museum, read cuneiform tablets from Nineveh. In eighteen seventy two he recognized a passage describing a flood, a ship, birds sent out after the waters receded, and the survival of a human hero. The discovery became famous because it resembled the flood account in Genesis. Smith's reading drew public attention to the fact that biblical stories belonged to a wider ancient Near Eastern literary world.

The reading of cuneiform had taken decades. The signs were not an alphabet. A single sign could represent a word, a syllable, or several related values. The same language could be written with signs borrowed from another language. Sumerian and Babylonian were not simply two forms of one language. Sumerian was a language isolate in the surviving record, while Babylonian belonged to the Semitic language family.

Scholars learned to compare repeated passages, bilingual lexical lists, royal inscriptions, grammatical patterns, and the contexts in which signs occurred. No single tablet supplied the whole key. Decipherment advanced through accumulated comparisons.

The name now written Anunnaki has its own history of reading. In Sumerian the form Anunna is common in modern scholarly transcription. In later Akkadian texts a related form appears as Anunnaki. The name is often explained as "offspring of An" or "princely seed," but the exact interpretation of the compound and its development across periods remain matters of philological discussion.

The meaning of the group also shifted.

A Sumerian text may use Anunna for a company of major gods. A Babylonian text may use Anunnaki for divine beings associated with judgment or the underworld. The word cannot be removed from its sentence and treated as the name of one unchanging species.

Jeremy Black and Anthony Green, in Gods, Demons and Symbols of Ancient Mesopotamia, describe the Anunna as a group of gods whose composition and function varied across the Mesopotamian tradition. Their account reflects a basic rule of cuneiform study: a divine name must be read in context.

The objects themselves set limits.

A tablet can show that a line was written, copied, corrected, or preserved. It can reveal the language, script, approximate period, and relationship between copies. It cannot reveal a private belief held by every person who heard the story. A school copy tells more about scribal education than about the response of every worshipper in Nippur.

The same caution applies to damaged passages. A broken edge can remove the name of a god, the subject of a verb, or an entire exchange. Restorations are based on parallels and grammar, but a restored line remains an interpretation when the clay no longer preserves it.

W. G. Lambert and A. R. Millard published Atra-hasis: The Babylonian Story of the Flood in nineteen sixty nine. Their edition joined fragments from different collections and provided a standard scholarly presentation of the Old Babylonian poem. The title uses Atra-hasis, the name of the human hero, although the story begins long before the flood, with the gods divided by labour.

Stephanie Dalley later included Atra-hasis and other Mesopotamian compositions in Myths from Mesopotamia. Such editions make the sequence readable, but they do not turn a scattered archive into one original manuscript. Each translation rests on the comparison of witnesses.

The Electronic Text Corpus of Sumerian Literature, produced at Oxford, presents transliterations and translations of many Sumerian compositions. It also makes visible the fragmentary nature of the evidence. A line may be secure because several tablets agree. Another may remain uncertain because only one damaged copy preserves it.

The old stories reached the modern world through this chain of objects: tablets made by ancient scribes, buried or discarded in Mesopotamian cities, excavated by nineteenth- and twentieth-century expeditions, transported into museums and collections, and read through the slow decipherment of cuneiform.

The chain is long, but it is not imaginary.

The clay can show what the scribes wrote. The surrounding evidence can show when and where copies were made. The interpretation must then follow the language rather than a later story imposed upon it.

The story begins before grain, before sheep, and before the ordered work of human beings.

In a Sumerian disputation known as The Debate between Sheep and Grain, the gods first appear in a world without the provisions that later sustain it. The Anunna are present at the beginning. They have been brought forth, but the things needed for food and clothing have not yet entered the world.

The text gives the scene through divine absence.

There is no cultivated grain. There are no sheep with wool. The gods lack the products that will later fill storehouses and clothe bodies. The Anunna are born before the gifts that make settled life possible, and they go hungry and naked.

The story does not begin with a human rebellion or with an alien arrival. It begins with a problem inside the divine order. The gods exist, but the world is not yet equipped for them.

The missing pair eventually takes form as two divine beings. Ashnan represents grain. Lahar represents sheep. They are brought into the world so that the gods can eat and drink, and so that clothing and offerings can become possible. The argument that follows gives the text its shape. Grain and sheep each claim priority. One provides food. The other provides meat, milk, wool, and the animals needed for sacrifice.

The Anunna hear the dispute.

Their role is not that of a family waiting for visitors from beyond the sky. They are the divine assembly before whom the value of the two gifts can be weighed. The story imagines abundance as something that must be distributed and ranked. Grain and sheep are not merely natural resources. They are divine powers with claims upon the gods.

The dispute ends with reconciliation and praise for both. The world needs grain and sheep together. The older hunger of the Anunna gives way to an economy in which the gods can be fed and clothed.

The story is brief, but its opening image is precise. The gods are not described as machines or travellers. They are dependent upon the food, textile, and sacrificial systems that later human communities will maintain.

Other Sumerian traditions approach the beginning from another direction.

An is the sky god. Ki is the earth. In the Sumerian conception preserved in several compositions, heaven and earth are joined at first. Their separation creates the space in which the gods, cities, plants, animals, and humans can exist. Enlil is associated with the act of separation in this tradition. Once heaven and earth are divided, the ordered world becomes possible.

The story is not presented in one complete Sumerian creation account. A passage may preserve a divine genealogy without explaining the entire beginning. Another may describe the separation of heaven and earth without listing every god born from it. A third may assume the world already exists and begin with a quarrel, a journey, or a royal decree.

The great gods are nevertheless treated as a divine family. An stands at the head of the heavenly order. Ki represents the earth. Their offspring include gods whose authority extends across the cosmos and the cities of southern Mesopotamia.

The texts do not always make the Anunna a simple biological generation. In some passages the name points to the offspring or seed of An. In others it identifies the great gods as an assembly. The word can mark rank, family, or a company of deities, depending on the composition and period.

The work of the Anunna is also expressed through judgment.

In Sumerian literature they appear as gods who decree the fates. This does not mean that every Anunna has the same office. It means that the group belongs to the upper level of divine authority. The fates of kings, cities, and human beings can be assigned in a divine assembly, and the Anunna are associated with that power.

The idea appears against a background of earthly administration. Nippur had scribes who measured grain, recorded labour, and preserved decisions in clay. Divine judgment was imagined in terms familiar to the city. A god could assign a fate as an official assigned a ration or a duty, though the texts did not reduce divine action to ordinary bureaucracy.

Enlil occupies a central place in this structure. He is a major god of Nippur, associated with authority, wind, and the ordering of the world. In traditions about the separation of heaven and earth, his action creates the conditions in which the other gods and living things can appear. In stories of labour and punishment, his house becomes the focus of complaint because he holds senior authority.

Enki, known as Ea in Akkadian, has a different character. He is associated with Eridu, with the subterranean freshwater domain called the Abzu, with wisdom, craft, and the protection of humans. When a story requires a plan, a technical solution, or a way through a divine decision, Enki often becomes the god who sees a possibility that others miss.

The arrangement is not a single theology written by one author. It is a set of traditions that overlap and sometimes compete.

Thorkild Jacobsen, in The Treasures of Darkness, interpreted Mesopotamian religion as a developing system in which divine powers were closely connected with the structures of city and state. His work emphasized the historical growth of the pantheon rather than a timeless, fixed catalogue of gods. The Anunna fit that pattern: a name inherited from earlier tradition but used within changing political and literary worlds.

The Debate between Sheep and Grain gives a glimpse of the earliest divine order known in that composition. The Anunna are born first. Grain and sheep follow. The gods' need for nourishment becomes the reason that productive life must be established.

The sequence matters.

The text does not say that the Anunna made all the gods from raw material. It does not offer one statement naming a single maker of the Anunna. Instead, Sumerian traditions place them among the descendants of An and Ki, while assigning particular acts of ordering to gods such as Enlil.

This is why a question about who made the Anunnaki has to be answered carefully. In one sense, the texts place the great gods within a genealogy descended from An. In another, the world is arranged by acts of separation, birth, naming, and decree. No single formula governs every text.

The clay copies confirm the variation.

The Old Babylonian school tablets from Nippur preserve literary Sumerian in a period when Sumerian was no longer the ordinary spoken language of the region. The compositions were copied by students and trained scribes. Their versions show how the stories were taught, but they do not preserve an untouched first edition from the age of the earliest Sumerian city-states.

The Electronic Text Corpus of Sumerian Literature presents the versions that can be reconstructed from those witnesses. Its translations make the Anunna visible as a divine group, but the evidence does not support one fixed definition valid across all periods.

The first image remains the simplest. Before grain and sheep, the gods had hunger. The Anunna belonged to the beginning of the divine order, and the order itself had to be supplied with food, clothing, labour, and judgment.

The labour began before human beings existed.

In the Old Babylonian poem later known as Atrahasis, the gods carried the work of the world. The opening lines place the divine community inside an organized division of labour. The great gods held authority. The lesser gods performed the physical tasks.

The rivers and canals had to be dug.

The text calls the junior gods the Igigi. They worked under the authority of the senior gods, the Anunnaki. The poem does not describe machines. It describes tools, earth, water, and bodies worn down by repeated labour.

The Igigi dug the channels and maintained the waterways. Their work supported the order of the divine world, but it was not presented as voluntary service. The burden continued year after year. The younger gods carried baskets and handled the instruments of excavation while the senior gods remained above them in rank.

The work became too heavy.

The Igigi gathered together. Their complaint was directed toward Enlil, the god whose authority stood at the centre of the divine hierarchy. They burned their tools. Then they went at night to Enlil's house.

The poem lets the threat emerge through movement. A crowd of workers leaves the fields and canals. Their tools are destroyed. Their bodies gather around the dwelling of the ruler. The senior gods are no longer secure inside the arrangement they created.

Enlil called the great gods together. An, Enlil, Enki, and the other authorities faced a crisis. The workers had rebelled, but the work still had to be done. The rivers could not be left undug. The canals could not simply disappear from the world.

Enki proposed the solution.

Human beings would be created to carry the labour of the gods.

The decision did not begin with a human request. It began with divine exhaustion. The humans were designed to enter an existing structure and take over its work. In Atrahasis, the purpose of humanity is therefore stated with unusual directness. People are made so that the gods can be released from toil.

The senior gods accept the plan, but the means of creation remains to be determined.

Another Sumerian composition, Enki and Ninmah, tells the same problem in a different literary form. The gods complain about their labour. Enki lies in the Abzu, while the great mother goddess is called upon to make a new race. The clay of the earth becomes the material for forming human beings.

Ninmah, also known in other traditions as Nintu or Mami, shapes the human body. Enki and Ninmah then test the limits of creation through a sequence of malformed or impaired humans. Each body raises a question about what a human being can be and what place that person might hold in the world.

The text is not a medical account. It is a literary contest between divine makers. Ninmah creates unusual bodies, and Enki assigns them places or conditions within society. The story explores the boundary between normal capacity, disability, dependence, and divine power.

Its opening problem is the same as in Atrahasis. The gods are burdened by work, and humans are created to take part in the order of labour.

The two compositions do not match line for line. Enki and Ninmah is a Sumerian text preserved principally in Old Babylonian copies. Atrahasis is an Akkadian poem preserved in several fragments, including Old Babylonian tablets and later witnesses. Each belongs to a different language and literary form.

W. G. Lambert and A. R. Millard reconstructed Atrahasis from fragments held in different collections. Their edition shows how the poem's sequence can be recovered even when no complete Old Babylonian tablet survives. The tablet dates belong to the early second millennium before the common era, while some later copies preserve additional portions or altered readings.

The division between Anunnaki and Igigi also has a history.

In the labour scene of Atrahasis, the Anunnaki are the senior gods and the Igigi are the workers. The distinction is clear within that passage. Elsewhere in Mesopotamian literature, however, the names do not always retain one rigid division. Later Babylonian texts can use the groups differently, and the boundaries between divine categories are not identical in every period.

The rebellion nevertheless depends upon hierarchy. A group cannot rebel as a labour force unless another group commands it. The poem makes the divine world resemble an institution with ranks, duties, and a central authority. The gods are immortal, but they are not free from work. Immortality does not remove the need to dig.

The scene at Enlil's house is also a scene of political danger. A crowd surrounds the residence of the senior god. The complaint has become collective action. The poem does not give a long speech explaining the theology of labour. It gives the physical signs of revolt: tools burned, workers assembled, authority confronted.

Enlil's response is fear and alarm. An and Enlil consult with the other gods. The issue is not whether the Igigi have suffered. Their suffering has already made the divine order unstable. The question is how to preserve the order without returning the senior gods to the canals.

Enki's proposal turns the problem toward creation.

In Enki and Ninmah, the divine mother is summoned. The text associates her with the shaping of the human body from clay. Enki directs the process and then examines the results. The work is both practical and intellectual. Bodies are made, tested, named, and assigned a place.

The Sumerian composition does not use the exact same sequence as Atrahasis. It does not present the later sacrifice of a god in the same way. Its emphasis falls on the contest between Enki and Ninmah and on the variety of human conditions.

Atrahasis moves toward a more precise ritual and material explanation. The gods need a substance that can carry divine life into clay. The poem therefore brings Nintu, or Mami, into the process. The worker chosen for death is not yet named in the surviving opening sequence in every witness. The next part of the story identifies the material taken from him.

A human race is thus made for labour, but the labour is not the only role later texts give to humans. People also become the keepers of temples, the providers of offerings, and the inhabitants of cities. The gods' food in Mesopotamian thought was connected to ritual offerings, but the texts do not require the claim that the gods physically depended upon human cooking in the same manner as human beings. The stories use the language of meals and work to describe the relationship between divine power and human service.

The rivers dug by the Igigi became part of the landscape in which cities could grow. Canals supported agriculture. Agriculture supported temples and palaces. Temples organized land, animals, labourers, and grain. The myth's divine work therefore resembled the infrastructure on which Mesopotamian society depended.

That resemblance does not turn the poem into a literal report of an ancient engineering project. It shows how the scribes imagined cosmic order through the realities around them.

The tablet's damaged edges remain part of the story. Some lines in Atrahasis are missing. Parallel passages help restore the sequence, but different editions may place a broken phrase differently or translate a disputed sign in another way. The broad movement is secure: the Igigi labour, rebel, and receive relief through the creation of humans.

By the end of the composition's first movement, the gods have solved one crisis by creating another order of beings. The Anunnaki remain senior. The Igigi are released from their work. Humanity enters the world carrying a duty assigned before any human voice has spoken.

The sacrifice began in the council of the gods.

In Atrahasis, the senior gods gathered after the Igigi had rebelled. The divine assembly had accepted Enki's proposal, but human beings still had to be formed. Clay alone would not be enough. A god would have to be killed so that the new creature could carry something of the divine world within its body.

The text names a god whose reading remains debated. W. G. Lambert and A. R. Millard read the name as We-ila. Other translations and discussions handle the damaged writing differently. The name is less important to the poem's immediate action than the role assigned to the victim.

The god was selected from among the divine beings.

The gods seized him and killed him. His blood and flesh became the material for creation. Nintu, also called Mami, took part in the work. The poem places her at the centre of the forming process, while Enki directs the ritual arrangement.

Clay was mixed with the substance taken from the slain god.

The text adds an image that is easy to pass over but difficult to explain with certainty. The gods spat upon the clay. The gesture may belong to a ritual act represented in poetic form. It may also preserve a traditional idea about the transfer of divine substance. The poem does not explain the action in the language of a later scientific description.

Fourteen pieces were pinched off from the prepared clay.

Seven were placed on one side and seven on the other. The birth-goddesses gathered around them. The number gives the scene a structure. Creation becomes a repeated process, divided into two groups, with the divine midwives assisting the bodies as they move toward birth.

The text does not describe one human being made alone. It describes a population formed through a coordinated act. The human race enters the world in a series of births, and each birth belongs to the plan made by the gods.

Nintu spoke as the mother who shapes life. The poem assigns her the work of handling clay, placing the divine substance within it, and bringing the human bodies toward birth. Mami is another name associated with this maternal and creative role. The names overlap across Mesopotamian traditions, but the exact distribution of titles changes from one text to another.

The human beings were made to carry the work that had burdened the gods.

Their bodies would dig canals, cultivate fields, gather grain, tend animals, and maintain the institutions through which the gods received honour. Atrahasis does not present humanity as an accidental by-product. Human beings are the solution to a crisis in the divine labour system.

The origin of humans is therefore double.

Clay gives them their earthly body. The blood and flesh of a god give them a connection to the divine order. The poem does not say that human beings become gods. They remain mortal and subordinate. Yet they carry within them a substance that links their existence to the sacrifice at the centre of creation.

The text also mentions the god's intelligence, or understanding, in a line whose wording has been debated. Lambert and Millard connected the divine substance with what makes human beings capable of thought or consciousness. Other scholars have treated the line more cautiously, reading the relevant term as a quality of mind, a mark of personhood, or a ritual attribute rather than a biological ingredient.

The uncertainty is limited but meaningful. The poem clearly links the slain god with the human condition. It is less clear whether the line describes intelligence as a physical substance, a divine capacity transferred through blood, or a poetic way of explaining why humans possess understanding.

The ancient scribes did not separate body and mind according to modern categories. Life, blood, breath, flesh, intelligence, and divine power could belong to one chain of images. A translation that chooses one English word can make that chain appear more precise than the Akkadian original.

The birth scene continues.

The birth-goddesses take their places. Clay is shaped, divided, and brought toward delivery. The process resembles a ritual as much as a household birth. The poem moves between the language of making and the language of labour, connecting divine creation with the work humans will later perform.

The gods have solved the revolt by transferring labour downward.

The arrangement does not end conflict among the gods. The creation of humanity brings relief, but it also creates a mortal population exposed to divine decisions. Later portions of Atrahasis will place humans at the centre of another crisis when their noise, growth, or presence becomes unacceptable to the gods. The first creation scene already contains that future danger because the humans are made in large numbers for a particular purpose.

Their purpose is not described as exploration, discovery, or the spread of a civilization from another planet. It is work within the divine economy of Mesopotamia.

The Sumerian Enki and Ninmah gives the same broad setting a different emphasis. There, the gods complain of toil, and Enki asks the mother goddess to shape humans from clay. Ninmah forms bodies that do not fit ordinary expectations, and Enki finds places for them within the human order. The story reflects a world in which bodily difference becomes a matter for divine judgment and social placement.

Atrahasis is more concerned with the origin of human labour and with the sacrifice that supplies the material of life. The two stories should not be collapsed into one text. They preserve related ideas, but they belong to separate compositions, languages, and scribal histories.

The physical tablets preserve that separation.

Atrahasis survives in fragments from the Old Babylonian period and in later copies. The edition by Lambert and Millard joined pieces that had once been separated by modern collections. Some lines are reconstructed from parallel passages. The story is therefore known through an editorial arrangement grounded in the tablets, not through one complete manuscript found intact.

The wording of the divine name We-ila, the line concerning intelligence, and several damaged passages remain subjects of scholarly discussion. The central sequence is more secure: a god is killed, his substance is mixed with clay, Nintu or Mami forms human beings, and the new race receives the labour once carried by the Igigi.

The Anunnaki stand behind the decision as senior gods. They do not descend from the sky in vehicles. They gather in council, assign a sacrifice, and establish a hierarchy in which humans will work for the divine order.

The clay held the shape of that idea. Human beings were earthly bodies made from earth, marked by divine blood, and created for the work of the gods.

In the poem known as Inanna's Descent to the Netherworld, the goddess did not travel as a ruler entering a province of her own. She left the world above, passed beyond its boundary, and went down toward the realm of Ereshkigal, the queen below.

Inanna abandoned her temples one after another. The poem names her cities and shrines, and at each place she prepared herself for the descent. She placed the instruments of authority upon her body: the crown, measuring rod, lapis lazuli necklace, breast ornaments, and garments that marked her as a goddess of power. Then she went toward the gate of the netherworld.

Her minister Ninshubur followed the preparations with alarm. Inanna had given instructions before leaving. If she did not return, Ninshubur was to mourn in the proper places and appeal to the great gods, beginning with Enlil, then with Nanna, and finally with Enki. The poem gave the servant a precise duty because the underworld was not a place from which a visitor could simply walk back.

At the first gate, the gatekeeper stopped Inanna.

The gatekeeper carried her message to Ereshkigal. Inanna claimed that she had come to attend the funeral rites of the husband of Ereshkigal, though the poem does not make the visit a simple act of family loyalty. Ereshkigal allowed the gatekeeper to admit her, but ordered that the visitor pass through the seven gates according to the law of the netherworld.

The first gate opened.

At each gate, something was taken from Inanna. At the first, the great crown was removed. When she asked why, the gatekeeper told her that the rules of the netherworld had to be observed. At the second gate, the measuring rod and line were taken. At the third, the small lapis lazuli ornaments. At the fourth, the breast ornaments. At the fifth, the gold ring. At the sixth, the breastplate. At the seventh, the final garment.

Inanna protested at every loss. The gatekeeper answered each time that the order came from Ereshkigal. The poem makes the stripping gradual. Authority disappears piece by piece, until the goddess who had entered as a sovereign stands without the symbols that made her recognizable above.

Then Inanna entered before Ereshkigal.

The queen of the netherworld summoned the judges. In the Sumerian poem they are the Anunna, the group of great gods who sat in judgment. Ereshkigal fixed upon Inanna the gaze of death. The word of judgment was spoken. Inanna became a corpse, and the poem says that the body was hung upon a hook.

The scene is brief and severe. There is no battle in which Inanna defeats the underworld. There is no escape by force. The goddess of the great city temples is treated as every other visitor must be treated. The law of the netherworld reaches her even when her authority above is immense.

Three days passed.

Ninshubur began the appeals. First, the servant went to Enlil at Nippur. Ninshubur mourned and asked for help, but Enlil refused. The underworld, he said in the poem, was a place from which no one returned.

Ninshubur then went to Nanna, the moon god, and made the same appeal. Nanna also refused. The boundary between the living and the dead could not be crossed simply because a great goddess had been caught within it.

Finally, Ninshubur went to Enki. His realm was Eridu, and his wisdom was associated with the deep waters beneath the earth. Enki did not answer by sending an army. Instead, he formed two small creatures from the dirt beneath his fingernails. The poem calls them the kurgarra and the galatur. They were not presented as warriors. They were creatures suited to entering the presence of grief.

Enki gave them the food and water of life. He told them how to approach Ereshkigal. They were to echo her suffering, not challenge her authority. When they found the queen in pain, they were to say that her condition deserved lamentation. Their response would be rewarded.

The creatures went down.

Ereshkigal lay in distress. The poem describes her body with the repeated language of suffering, and the creatures answered each expression of pain with sympathy. They did not ask for riches. They did not demand that the dead be released. Their success came from recognizing the queen's misery.

Ereshkigal offered them a gift.

The kurgarra and galatur asked for the corpse hanging on the hook. Ereshkigal gave it to them. They sprinkled the food and water of life over Inanna, and the goddess rose.

Her return was not yet free. The judges of the netherworld followed her. Inanna came out with the creatures and with a company of demons, but the poem said that no one could leave the underworld without providing a substitute. The balance of death had to be restored.

Inanna moved from one city to another. At each place, a demon looked for the person who could take her place below. The figures around her were not treated in the same way. Ninshubur had mourned and appealed for her. The goddess did not surrender her servant.

Inanna found her sons and saw that they were in mourning. She did not give them up.

Then she came to Dumuzi. He was not dressed as a man in mourning. He sat in splendor, clothed in fine garments, and the sight angered Inanna. She designated him as the substitute. Dumuzi appealed to Utu, the sun god, asking that his hands and feet be changed so that he could escape the demons. The poem then developed the terms of the exchange. Dumuzi would spend part of the year in the netherworld, while his sister Geshtinanna would spend another part there in his place.

The descent ended not with the abolition of death but with an arrangement. Inanna returned to the world above. Dumuzi entered the cycle of descent and return. The underworld remained a realm with its own law.

The Anunna had appeared here as judges, not as workers building the universe and not as a general name for every deity. Their authority belonged to the court of Ereshkigal. That role is one reason a single definition of the Anunnaki cannot be carried unchanged from one text to another.

The Sumerian poem belongs to a tradition copied in the early second millennium before the common era, although its story and language drew on older compositions. The version used by modern readers is reconstructed from cuneiform tablets, and damaged lines leave some details uncertain. The Electronic Text Corpus of Sumerian Literature presents the poem in a critical modern edition based on those witnesses.

The movement of the Anunna between realms also belongs to a long history of changing divine geography. In Sumerian compositions, Anunna could mean the great gods associated with the assembly of heaven and earth. In underworld texts, the name could belong to the judges below. Later Babylonian texts use the related form Anunnaki in different arrangements, including a division between gods above and gods below.

Thorkild Jacobsen treated such changes as part of Mesopotamian religion's development from local powers toward more organized divine communities. Jeremy Black and Anthony Green described the Anunna and Anunnaki as a shifting group rather than a fixed pantheon with an unchanging membership. The poem of Inanna supports that caution. Its judges are real within the story, but their presence does not establish one permanent office for the name across every period.

The corpse on the hook remained the turning point. A goddess had crossed the gates carrying the signs of authority, and those signs had been taken from her. The Anunna judged her according to the order of the world below.

Before Babylon became the center of the great creation poem, the gods had already been arranged in generations.

The poem later called Enuma Elish began before heaven and earth had received their names. Apsu, the fresh water, and Tiamat, the salt water, were mingled together. From their union came younger divine beings. The text then named Lahmu and Lahamu, followed by Anshar and Kishar, and after them Anu. Anu fathered Ea, who was also known in Sumerian tradition as Enki.

The younger gods disturbed Apsu. Their movement and noise troubled him, and he decided that they should be destroyed. Tiamat objected. She did not accept the plan, but Apsu continued with the intention of killing the younger gods.

Ea learned of the danger. Through his wisdom and ritual power, he cast a spell over Apsu and killed him. Ea then took possession of the place that had belonged to Apsu and established his own dwelling there.

Tiamat remained alive.

The younger gods continued to disturb her. Tiamat's anger grew, and she created monsters to fight on her behalf. The poem named a serpent, a dragon, a great lion, a mad dog, a scorpion-man, storm demons, and other creatures. She gave Kingu command of the host. She placed the tablets of destiny upon his breast, making him the bearer of authority.

The younger gods met in fear. No one wanted to confront Tiamat. Anu failed to face her. Ea did not accept the battle. At last the gods turned to Marduk, the young god of Babylon.

Marduk agreed to fight, but he demanded a reward. If he defeated Tiamat, the gods would grant him supreme authority. They would assemble and confirm his word. The assembly accepted the bargain.

Marduk prepared his weapons. He took a bow and arrows, a club, a net, and the four winds. He made the winds hold Tiamat in place. He mounted his storm chariot, whose horses were described as fierce and swift, and moved toward the battle.

Tiamat opened her mouth. Marduk sent the evil wind into it, preventing her from closing her jaws. The winds filled her body. Marduk then shot an arrow into her belly. Tiamat fell.

Marduk cut her body in two. One half became the sky. He set a barrier in place so that the waters would not escape. He established stations for the great gods and fixed the stars. The text presented cosmic order as an act of military victory followed by measurement and placement.

Marduk then turned to the defeated army. He captured Kingu and removed the tablets of destiny. Kingu was bound and delivered to punishment. The poem had reached the question of labor.

The gods complained that they had no work. Marduk and Ea decided that a new being should be made to carry the burden. Ea asked which god had led Tiamat's rebellion. The answer was Kingu.

Kingu was killed. From his blood, humanity was formed. The poem said that humans would bear the service of the gods, so that the gods could rest. Humanity was not created in this account as an independent political community. The purpose given by the text was labor for the divine order.

Marduk then organized the gods.

Three hundred Anunnaki were assigned to the heavens, and three hundred were assigned to the earth. The poem did not present this division as a scientific map. It was a distribution of divine labor and jurisdiction. Some gods belonged above, others below, while Marduk ruled over the ordered whole.

The Anunnaki were then given tasks. Marduk instructed them to build his city. The work began with Babylon. The gods carried baskets, raised walls, and made the city. They built Esagila, Marduk's great temple, and established its high platform. The city was both a divine residence and a monument to the victory that had reordered the cosmos.

The gods celebrated. They prepared a banquet, and the assembly confirmed Marduk's fifty names. Each name expressed an aspect of his authority: creator, judge, organizer, and lord of the ordered world. The names gathered older divine functions around the patron of Babylon.

The poem ended with praise of Marduk and Babylon. The city was not merely one settlement among others. In the poem's political theology, Babylon was the place from which the gods' order was administered.

The version of Enuma Elish usually read today comes from Babylonian tablets copied in the first millennium before the common era, with important witnesses from the library of Ashurbanipal at Nineveh and from other sites. Its composition is generally placed in the late second millennium before the common era, although the poem incorporated older traditions. The text is therefore not a single window onto one moment. It is a Babylonian work that reshaped earlier divine genealogies around Marduk.

Stephanie Dalley translated the poem in Myths from Mesopotamia and emphasized its structure as a creation and kingship narrative. The political setting matters. Babylon's rise gave Marduk a reason to receive the highest place in the divine assembly, and the poem gave that rise a cosmic explanation.

The text did not say that Marduk had always been the only supreme god. It showed him winning authority through a bargain, a battle, and a formal confirmation by the gods. The Anunnaki were not his creators. They were members of the divine order whom he assigned to different realms and directed toward the building of Babylon.

The poem also differed from the story of Atrahasis. In Atrahasis, the lesser gods rebelled because of exhausting labor, and humans were made to carry that work. In Enuma Elish, the gods' labor was reorganized after Tiamat's defeat, and humans were made from Kingu's blood to serve the gods. The two traditions shared a concern with divine work and human obligation, but they arranged the causes differently.

The foundation of Babylon therefore followed two foundations at once. Marduk had divided Tiamat's body to make the world, and the Anunnaki had built the city that represented his rule. The city stood as a political claim written into a creation poem.

In the twentieth century, the Anunnaki left the cuneiform tablets and entered a new kind of story.

The central figure was Zecharia Sitchin, a writer born in the Russian Empire and later established in the United States. In nineteen seventy six, he published The Twelfth Planet. The book presented a history of humanity in which the Anunnaki were not gods in the ancient religious sense. They were visitors from another world.

Sitchin identified their home as Nibiru. In his account, Nibiru was a large planet with a long orbit that brought it into the region of the solar system once every several thousand years. Its inhabitants, the Anunnaki, needed resources. Sitchin gave special importance to gold. According to his reconstruction, the visitors came to Earth to mine it, first by using their own labor and later by creating a worker species.

Humanity, in this account, was made through genetic intervention. Sitchin connected the Anunnaki with the creation of modern humans and described the process in terms of combining the visitors' biological material with that of an existing hominin population. The ancient stories about divine creation, labor, kingship, and descent were treated as memories of technological events.

Sitchin did not present this as a small adjustment to the old myths. He proposed a different category of explanation. The figures called gods in Mesopotamian texts were, in his reading, advanced beings whose machines, biology, and knowledge had been mistaken for divinity by later humans.

The argument attracted readers because it connected many separate elements. The creation of humans from divine material became genetic engineering. The descent from heaven became space travel. The work assigned to humans became mining. The long journeys of gods became orbital movements.

The books multiplied. Sitchin published further volumes in the Earth Chronicles series, and other writers adapted his claims. Some linked the Anunnaki with stories from Egypt, the Bible, or other ancient cultures. Later websites, videos, and online communities repeated the account in shorter and more dramatic forms. The terms Anunnaki and Nibiru became part of a modern vocabulary about ancient visitors.

The claim rests on translations of cuneiform signs. That is where the argument meets the evidence.

Specialists in Assyriology do not read the Sumerian and Babylonian texts in Sitchin's way. The languages have been studied through thousands of tablets, bilingual lexical lists, school exercises, royal inscriptions, administrative records, hymns, legal documents, and myths. A sign can have several values, and a word can change meaning with grammar and context. Reading a text requires more than assigning a modern idea to an isolated mark.

The name Anunnaki appears in ancient texts as a divine group. The members, functions, and location of the group vary. The word does not mean astronauts. The texts describe the Anunnaki as gods within systems of worship, sacrifice, kingship, judgment, and cosmic order.

The ancient evidence for Nibiru is different from Sitchin's planet. In Babylonian astronomical and mythological texts, the term could identify a crossing point or a marker associated with an astronomical body. In passages connected with Marduk, Nibiru was linked to the star or position that marked his authority. The word was not the fixed name of a distant home planet inhabited by the Anunnaki.

The meaning of Nibiru depended on context. Babylonian astronomers used a technical vocabulary for celestial movements, and the same term could be applied in different ways. Modern specialists therefore do not treat Nibiru as evidence for a planet on the orbit proposed in The Twelfth Planet.

Sitchin also used images as evidence. Mesopotamian cylinder seals sometimes show stars, crescents, disks, or arrangements of points. He interpreted some of these as diagrams of the solar system. Assyriologists read such objects according to their artistic and cultural settings. A group of celestial symbols does not automatically represent a modern planetary chart. Ancient artists used signs for gods, stars, and divine presence, and their meanings must be established from comparisons with known texts and images.

The question of gold follows the same pattern. Mesopotamian texts valued gold and used it in temples, ornaments, royal display, and divine imagery. They do not describe an industrial program in which the Anunnaki mined gold from Earth to repair the atmosphere of their planet. That detail belongs to Sitchin's modern reconstruction, not to the ancient poems.

The genetic claim also reverses the direction of the evidence. Ancient texts use the language of fashioning, forming, mixing, birth, blood, clay, and divine decision. In Atrahasis, humans are made from clay mixed with the blood of a slaughtered god. In Enuma Elish, humanity is made from Kingu's blood. These are accounts of ritual and social purpose. They explain why humans serve the gods within the story. They do not describe laboratory procedures.

W. G. Lambert and A. R. Millard's edition of Atra-hasis presents the Babylonian flood story through the evidence of its tablets and language. Nothing in their analysis requires, or supports, a technological visitor interpretation. The words are embedded in Mesopotamian ideas about divine labor, noise, mortality, and obligation.

The difference between the two readings is therefore not a dispute over one obscure detail. It is a dispute over the kind of document being read. Sitchin treated mythic language as a coded report of physical events. Specialists treat the texts as products of ancient scribal traditions, even when those traditions preserve older memories and political claims.

This distinction does not depend on contempt for modern readers. Sitchin's work became influential because it offered a single narrative that connected gods, stars, human origins, and lost knowledge. Its spread shows how ancient names can be removed from their original literary settings and placed inside a new account of history.

It also shows the importance of grammar. The ancient texts do not call the Anunnaki a crew. They do not describe spacecraft arriving from Nibiru. They do not state that gold mining caused the creation of humanity. Those elements entered the modern story through Sitchin's interpretations.

The word Anunnaki survived the transformation, but its role changed. In the tablets, it belonged to divine assemblies, judgments, underworld courts, and the organization of cosmic labor. In The Twelfth Planet, it became the name of an extraterrestrial population.

The same name could carry two very different histories. Only one of them is present in the cuneiform texts.

A tablet did not begin as a modern book. It began in a society where temples owned fields, herds, workshops, and storehouses, and where writing recorded the movement of grain, wool, animals, metal, and labor.

The scribes who copied myths worked within that world. Their texts placed the gods above human society, but the gods were not distant abstractions. They had houses, estates, servants, clothing, food, and offices. A temple was the household of a deity, and the surrounding land could be understood as belonging to that divine owner.

In Sumerian stories, people were made to perform labor for the gods. The language differed from one composition to another, but the social idea remained recognizable. Humans dug canals, cultivated fields, carried loads, prepared offerings, and maintained the order on which cities depended. The mythic explanation gave this labor a divine origin.

The Anunnaki belonged to that hierarchy. They could be great gods in an assembly, judges who fixed destinies, or powers assigned to heaven and earth. Their work was not described as a single profession. Their place changed with the text's setting.

When the gods decided the fate of a city, the Anunnaki could appear as the assembly whose decision shaped kingship and disaster. When Inanna entered the netherworld, the Anunna judged her. When Marduk reorganized the cosmos, the Anunnaki received assignments above and below and built Babylon.

Thorkild Jacobsen argued that Mesopotamian religion changed as social and political structures changed. In The Treasures of Darkness, he described early divine powers as closely connected with natural forces and later gods as more personal, political, and humanlike. On this view, the gods' councils and judgments reflected the institutions of cities and kingdoms. The Anunna were not a fixed list because the divine world itself was organized through changing relationships.

Jeremy Black and Anthony Green took a more cautious approach to divine groupings. Their work shows that Mesopotamian gods could acquire different names, attributes, and associations in different periods. The Anunnaki were a category used by scribes, but not a pantheon whose membership could be listed once for all time. The variation was part of the tradition.

The temples give the texts their physical setting. Offerings of food, drink, animals, oil, and precious materials appear in administrative records. Such records do not reveal the private feelings of each person who brought an offering. They do show that temples required organized supplies and that divine service was woven into the economy of the city.

The flood stories place the same structure under pressure.

In Atrahasis, the lesser gods perform labor. They dig the canals and bear the burden of work. They rebel because the load has become intolerable. The gods then create humans to take over the labor. Later, the noise of humanity disturbs the gods, and the divine assembly decides to send a flood.

The text gives the flood as a decision of the gods. It does not present one unanimous moral explanation. Enki warns Atrahasis while the other gods pursue the plan. After the flood, the gods gather around the sacrifice because hunger has returned to them. The story treats divine power as dependent on human service even while humans remain vulnerable to divine judgment.

Enuma Elish arranges the same relationship differently. There, humans are made after Marduk's victory so that the gods can rest. The human role is not the result of a labor revolt in the same sequence. It belongs to the foundation of Marduk's ordered world.

The differences matter. A scribal tradition could preserve several explanations for human existence without resolving them into one creed. The gods' work, human labor, and divine judgment were retold for different cities and patrons.

The records can establish that scribes copied these narratives, adapted them, and placed them in temple and royal settings. They cannot establish whether every listener accepted each story literally, symbolically, ritually, or with some mixture of responses. Ordinary people left few statements about private belief.

The texts do show a world in which gods owned the land, temples represented divine households, and human work supported cosmic order. The Anunnaki belonged to that world because they expressed the authority of the divine assembly.

Their flexibility was not a scribal error. It was one of the ways Mesopotamian religion worked.

The ancient texts establish several things about the Anunnaki.

They present the Anunna or Anunnaki as a group of great gods. The name belongs to Sumerian and Babylonian traditions, and its form and membership vary across time. In some compositions, the group stands near the divine assembly associated with An, the sky god. In others, the Anunnaki receive authority under Enlil, Enki, Ea, or Marduk.

The texts also give them work.

They can decide fates. They can judge the dead and the living. In Inanna's Descent to the Netherworld, the seven Anunna sit with Ereshkigal and fix the gaze of death upon Inanna. In Enuma Elish, Marduk divides the Anunnaki between the heavens and the earth, then assigns them to the ordered universe and directs them toward the building of Babylon and Esagila.

The texts connect them with human origins, but not in one single account. In Atrahasis, humans are made to carry the labor of the gods. In Enuma Elish, humans are formed from the blood of Kingu after Tiamat's defeat so that the gods can rest. These stories explain human service inside their own mythic worlds.

The texts do not establish one fixed meaning for the name. The Anunnaki are not always located in the same realm, do not always perform the same function, and do not belong to a single unchanging list.

The record cannot establish the private belief of every person who heard or copied these stories. Administrative tablets show temple economies, not the inner thoughts of farmers, laborers, merchants, or worshippers. A hymn shows what a text praises. It does not tell how every listener responded.

The record cannot establish one exact date for the origin of every Anunnaki tradition. Sumerian compositions were copied and reshaped across centuries. Babylonian poems incorporated older material and placed it inside new political and theological arrangements.

The record also cannot establish any physical reality for the gods. Archaeology can recover temples, tablets, seals, statues, offerings, and city plans. Those objects show how people represented and served the gods. They do not turn the gods into biological visitors from another planet.

That modern transformation belongs to Zecharia Sitchin and The Twelfth Planet, published in nineteen seventy six. Sitchin's Anunnaki came from a planet called Nibiru, mined gold, and altered human biology. Specialists reject those readings because the ancient languages, signs, astronomical terms, and myths do not say what Sitchin claimed. In Babylonian texts, Nibiru was an astronomical term associated in some passages with Marduk's celestial authority, not the established name of an inhabited planet on the proposed orbit.

The ancient stories are more varied than the modern invention. They move from heaven to earth, from the city temple to the underworld, from the assembly of gods to the blood of a defeated enemy. Their Anunnaki are judges, members of divine councils, and powers assigned to maintain a world ordered around temples, kings, labor, and fate.

The imagined schoolboy at Nippur belonged to the opening scene of this account. He was not a discovered individual. No tablet gives his name, his thoughts, or the words he might have spoken. He was an imagined figure, placed beside the real remains of a city where scribes wrote and copied the stories.

What can be known is the world around him. Nippur was a major religious center. Clay tablets preserved the work of scribes. The names of An, Enlil, Enki, Ninmah, Inanna, Ereshkigal, Marduk, Tiamat, Kingu, Atrahasis, and the Anunnaki moved through different texts and different centuries.

The boy's private belief is lost.

The tablets remain, and they speak of gods made for stories, cities, and order.
