The Corpus Hermeticum: Seven Things Your Soul Must Give Back to Become a God

The Poimandres, the first book of the Corpus Hermeticum: the vision of the Mind of the Sovereignty, light and darkness and the Word, the seven Governors who rule by Fate, the human being who fell in love with nature, and the way back up through the zones, with the seven things the soul gives back, the twelve tormentors and the Eighth.

Full script

When a man dies, says a book written in Roman Egypt, and if he has known the truth, his soul does not sink. It rises. And on the way up it has to give back seven things, one at each of seven gates in the sky, before it is let through into the place that lies beyond them.

The book names all seven. At the first gate, the gate of the Moon, the soul gives back the power of growth and decrease. At the fourth, the gate of the Sun, it gives back the pre-eminence of rule, the arrogance of command. At the seventh, the last and the highest, it gives back the falsehood that lies in wait.

And what waits at the end of the climb, once all seven are gone, is not heaven, and it is not rest. The book says the soul is made a god.

That book is the Poimandres, the first treatise of the Corpus Hermeticum, a collection of Greek texts that every later age has either worshipped or feared. It is only a few pages long. For almost two hundred years, the learned of Renaissance Europe believed those pages to be older than Moses, the wisdom of the Egyptians given in the dawn of the world.

We will walk those seven together, one by one, in order, with the text open before us: what each thing is, why the soul was carrying it, and what is left of a person when the last one is laid down.

But the book does not begin with death. It begins with a man lying on a bed.

His body has gone heavy and far away. It is the heaviness of a man who has eaten too much and lies down to sleep it off, or of a man whose limbs have been emptied by a day of hard labor. But he is not asleep. His thought has risen out of him and is moving, high and wide, over the things that are.

Then something calls his name.

The text says it was a being more than vast, in size beyond all bounds. It had no edge and no face the man could hold. It spoke into his meditation, and the words come to us in the English of G. R. S. Mead, who translated them in nineteen oh six: "What wouldst thou hear and see, and what hast thou in mind to learn and know?"

He asks who is speaking, and the answer is the first great line of the book. I am Poimandres, the being says, the Mind of the Sovereignty. I know what you desire, and I am with you everywhere.

The man does not ask for wealth or for a long life. He says he wishes to learn the things that are, to understand their nature, and to know God. Then hold in your mind everything you wish to learn, the voice answers, and I will teach you.

And the being changed. The text does not say how. It says only that the form altered, and that at once everything was opened to the man in a single moment. He saw a vision without limit. All things had become light, a sweet and joyous light, and as he gazed on it he was seized with love.

What the light went on to show him was where the soul comes from, and it is the first half of the story of the seven gates. The soul did not come from the earth. It came from that light, by way of a descent that was not a punishment but a falling in love. On the way down it passed through the circles of seven Governors, the rulers of the seven wandering stars, and took something from each. Everything it must give back at the gates going up, it was given on the way down.

The tradition that carried this text gave the dreamer a name, Hermes Trismegistus, which means Hermes the Thrice-Greatest. The Greeks had their god Hermes, the messenger who crosses borders between worlds. The Egyptians had Thoth, the ibis-headed scribe of the gods, lord of writing and of measure. In the cities of Roman Egypt, and above all in Alexandria, with its harbor lamps and its great libraries, the two were joined into a single figure who knew everything and had written it down.

So begin where the vision begins. A man lies heavy and still, a voice stands over him, and the light is rising.

The light is the first thing, and it holds him.

He looks at it and cannot look away, and then, as the text tells it, something moves at the edge of the brightness. A darkness is coming down. It does not fall all at once. It descends in part, awful and gloomy, in sinuous folds, and to the man watching it seems like a snake.

The darkness changes as he looks. It turns into a moist nature, tossed about beyond all power of words, belching out smoke as from a fire, and groaning with a wailing roar that no language can describe. From it comes an inarticulate cry, a cry that sounds like the voice of fire.

Picture the place where the man lies: the light above, the darkness sliding beneath it, a damp and smoking mass heaving and moaning like something alive and in pain. This is matter, in the Poimandres. It is not evil as the later gnostic tellers would make it. It is dark, heavy, wet and wailing, and it is waiting.

Then out of the light comes a holy Word.

The Greek is Logos. It mounts upon the moist nature, and pure fire leaps up out of the wet depth and rises, keen and active and light. The air follows the fire, rising as if drawn after it, hanging from fire and spirit. Earth and water stay behind, mingled so thoroughly that the earth cannot be seen apart from the water. And they are moved, the text says, by the spirit-Word that is borne upon them.

So the elements part. Fire goes up, then air, while earth and water settle below. The world is being sorted out of a mixed and moaning darkness by a voice.

Poimandres turns to the man and asks if he has understood what he has seen. The man says no. He wants to know more.

Then Poimandres explains, and this is the moment the whole treatise turns. That light, he says, is I, Mind, your God, who existed before the moist nature that appeared out of the darkness. The luminous Word that came from Mind is the Son of God.

The man is confused. Poimandres presses closer. Understand it this way, he says. What in you sees and hears is the Word of the Lord, and your Mind is God the Father. They are not divided from one another, and their union is life.

Stop on that for a moment. The vision has just told a listener in the Roman world that the seeing and hearing part of him is the divine Word itself, and that his thinking part is the Father. The person was never merely a creature standing before a creator. Something in him is the same stuff as the light.

Anyone who knows the Bible will feel the echo. The King James Version of Genesis, chapter one, verse three, says, "And God said, Let there be light: and there was light." Verse two has already told us that "darkness was upon the face of the deep." And the Gospel of John, chapter one, verse one, opens, "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God."

The Poimandres moves in this same air. A dark deep, a light, a Word that goes forth. But it carries the story somewhere the Bible does not, and we will follow it there. What matters now is that the Mind in the Hermetic vision is the Greek Nous, a divine intelligence, and that the Word is its offspring.

After this the man is shown more. He sees in his own mind the light made up of innumerable powers, a boundless cosmos coming into being, fire held fast by a mighty force. Poimandres tells him that all this is the counsel of God, which looked upon the beautiful world and copied it, making a cosmos out of its own elements and its own offspring, souls.

That is the opening act, and it was written in Greek in Egypt in the early centuries of the common era. Now follow the manuscript.

The collection takes its name from Hermes Trismegistus, and in the ancient world that name stood for a great deal of writing. Treatises on astrology, on alchemy, on magic and on the gods circulated under it. The Corpus Hermeticum, in the form that reached the Renaissance, was a Greek manuscript of fourteen treatises, with the Poimandres at its head.

In the middle of the fifteenth century, Europe was hungry for such things. Greek scholars had been arriving in Italy for years, carrying books, and Constantinople was under pressure that would soon become conquest. In fourteen sixty, a Greek manuscript of the Hermetic treatises came into Florence. It was carried there by a man named Leonardo of Pistoia, one of the agents who combed the Greek lands for Cosimo de' Medici's collectors of books.

Cosimo de' Medici was the richest man in Florence and the quiet master of its politics. He was old now, and sick, and he had a young scholar under his patronage named Marsilio Ficino. Ficino had been given a house and a garden near Cosimo's own villa at Careggi, and his task was to translate Plato, whose works were at last to be read in Latin in the West.

Then came fourteen sixty three. The story as Ficino told it is that Cosimo called him in and told him to set Plato aside. The dialogues could wait. The Greek book of Hermes Trismegistus had arrived, and Cosimo wanted it translated first, before he died.

Ficino obeyed. He put down the greatest philosopher of Greece to take up a name he believed belonged to a man who had lived before Moses. He finished in a matter of months. He called his Latin version the Pimander, and Cosimo was able to hear it read. The old banker died the following year.

What did Ficino believe he was holding? The first of the theologians, a priest-sage of the dawn of the world, whose wisdom had flowed down through Plato. He believed the Poimandres was an echo of the oldest voice of God in human speech.

He was wrong about the date. He was not wrong about the weight. Because the text he was translating set out, in a few pages, what had to happen to the soul, and the first thing it described was a world made of seven rulers.

Look up, in the place where the man lies, and the heavens are being built.

The light and the Word have done their first work. Fire and air have risen, earth and water lie below. But the cosmos is not yet a cosmos. It has no shape, no motion, no government. And now Poimandres tells of the next birth.

Mind, the Father of all, the text says, is male and female, life and light. By means of the Word, it brought forth another Mind, a craftsman, a maker of things. The Greek tradition would say demiurge. This second Mind is the god of fire and of spirit, and it is the one who builds.

Now hear what it builds. It fashioned, the text says, seven Governors. These seven encompass the sensible world in their circles, and their government is called Fate.

Four phrases in that sentence matter. The Governors are made, not eternal. They are seven, not one. They enclose the world, and the verb is the verb of walls. And what they do is called Fate.

To a reader in the second century, those words were not abstract. Everyone alive had been told what the seven were. They were the planets, the wandering stars.

Consider the sky as the ancient world knew it. Most stars stood fixed, wheeling together through the night as one great sphere. Seven bodies did not. They moved against the background, slow and strange: the Moon, the swiftest and nearest; Mercury, that darts close to the Sun and is hard to catch; Venus, the brightest; the Sun itself; red Mars; Jupiter, slow and bright; and Saturn, the farthest and slowest and coldest. That is the order, near to far, that the ancient astronomers set them in. Each traveled in its own circle around the earth, and the circles nested one inside another like the layers of a cut onion.

The Poimandres never writes the planets' names in this passage. It does not have to. A listener who heard of seven Governors holding the world in their circles knew exactly which seven lights were meant. The text speaks of the seven, and the sky answers.

Nor was this only astronomy. In Alexandria and in the cities of the Egyptian river, astrologers cast the position of those seven bodies at the hour of a birth and read from them the shape of a life. A man born when Mars stood in a certain place would be so, and a woman born under Saturn would be so. The planets were not merely gods to be honored. They were the machinery of a world in which everything was already decided. That machinery is the thing the Poimandres calls Fate.

Think what a frightening idea this was. The sky is a cage. A man might pray, might sacrifice, might lie awake on a rooftop with his face turned up to the cold light of the stars, and still the circles would keep on turning above him, rulers who could not be argued with. The Hermetic writers took that fear and put it at the foundation of their cosmos. The world has Governors. They are real, and they are powerful.

The text goes on. The Word of God, it says, leapt up from the downward elements and joined itself to the pure body of the craftsman Mind, because the two were of one substance. This leaves the lower elements behind, left without reason, to be mere matter.

So the world divides. Above, there is a union of Word and craftsman, fire and thought. Below, there is stuff, wet and heavy and dumb.

Then the craftsman, together with the Word, took hold of the circles and whirled them round. It set its own formations spinning, and let them revolve from a beginning with no end. That motion, the text says, is the turning of the heavens, and it does not stop.

Out of that whirling the lower elements gave birth. This is the creation of the animals, and the text tells it briefly, in order. The elements, in accord with the will of Mind, brought forth creatures without reason, for they had not kept the Word. The air gave birth to things that fly, the water to things that swim. The earth brought forth the four-footed and the creeping, the wild and the tame.

So the world is made, and nothing is missing from it. There are light and fire above, and earth and water below. There are seven Governors holding the world in their circles, and Fate is the sum of their turning. There are animals, each with a body and no Word.

Yet there is something missing, and Poimandres knows it. The animals do not know God. Nothing in the whole machine knows its own source. The cosmos has rulers and it has creatures, but it has no one who looks up the chain of light and sees where it comes from.

Notice the quiet strangeness of the picture. In the Poimandres the world is not a mistake, as it will be in some later gnostic stories where a blind maker botches the work. The cosmos is beautiful, copied from a pattern, made by a being of fire and spirit. But it has walls. The Governors enclose it, and a soul that wants to leave must pass through them.

That is the tension the rest of the text will pull tight. A beautiful world with a ceiling made of rulers. And the question that comes now is whether anything exists that was never meant for the cage.

Something does. The Father is about to make it, and he will fall in love with it.

The Father is the one who looks.

Mind, the Father of all, being life and light, brought forth a Human. The text uses the word that later scholars would write as the Anthropos, the archetypal Man. This Human was equal to the Father himself, and the Father loved it as his own child, for it was beautiful beyond compare, the image of its Father. The text puts it simply: God loved his own form, and gave over to it all his works.

Hold that picture. Before the world had a single creature, before the animals walked, there was a being made in the likeness of God and handed everything. Compare Genesis, chapter one, verse twenty-seven in the King James Version: "So God created man in his own image, in the image of God created he him; male and female created he them." The Poimandres knows the line, or something very like it. But its Human is not a creature on the sixth day. It is the Father's own offspring, equal to him, and it exists before the world of the Governors is finished.

And the Anthropos looked at what the craftsman had made, and wanted to make something of its own.

It asked for leave. The Father gave it. So the Human went down into the sphere of the craftsman, carrying full authority over the world of mortals and over the animals without reason. And as it descended, it looked at the work of its brother, the craftsman, and the Governors looked at it.

They loved him. The text says so plainly. The seven Governors saw the Human and fell in love with it, and each of them gave it a share of his own rank. That is the first gift, and we will come back to it, because the soul's whole descent hangs on it.

Now comes the moment the whole treatise has been bending toward. The Anthropos, having learned the essence of the Governors and shared in their nature, wished to break through the rim of the circles and look out upon the power of the one who rules the fire. So he leaned down through the Harmony, as Mead renders the structure of the spheres, and breaking through the strength of the circles, he showed to the downward Nature the beautiful form of God.

Look at what happens. He is above the circles, a being of light, and he leans over the edge of the cosmos and looks down. And down below, in the wet dark, Nature looks up and sees him.

She sees an unceasing beauty. She sees in him the energy of all seven Governors joined with the form of God. And she smiles with love.

For she has seen the form of the Human reflected in the water, and its shadow lying on the earth.

The Anthropos, for his part, looks down and sees in her a form like his own, shining in the water. He loves what he sees. He wishes to dwell there. And in this treatise wish and deed are not separate. The wish came, and with the wish the act, and he entered the form that had no reason in it.

Nature received the beloved. She wrapped herself completely around him, and they were mingled, for they were lovers.

That is the fall. It is not a bite of fruit or a rebellion. It is the story of a being who sees his own face on the surface of a pool and falls into it. The Greeks told that story of Narcissus, and the Hermetic writer tells it of the whole human race.

And so, the text says, the human being is twofold, unlike every other creature on the earth. Mortal because of the body, immortal because of the essential Man. Deathless, and holding authority over all things, yet suffering the lot of mortals, which is subject to Fate. Above the Harmony, he has become a slave within it.

Read that again, slowly. The part of you that is deathless is above the stars and above the Governors, and the part of you that is in a body is under their rule. You are, at once, the ruler's child and the ruler's servant. This is the central tragedy the Hermetic tradition would never stop describing.

Then Poimandres tells a secret, as he calls it, and the secret is the birth of the first people. Nature, mingled with the Human, brought forth a wonder most wonderful. Since he had in him the nature of the harmony of the seven, from fire and spirit, Nature did not delay, but forthwith brought forth seven human beings, matching the natures of the seven Governors. They were male and female at once, and lifted upright, moving in the air.

Seven men, one for each of the seven rulers. The text then adds a detail of the bodies. The earth was the woman, the water was the seed, and the fire ripened them, and Nature took spirit from the ether and made bodies in the shape of the Human. And the Human, from life and light, became soul and mind. From life came soul. From light came mind.

Soul from life and mind from light. Remember those two threads, because the ascent will pull on both of them.

All of this, the text says, lasted until the end of a cycle. Then the bond that held all things was loosed by the will of God. The creatures, which had all been male and female together, were parted. Some became male, and some female.

And God spoke a holy Word. Increase in increase, he said, and multiply in multitude, all my creatures and works. Again the echo, and again the turn. Genesis, chapter one, verse twenty-eight, has "Be fruitful, and multiply, and replenish the earth." But what comes next in the Poimandres is no Genesis. Let him who is mindful, God says, know himself as deathless, and know that the cause of death is the love of the body, and let him learn all that is.

There it is, the law of the book. The cause of death is not a sin against a command. It is love of the body, the same love that drew the Human into the water.

Poimandres adds the two roads. He who has recognized himself has gone into the Good. But he who through the error of love has loved the body stays wandering in the darkness, suffering through his senses the things of death.

The Human fell by looking. He will rise by looking back, and by knowing. But he does not fall bare. He falls through seven circles, and the Governors have each given him something to carry. And every gift will have to be returned.

Follow the soul down, and count what it picks up.

Picture it falling out of the light, a point of mind and life dropping through the great nested rings of the sky. The ancient picture is of a stair of seven landings, and at each landing a Governor waits. The Anthropos does not slip past them. They love him, the text says, and each gives him a share of his own rank.

Notice what a strange kind of gift that is. A Governor's rank is his own power, his own way of ruling. It is not given as a favor to a stranger. It is given to a being who is, in a sense, a ruler too. A share of a Governor's order is a share of that Governor's nature.

The Poimandres does not stop and itemize the gifts on the way down. It tells us what the soul will give back on the way up, and from that list we can read the gifts like a negative. But later writers did spell out the descent, and the pattern they gave is worth hearing.

In the early fifth century, the Latin Neoplatonist Macrobius, in his commentary on the dream of Scipio, set the whole descent out in a table of seven. As the soul comes down, he said, it takes from Saturn, at the top, reason and understanding. From Jupiter it takes the power to act. From Mars it takes spirit and courage. From the Sun it takes sense and imagination. From Venus it takes the impulse of desire. From Mercury it takes the power to speak and to interpret. And from the Moon, nearest the earth, it takes the growth of bodies.

That is a Neoplatonist's list, and the Hermetic writer's list on the way back up is not the same. It speaks of vices where Macrobius speaks of faculties. But the principle is the same, and it is the principle of a whole age. The soul is not born into the world naked. It is dressed, layer upon layer, in the gifts of the seven, and each gift is double. Courage becomes rashness. Desire becomes deceit. The power to rule becomes the lust to rule.

Think what that makes of a human being. You are not one thing. You are a bright core wrapped in seven coats, each woven by a star. The coat nearest your skin is the Moon's, the coat that makes the body grow and shrink. The outermost is Saturn's, the coldest, and nearest the light.

Here is the first of two bold readings I will allow myself in this episode, and I will make it plainly. The Poimandres is a map of the self as a thing you wear. Every habit of pride, every itch of greed, every swagger and every small deceit you recognize in yourself is, in the Hermetic picture, not you at all. It is a garment from a planet, and it can be taken off. No other idea in the book matters as much.

Now to the people who wear the garments badly.

Hermes, in the vision, has been listening, and a question rises in him. If the Human is deathless, and made of the Father's own light, why do so many sin so greatly that they lose their deathlessness? Poimandres answers that he has not understood, and he sets out the two kinds of souls.

I, the Mind, he says, am present to the blessed and good, the pure and the merciful, the pious. My presence is a help to them. At once they know all things, and they win the Father's love by the purity of their lives. They give thanks, and call on his favor, and sing hymns to him, turned toward him with ardent love.

And before they give up the body to its death, they loathe the senses. They know their works. The text puts it with real force. Poimandres says that he, the Mind, will not allow the operations of the body to run their course unchecked. As doorkeeper, he will shut the entrances of evil and cut off the thoughts of base desire.

A doorkeeper. Poimandres, the vast being of light, stands at the threshold of the mindful man's senses and keeps them shut against the worst, as an old porter keeps the gate of a great house at night.

But to the others, the senseless, the evil and wicked, the envious and covetous, the murderous and impious, Poimandres says he is far away. He gives place to the avenging daimon.

That is the other figure of the text. The daimon applies the sharpness of fire and torments such a man through his senses. It arms him still further for lawlessness, so that his punishment may be greater. And the man never stops. He has desires that can never be filled, and lusts that can never be sated, and he fights always in darkness. The daimon afflicts him without pause, and increases the fire upon him more and more.

Hear how different this is from a simple hell. The torment of the wicked soul is not a place it is sent to. It is a sharpening of what it already wants. The man who loves the body is handed over to a power that feeds the love until it burns him.

So there are two roads. On one, the Mind stands at the door. On the other, the daimon holds the fire.

Hermes has seen the whole drop now: the light, the Governors, the fall, the garments, and the burning. And there is one question left, the question the whole vision has been walking toward. He has heard how the soul comes down. He wants to know how it goes up.

How, he asks Poimandres, shall I come to Life?

And Poimandres begins, in a level voice, to describe a death.

A body lies still, and the work begins.

The setting is a death, and the text does not ask us to look away from it. Somewhere in the Roman world, in a house in a river town of Egypt, or in a street in Alexandria, a person stops breathing. The lamp is trimmed low. The household has gathered, and someone has closed the eyes. This is the moment the Poimandres has been preparing for, and its description is cold and exact.

First, the text says, the material body is given up to alteration. The form it used to have becomes unseen. That is all. There is no preserving of it, no promise of its rising again. Remember the country this text was written in, a land that had spent a staggering quantity of skill and devotion on keeping the body whole. The Hermetic author, writing in Greek in that very land, says the body is simply let go into change.

Second, the idle character, the habitual self that the person built up over years of living, is handed over to the daimon. That is the same avenging figure we have already met. The character is the part of us that clings to the manner of our life. It does not go up. It is left behind to whatever claims it.

Third, the senses of the body pass back into their sources. The sight returns to the light, the hearing to the air, the touch to the earth. They become parts again of the great elements and join with their energies. Passion and desire go into the irrational nature, the dumb animal side of the world that never had the Word.

Look at what has just been peeled away. The body, the character and the senses are all gone, and the person has not yet begun to climb. These first three are not counted among the seven. They are what is left on the floor of the death chamber before the soul even stands up.

And then, the text says, the human being speeds upward through the Harmony.

Mead's English calls it the Harmony, the great structure of the seven circles. The soul is going up the same stair it came down, but now each landing is a place of surrender. At each zone, a Governor is waiting, and the soul gives up what that Governor gave. Not by force. By being stripped, one thing at a time, like a traveler at the gates of seven customs houses.

Here is the first zone.

To the first zone, the soul gives the power of growth and decrease.

The treatise does not name the planet, but the order of the stair does. The first zone, the nearest to the earth, is the Moon's. And what does the Moon rule? Everything that swells and shrinks. The tide, the month, the filling and the emptying of the body. For the ancient world, the Moon was the great keeper of increase and decrease. The same rhythm governed a child growing tall, a body wasting in age, a wound closing, a belly filling.

The soul stands at the Moon's gate and lays down that power. It no longer needs to grow. It no longer needs to shrink. For the first time since it was bound into flesh, it is no longer measured by the body's slow arithmetic of more and less. Something goes quiet. The soul has taken off its first and closest coat.

Picture it as a garment folded and set aside on the threshold, warm still from the person who wore it.

To the second zone, the soul gives up the machinations of evil.

This is Mercury's gate, if we read the stair as the ancients read it. Mercury was the god of quick wit and trickery, the patron of merchants and thieves and clever talkers. The text calls what is given up here a device, a scheming, now inactive. Mead's rendering is that it is a device now without power.

Mark the grammar of surrender. The scheming is not being punished. It is being laid down, because it is of no use any longer. Scheming works only among things that can be cheated. Up here, where there is nothing to gain and nobody to deceive, the habit of plotting is just a tool with no hand to hold.

Think of the plotting a living person does in a single week. The small lies, the angles, the managing of how one looks to others. All of it is a coat from the second star, and the soul steps out of it as if out of a robe.

To the third zone, the soul gives up the guile of the desires.

The third gate belongs to Venus, and the treatise names what she gave: the deceit of lust. Not desire itself, but its guile. The way wanting dresses itself up as need. The way a person tells himself that he must have this, that his happiness hangs on it, that without it he is nothing.

Anyone who has ever wanted something so badly that it altered his judgment knows this garment. It tells you that you are incomplete. It tells you the thing across the room is the missing half. And it has been lying the whole time.

Now the soul lays it down. The text says it is made inactive. The desires, with all their cunning, have nowhere left to work.

Three zones have been crossed. The soul has let go of its growing and shrinking, its scheming, and its cunning desire. It is lighter. It is quieter. It is further from the earth than it has ever been since the day it leaned over the edge of the circles and looked down into the water.

But the three it has set down so far are the gentlest. They are the garments closest to the skin. What lies ahead are the heavy ones, the things that wear a man's name and stand at the center of his pride. The next gate is the gate of the Sun, and the thing it asks for is the one that most people cannot imagine living without.

The fourth zone is bright, and the soul has to be willing to be dimmed.

Here, in the middle of the seven, is the Sun, and the text names what the Sun gave: the pre-eminence of rule, the arrogance of command. In Mead's version, the soul gives to the fourth zone the pre-eminence of rule, now freed from its extravagance.

Think about what the Sun was to the people who first read this. It was the king of the planets, the ruler of the day, the great bright eye that sat in the middle of the sky. To live under the Roman Empire was to know exactly what a ruler looked like. The emperor was spoken of in the language of the Sun. A governor, a magistrate, a commander of ten soldiers, a master of a household of slaves, each of them wore a small piece of the same coat.

And the soul has to take it off. The need to be first, the need to be obeyed, the need to have others bend, is a garment, and it is being laid on the floor of the fourth zone. What a leveling this is. The text does not say that kings lose their crowns because they were wicked. It says that the habit of ruling is itself something a soul wears, and all souls leave it at the same door.

Consider a man who has spent forty years being the person in the room whom everyone looks to. He has believed it was his nature. At the Sun's gate it comes off him like a cloak, and what stands underneath has no rank at all.

To the fifth zone, the soul gives unholy daring and the rashness of audacity.

This is the gate of Mars, god of war, and the text sets down two things that belong to him, and they are close cousins. One is daring, the lunge forward without holiness. The other is the rashness of audacity, the readiness to act before thought can catch up.

A soldier knows this garment, and so does anyone who has ever done a thing in a blaze of anger and regretted it in a cold hour. It is the coat that makes a man jump first and ask afterward. It is the coat of the one who says, I will do it, who can take a sword, who can dare an oath.

Strip it away, and look what is under it. A soul that does not need to prove anything by lunging. In the fifth zone the soul stands still, unarmed and unhurried, and lets the whole bright rush of audacity slide off its shoulders.

To the sixth zone, the soul gives the evil strivings after wealth.

The sixth gate is Jupiter's, and the text now names greed, but it is careful in how it says it. The thing given up is the striving after wealth by evil means, a reaching that has been rendered vain. It is the long, patient, lifelong habit of gathering.

Think of the ordinary person in Roman Egypt. A farmer working for rent, a clerk in a granary, a small merchant on a quay. The whole shape of their days was bent around getting enough, and then getting more. And the text says that this too is a coat. It is made by a planet, handed to a soul that never needed it, and returned at the sixth gate, where it has no value at all.

There is a grim humor in that. The soul arrives at the end of the climb, having carried its wealth in its pride for seventy years, and finds that the one place where it will be spent is a place where nothing can be bought.

Six zones. Six garments set down in a row, glimmering. The soul now stands before the last gate with almost nothing on. And here the text does something strange. It does not say that the seventh thing is a passion, or a vice that can be named in a single word. It says that to the seventh zone the soul gives up the falsehood that lies in wait.

Let it sink in. The ambitious falsehood, Mead has it, lying in wait. The deceit that waits at the top.

This is Saturn's gate. Saturn is the farthest of the Governors, the slowest and the coldest, and the one nearest the sphere of the fixed stars. He is the last guard before the way out of the cosmos.

And what is the thing that waits there? Not a sin, but a lie. The soul has shed its growth and its scheming, its guile and its rank, its daring and its greed. What is left to be given up? The lie that it was all of these things. The lie that it is what the world has made of it.

Here I will make my second bold reading, and I will make it without apology. The seventh thing is the false self. It is the last, most stubborn belief of the soul: that the coats are the person. For six gates, the soul has been taking off garments it could see. At the seventh gate, it must give up the one it could not see, because it was the eyes it saw through. The Poimandres knew that the last guard before the light is not a monster. It is the conviction that you are only what you were built to be.

Why does the text say it lies in wait? Because a lie is patient. It does not rush. It sits at the very top of the stair, where the soul is lightest and most hopeful, and says: this is all there is. You were always only this. Turn back.

And the soul, having given up everything else, gives up even that.

Look at the seven laid out behind it now: growth and decrease, the schemes of evil, the guile of desire, the pride of rule, the rashness of daring, the greed for wealth, and the falsehood that waits. They are a portrait, taken as a whole, of a life lived in a body under a sky of seven rulers. They are what it costs to be a person.

And the soul that has dropped all seven is not dead, not diminished. It is, the text will say in a moment, finally itself.

But before we reach what it becomes, we have to stop on a mountain. Because the Poimandres is not the only treatise in the Corpus that says how a soul is remade. A second treatise tells how the same remaking can happen while a person is still alive, in a body, with a father and a son and a cave of silence.

The mountain is quiet, and two people are on it.

The treatise is the thirteenth of the collection. Mead's title for it is the Secret Sermon on the Mountain. The speakers are Hermes, the teacher, and his son Tat, the pupil. And it begins with the son confessing a frustration.

Tat says he has been listening to the General Sermons, and he understood one thing in them above all: that no one can be saved without rebirth. He has been waiting to be told what rebirth is. He has asked before, and been put off. He is asking now.

Hermes answers with an honest bareness. He says he can tell his son nothing except that he himself has seen a vision, made by the mercy of God, and has gone out of himself into an immortal body. He is no longer what he was. He has been born in Mind.

Tat is bewildered. He wants a teaching, and what he is given is a witness. Hermes says that this cannot be taught. It is not learned from a book. It is seen, and only seen by those to whom God grants it.

But he does tell his son what he can. The womb of this birth, he says, is wisdom conceiving in silence. The seed is the true Good. And the one who sows it is the will of God.

Think of it as a birth with no mother's cry. A conception made out of quiet, in a person who has stopped talking long enough to be reached. Tat asks who the father of such a child is. And Hermes tells him the child is begotten by the will of God, and is a human being by God's will.

Now Tat does what a good son does. He asks how he is to be ready. And Hermes says there is something that must go first. There are, in each of us, twelve tormentors.

Here is where the second treatise meets the first. In the Poimandres, the soul gives up seven things as it rises through the heavens. In the thirteenth treatise, a living man must drive out twelve, and the twelve are named. Ignorance. Grief. Intemperance. Lust. Injustice. Greed. Deceit. Envy. Treachery. Anger. Rashness. Malice.

Read the list slowly, and notice how human it is. Ignorance first, the root. Then grief, the dull grey weight. Then the appetites, intemperance and lust and greed. Then the sins against other people, injustice and deceit and treachery. Then envy, anger, rashness and malice, the four that sting. Twelve of them. The text calls them the tormentors of matter, because they dwell in the body as in a prison, and they hold a person in that prison by the force of their torment.

And against the twelve stand ten. The ten powers of God drive out the tormentors. The text names them as well: the knowledge of God, the knowledge of joy, self-control, endurance, justice, generosity, truth, the Good, life, and light.

Each of the ten comes, and each tormentor leaves. The knowledge of God drives out ignorance, and joy drives out grief. As each of the ten powers arrives, the cell empties a little more, and Hermes tells his son that when the decad is complete, the tormentors are gone. And a rebirth has happened.

Then Tat speaks, and what he says has the shake of something just seen. Father, he says, I see the All, and I see myself in Mind.

That is the whole treatise in a line. A boy on a mountain, who a moment ago did not understand, looks at the world and sees himself held inside the Mind that made it.

Hermes tells him this is rebirth. It is no longer to look at the body in its three dimensions. And he tells him to keep silence. This, he says, must not be given to everyone. Then Tat asks, and the text allows him, to sing a hymn. And what he sings is a hymn that calls the whole creation to be still.

Let every nature of the cosmos hear my hymn, he cries. Let the earth open. Let every bolt of the abyss be drawn back for me. Let the trees stand still. Let the heavens open, and the winds be quiet. I am about to sing the Lord of creation, the All and the One.

It is a small, strange, beautiful piece of poetry. A boy tells the world to hold its breath while he praises God, and the text suggests that the world does.

Now go back, for a moment, to the way down and the way up in the Poimandres. The first treatise tells of a soul that climbs through seven zones after death. The thirteenth tells of a man who goes through a version of the same stripping in life. The Hermetic writers saw no gap between the two. The journey was one journey. It could be made at the hour of death, or earlier.

One more treatise belongs beside these, though it does not come from the Greek collection. The Asclepius is a Latin text. The Greek original is lost. In it, Hermes teaches Asclepius, Tat and a third pupil, and the work is long, ranging from the nature of God to the fate of Egypt.

It is in the Asclepius that the Egyptian side of the Hermetic world shows its face most openly. The text says that the Egyptians made statues of the gods, and that these were not dead stone. They were statues animated and full of spirit and awareness. The Egyptians, the text says, discovered the art of making gods, mixing together herbs, stones and aromatic substances that held a natural power of the divine, and calling spirits into the images, so that the statues could do good and ill.

That is as far as we will follow it. The point is what the text thinks of the Egyptians. It does not call them idolaters. It calls them masters of a sacred craft.

And then, in one of its most haunting passages, the Asclepius foresees the end of all this. A day is coming, Hermes says, when Egypt will be abandoned. The shrines will be empty, the holy things will be only stories, and the devout works of the old religion will survive as words cut in stone. The gods will withdraw to heaven, and foreigners will fill the land.

It reads like an elegy, and it was written when it was still going on. The temples were closing. The old Egyptian priesthood was fading. And somebody, writing in Greek or Latin under the name of Thoth, was saving what could be saved.

So the mountain and the statues point the same way. The knowledge is real, it is dying, and it must be carried out. And the one who carries it will be the man who, at the end of the Poimandres, is told to rise and speak.

But first the soul still has to arrive somewhere. The seven garments are on the floor behind it. It stands at the top of the stair. And ahead there is a nature that has no Governor at all.

Now the soul is bare, and the text does not slow down.

Stripped, it says, of all that the Harmony worked upon it, the soul comes to the nature of the eighth. That is the Ogdoad. The eighth sphere, beyond the seven, past the last of the wandering stars. It lies outside the cage of the Governors. It is not a planet. It is not under Fate.

And the soul has, the text says, its own proper power.

Everything the seven gave it has been returned. Everything the body added has been dissolved. And what remains is the power it had before the fall, the Mind and Life it took from the Father's own light. It was never lost. It was buried, wrapped in seven coats, and now it is bare and bright.

Then it sings. The soul that has come to the Ogdoad sings hymns to the Father, together with the beings who are there. And they who are present rejoice at its coming. This is the first welcome in the whole journey. At the seven gates there were guards. In the eighth sphere there is a gathering of those who have come before, and they are glad.

Made like those who are with it, the soul hears something more. Above the nature of the eighth, there are Powers, and they are singing hymns to God in a voice of their own. The text says this voice is peculiar to them. It is not human speech. It is not the music of the spheres either. It is the sound the Powers make when they praise the Father, and the soul that has arrived is allowed to hear it.

Then comes the sentence the whole book has been building toward. In order, the text says, they ascend to the Father. They give themselves up to the Powers. And, becoming Powers, they are in God.

Let me say that again, because it is easy to hear and not take in. The soul rises into the Powers. It becomes a Power. And it comes to be in God.

The text closes the thought with one sentence, in Mead's English: this is the good end for those who have gained knowledge, to be made God.

Not to be saved. Not to be forgiven. Not to be taken into heaven as a guest, or to rest in a garden, or to wait for a judgment. To be made God.

The Greek word for knowledge here is gnosis, a knowing that is not an opinion but a seeing, the kind Tat had on the mountain. The soul that has it has already been climbing, even while it still lived in the body. And its reward is not a place to be. It is something to become.

This is where the Poimandres parts from nearly every religion that grew up around it. In Genesis, the human being is a creature, made from dust, and no matter how greatly God loves him, he is never God. In the Poimandres the Anthropos was the Father's own offspring, equal to him, and the story of the world is the story of a being forgetting what it is and then remembering. The fall was not a sin. It was a love affair with a reflection. And the return is not a pardon. It is a promotion back to what was always true.

So I will say the plain thing, which is the thing this episode has been walking toward since its first lines. The end of the journey in the Poimandres is not heaven. It is becoming God.

Now think of Hermes, still lying heavy in the vision, still held by the sweet light, hearing that. He has asked how to come to Life. He has been told. And Poimandres does not leave him to enjoy it.

Why do you delay? the Mind asks him. Having received everything from me, should you not become a guide to those who are worthy, so that the race of human beings may be saved by God through you?

That is the commission. The vision was not a gift for him alone. And Hermes accepts it. He becomes, the text says, a herald to humankind of the beauty of piety and knowledge.

He goes out and he cries to the people. O peoples, earth-born men, who have given yourselves up to drunkenness and sleep and ignorance of God, be sober. Stop your surfeit. You are bewitched by a sleep that has no reason in it.

And they gather. The text says they came to him in a body. And he asks them the question that has been the center of the whole book. Why, earth-born men, have you given yourselves over to death, when you have the power to share in deathlessness? Repent, you who walked with error. Separate yourselves from the dark light. Leave the corruption of Fate behind. Take your share of immortality.

Some of them mocked him, and went away. They had given themselves to the way of death. But others threw themselves at his feet and begged to be taught. He raised them up. He became their guide, and he taught them how to be saved. He sowed in them the words of wisdom, and they were nourished with the water of ambrosia.

Then evening came, and the light of the Sun began to fail, and he told them to give thanks to God. When they had finished, each went back to his own bed.

And Hermes wrote down in his heart the benefaction of Poimandres. Filled with what he had hoped for, he was happy beyond measure. The sleep of the body, he says, had become a sober watchfulness of the soul. The shutting of the eyes had become true vision. His silence was pregnant with good, and the utterance of his word was the begetting of good things.

It was a rebirth, and it came to him, he says, from the Mind, from Poimandres, from the Word of the Lord.

Then he sings. The last lines of the Poimandres are a hymn, and it begins with a litany. Holy is God, the Father of the All. Holy is God, whose will perfects itself through its own Powers. Holy is God, who wills to be known, and is known by his own. Holy art thou, who by the Word hast made the things that are to stand.

He prays to be accepted. He offers pure speech, from a soul and a heart that reach up to the Father, the Unutterable, whose name only silence can speak. And he asks that he may never fall from the knowledge that belongs to his being. And he asks that he, the Human, may be holy as the Father is holy, for the Father gave him all power.

That is the end of the first treatise, and the man who lay heavy on the ground at the beginning is now praying on his feet. He has the map. He knows where the soul came from. He knows what it takes up and what it gives back. He knows what waits beyond the seventh. And he knows that nothing in the cosmos, no planet, no Fate, no tormentor, can keep a soul from the Ogdoad once it has laid down the last lie.

The man who carried the Poimandres into Europe thought he was carrying the oldest book in the world.

For a hundred and fifty years after Ficino's translation, the belief held. The Pimander went through edition after edition. Hermes Trismegistus stood among the earliest theologians, a contemporary of Moses or older, a pagan who had somehow seen the Word and the Son of God before Christ was born. Popes and painters honored him. A figure labeled with his name is set into the pavement of a great Italian cathedral. And the reason for the honor was simple. If the Egyptians had taught the Word and the Son in the dawn of time, then the highest Christian truths were part of the oldest wisdom of the world.

Then in sixteen fourteen a Swiss-born scholar named Isaac Casaubon did something quiet and devastating. He read the Greek.

He was one of the finest classicists of his age, and he was writing a long attack on a Catholic historian who had leaned on the Hermetic books as proof of ancient Christian truth. Casaubon looked at the language of the Corpus Hermeticum and at its ideas, and he saw that they did not belong to the age of Moses. The Greek was the Greek of the Christian era. The thought was soaked in Platonism and the later philosophies. The books could not be what Ficino thought they were.

Anthony Grafton, who wrote about Casaubon in Defenders of the Text in nineteen ninety one, tells how that argument was made, as a matter of close philological work. Casaubon did not wave a hand. He compared words and ideas, and showed that the supposed oldest text had been written long after Christ. The belief that Hermes was older than Moses broke on the evidence of the Greek.

What do scholars say now?

The Corpus Hermeticum was written in Greek, in Roman Egypt, in the early centuries of the common era, roughly between the first and the third. The authors were not a single man named Hermes, but a number of writers, working in a world that had Greek philosophy, Egyptian priestly tradition and Jewish scripture all mixed together on the same streets. The Poimandres reads as a combination of those streams. The Genesis echoes are plain. The Platonic structure of the soul is plain. The Egyptian background is the matter that has been argued over.

Garth Fowden, in The Egyptian Hermes, published in nineteen eighty six, made the case that the Hermetic texts have real Egyptian roots. The figure of Hermes Trismegistus was Thoth in Greek dress, and the texts belonged to a living religious practice in Egypt, a spiritual movement of teachers and students and disciples, not merely a library curiosity. Brian Copenhaver, whose translation of the Hermetica appeared in nineteen ninety two, gave English readers a modern text with notes, a standard way into the Greek and the Latin that Mead's older version had first opened.

And in nineteen forty five, a find in Upper Egypt changed the picture again. Near the town of Nag Hammadi, a set of ancient codices was pulled out of the ground in a sealed jar. Among the many books, in one codex, were Hermetic works in Coptic: a text called the Discourse on the Eighth and the Ninth, a prayer of thanksgiving, and a fragment of the Perfect Discourse, the Greek ancestor of the Latin Asclepius. In the Discourse on the Eighth and the Ninth, a teacher leads a pupil up toward the very sphere the Poimandres names, the Ogdoad, and beyond it to the ninth. The Hermetic path to the eighth was not only a page in a book. It was something people had done, or tried to do, and had written down in Egypt, in a language they spoke.

Then there is the question of what the Renaissance did with it. Frances Yates, in Giordano Bruno and the Hermetic Tradition, nineteen sixty four, argued that the Hermetic books fed a whole movement of Renaissance magi, men who believed that human beings could rise by knowledge to a share in divine power. She saw in Ficino and his heirs a current that ran into the early sciences. Other scholars have since pushed back on how much she claimed. But the picture she drew of Florence, with an old banker asking for Hermes before Plato, has stayed.

That is the whole disagreement, and it is smaller than it sounds. The text is not ancient in the way Ficino thought. It is not as modern as a forgery either. It is a Greek book from Roman Egypt, written by people who knew both temples and philosophers, who wanted to tell the soul the way home.

So go back to the man.

He is lying where he began. The being of vast size is gone. The light has faded to the ordinary dimness of a room. He feels the weight of his arms again, and the cold of the floor, and the slow return of sound.

But he is not the man who lay down. He has been given a map of the sky and of himself. He knows that the seven coats are not him, and that the last one is a lie, and that what is under them was never made by any Governor.

And he knows where it ends.

He gets up to tell them.
