Full script

The Key of Solomon, the Clavicula Salomonis, was the most copied handbook of ceremonial magic in Europe, and it claims to be nothing less than King Solomon's own instruction to his son Roboam on how spirits are summoned, commanded and sent away again. It tells its reader how to make the knives, how to bathe and fast, how to draw the circle, and what to say when the spirits refuse to come.

Take the making of a single tool. The book directs that the knife with the black hilt be made in the day and hour of Saturn, dipped in the blood of a black cat and in the juice of hemlock, inscribed with names, and wrapped when it is finished in a black silk cloth. That knife has one task: to draw the circle and strike terror and fear into the spirits. A magician with this book beside him spent days making a tool of that kind before he spoke a single word to anything unseen.

You will find that this is the pattern of the whole work. The summoning, which is what everyone pictures, takes up a few pages. The rest is preparation: of the body, of the tools, of the hour, of the conscience. That is why the book repays slow teaching, and why I mean to teach it slowly.

The Key is divided into two books. The first holds the circle, the prayers, the conjurations, the pentacles and the practical experiments. The second is the workshop: the fasting, the baths, the garments and every instrument. I will not follow that order. I will take the book as a magician lived it, from the first day of preparation to the last licence to depart, because the book itself insists that one detail left out spoils the whole.

Three ideas carry everything, and if you hold them you will understand every page that follows. The first is that the power is not the magician's. The spirits obey, says the book, because of the names of God: Adonai, Elohim, Shaddai, and above all Tetragrammaton, which is the Greek word for the name of four letters, Yod He Vau He, the name God gives himself in the Hebrew Bible. The operator is a petitioner who carries those names, not a master who owns them. The second idea is that time is not neutral. Every day and every hour belongs to a planet and an angel, and an operation begun at the wrong moment is, in Solomon's own words, falsehood and vanity. The third is that the operator must be clean in body and in conscience, or nothing will follow. Name, time and purity. Everything in the Key is a way of delivering one of those three.

There are eight parts ahead. First comes the legend the book tells about itself, with a dead king and a casket of ivory. Then the nine days of preparation; the instruments and how each was made; the planetary days and hours; the circle and the ladder of conjurations; the forty-four pentacles; and the experiments, from finding a thief to making a man invisible. By the end you will know what the book instructs from the first day of fasting to the last word of dismissal, and you will be able to open any page of it and say what it is doing and why.

Two questions I am keeping for last, because the answers change how everything before them sounds. Who really wrote this book? And what did the authorities do to people who were caught holding a copy? The English I quote throughout is that of S. L. MacGregor Mathers, whose edition of eighteen eighty-nine is still the one most readers meet, and I give his words only in short lines.

Begin, then, with the legend, and with an old king speaking to his son.

"Treasure up, O my son Roboam! the wisdom of my words, seeing that I, Solomon, have received it from the Lord." So the Latin introduction opens, in the English of Mathers, and the voice is a father's, near the end of his life.

What follows is the book's own account of itself. The manuscripts tell it in a straight line, so I will tell it the same way.

Solomon says that on a certain night, before he slept, he called upon the holy name of God and asked for wisdom. The angel of the Lord, whose name was Homadiel, appeared to him and spoke courteously. Because Solomon had asked neither for long life, nor for riches, nor for the souls of his enemies, but only for wisdom to do justice, he was given a wise and understanding heart, and with it the knowledge of every creature in the heavens and beneath them. You will hear in that speech an echo of the Bible, and we will come back to it.

Having understood all this, Solomon says, he composed a work in which he gathered the secrets of every magical art, and he gave it a name with a purpose. He wrote it, in the words of the text, "so that like as a key openeth a treasure-house, so this Key alone may open the knowledge and understanding of magical arts and sciences." That is the title explained: the book is a key, and what it unlocks is every other book of its kind.

Then comes the command. Solomon ordered his son to make an ivory casket, to place the Key inside it, and to lay it in the sepulchre beside him when he died, "lest at another time it might fall into the hands of the wicked." And as Solomon commanded, so was it done.

Time passes. Some years after, there came to the tomb certain Babylonian philosophers, who agreed to renew the sepulchre in the dead king's honour. They dug it out and repaired it, and in the course of the work they found the ivory casket, and in the casket the Key of Secrets. They opened it with joy, and not one of them could understand a word. The wording was obscure, the arrangement occult, the sense concealed. They were not worthy, the text says, to possess the treasure.

One of them, older and more worthy than the rest, was called Iohe Grevis. Mathers adds a footnote here that is worth hearing, because it tells you what these manuscripts are like: the name is very indistinctly written in his copy, and another copy gives it as something like Iroe Grecis. Even the hero of the tale is a name that scribes struggled to read.

Iohe Grevis says to the others: "Unless we shall come and ask the interpretation from the Lord, with tears and entreaties, we shall never arrive at the knowledge of it." That night each man goes to his bed. Iohe falls on his face on the ground, weeping and striking his breast, and asks why so many men can neither understand nor interpret this knowledge, and why he himself is so ignorant. Then, on his knees with his hands stretched to heaven, he prays to the God who gave such wisdom to Solomon the son of David.

The angel of the Lord appears, and he does not explain the book. He tells Iohe that if Solomon's secrets seem obscure, it is because the Lord wished it, so that such wisdom might not fall into the hands of wicked men. Then he asks for a promise: that Iohe will give this knowledge to no living creature except to the honour of the Lord, with much discipline, to penitent, secret and faithful persons, and that whoever receives it must keep it to himself, otherwise the secrets are profaned and no effect can follow. Iohe promises. The angel says, "Go and read the Key," and ascends into heaven in a flame of fire.

Iohe reads, and the Key is changed. It is plain to him in every part. Understanding that the work might one day fall into ignorant hands, he lays a conjuration upon whoever shall come to possess it: that by the power of the Creator this treasure may come to no unworthy person, nor be shown to anyone unwise or anyone who does not fear God, and that if the reader acts otherwise, he may never be worthy to attain the effect he desires. And then Iohe puts the Key back in the ivory casket.

Stay with that last picture a moment, because it holds a lesson about how the book protects itself. The angel's promise and Iohe's conjuration mean that a failed operation is never the book's failure. Later, in Book Two, the text says it outright: if an experiment was done with all the right days, hours and ceremonies and still did not succeed, then something in it was false or defective and the operator must have erred in some point. The legend and the instructions are of one piece.

A second manuscript, a French one now numbered Lansdowne twelve hundred and three, tells the origin differently. There is no casket. Solomon, at the point of death, leaves his son a testament, and the angel appears to him as a light in the form of a blazing star at the end of a shaded avenue of trees, speaking in a voice of thunder. The rabbis who kept the testament engraved it on pieces of bark, and its pentacles in Hebrew letters on plates of copper, to be preserved in the Temple. Later a Rabbi Abognazar, a name Mathers guessed was a corruption of Aben Ezra, translated it from Hebrew into Latin and carried it to Arles in Provence. There, after the destruction of the Jews of that city, it fell into the hands of the Archbishop of Arles, who put it into the common tongue without changing a word. You can see what that tale does: it takes a Temple book and walks it, step by named step, into a Christian bishop's study.

Why Solomon? Two reasons, one from the Bible and one from history. In the first book of Kings, chapter three, God appears to Solomon in a dream at Gibeon and offers him whatever he wishes, and Solomon asks for an understanding heart. God answers, in the Authorised Version, "Lo, I have given thee a wise and understanding heart." Chapter four then describes the range of that wisdom. Solomon "spake of trees, from the cedar tree that is in Lebanon even unto the hyssop that springeth out of the wall," and also of beasts, birds, creeping things and fishes, in the same verse. A king credited with that kind of knowledge was easy to imagine as the author of a book on the virtues of herbs, stones and animals. You will meet hyssop again very soon, in the book's water-sprinkler. The Bible, however, says nothing about commanding demons.

For that we need Josephus, the Jewish historian who wrote his Antiquities of the Jews in the last years of the first century. Josephus says God gave Solomon the art against demons, for the benefit and healing of men, and that Solomon composed incantations and left behind forms of exorcism. Then he tells what he knew of its use. A Jew named Eleazar, in the presence of the Roman general Vespasian, his sons, and a crowd of officers and soldiers, freed men possessed by demons. He held to the nose of the possessed man a ring with a root, of a kind Solomon had prescribed, set under its seal. He drew the demon out through the nostrils. Then he forbade it ever to return, speaking Solomon's name and reciting the incantations Solomon had composed. And, says Josephus, to prove the demon had really gone, Eleazar set a cup of water a little way off and commanded the spirit to overturn it as it left, and it did.

By late antiquity a Greek work, the Testament of Solomon, had taken the story further, giving Solomon a ring from the archangel Michael and a legion of bound demons to labour on the Temple. Mathers, introducing his edition, saw no reason to doubt the old tradition of Solomon's authorship, and pointed to Josephus, to Eastern traditions, and to the Arabian Nights, where Solomon's magical skill is often mentioned. What the manuscripts themselves say, we will leave for later.

So the Key carries Solomon's name, his ring and his seal. But before it lets a man near a single spirit, it asks something of him. It asks for nine days.

Before the Key lets a magician wash, fast or pray, it asks him to write. A man following Book Two would first have taken paper set aside for the purpose and written out the whole operation: the conjurations and exorcisms he meant to use, the day and the hour, what must be prepared, what might be added and what left out. His mind, says the text, was to be free from all business and from every extraneous idea. Only then did he look for a place, and only then did the nine days begin.

Nine is the number the book keeps returning to. For nine days before the work, the master and his companions were to abstain from sensual pleasures and from vain and foolish conversation. The text spells out what that meant in the course of a day: no eating or drinking to excess, no buffoonery, no slander or calumny, no useless talk. In their place came good deeds, honest speech, and modesty in walking, in conversation and at table. Each morning once and each evening twice, the master recited a prayer asking pity, pardon, and, remarkably, power to see and know the spirits he wished to call.

The nine days had a shape, though the chapters of the book do not tell it identically. Do not be surprised that Book One and Book Two differ in the small print; the manuscripts differ among themselves. In Book One's account, six days pass in restraint, with the prayer and the confession said often. On the seventh day the master, alone, entered a secret place and stripped. He then bathed from head to foot in water that had been exorcised, which means water over which prayers of cleansing had been said so that nothing hostile should remain in it. He spoke a prayer beginning "O Lord Adonai," asking that the water serve for the purification of his body and soul. Book Two adds that salt was blessed and cast into the bath, and that he entered the water twice, with psalms. Afterwards he put on clean white linen.

Then he turned to his companions. He had them strip, poured exorcised water over their heads so that it flowed down to their feet, and told them that they were regenerated, renewed, washed and pure. They too put on white linen.

The last three days were the fast. The magician ate only once a day and only fasting food, with bread and water counted the better course, and Book Two has him pray at a steady rhythm: once in the morning, twice about noon, three times in the afternoon, four in the evening and five before sleep. That is fifteen prayers a day. On the very last day he ate nothing. The text asked for calm weather, without wind and without clouds rushing across the sky. The master and his companions went to a spring or a running stream, bathed again, and dressed once more in fresh white linen.

Then came the confession, and it is the most human passage in the book. It is long. The master confesses pride, avarice, gluttony and drunkenness; theft, violence and homicide; failing to visit prisoners, depriving the dead of burial, neither feeding the hungry nor giving drink to the thirsty. He confesses irreverence in church, lies, slander and false vows, and then the sins of each of the five senses in turn. It ends with him recognising himself, in the book's own words, as in God's sight "the most criminal of all men." And in the next breath he asks that he may see and know all the spirits he is about to invoke. The companions said the same confession in low voices. The master then kissed each of them on the forehead as a sign of penitence, each kissed the other, and the master extended his hands over them in absolution and blessing.

The book is particular about who stands beside him. In any operation to be carried out in a circle, three companions were wanted, though five, seven or nine would serve. They were bound by oath to do whatever the master ordered, and the text warns that those who disobeyed would suffer pains and dangers raised by the spirits, and might even die.

If no companions could be found, the book allows something unexpected. The master might take a faithful and attached dog, bathe it in exorcised water, perfume it with incense, and address it, asking that it be his true companion and faithful friend. Better still, says the text, was a little boy or girl. The child's nails were pared, the child was bathed, and the master told the child that it would have neither will nor power to hide anything from him or to keep back the truth in all he asked. Then he washed the child again, so that the spirits might neither harm it nor abide in it.

Now the clothing. The outer garments and those beneath were of linen, or of silk if the magician had the means, and the thread was to have been spun by a young maiden. Special characters were embroidered on the breast in red silk. The shoes were of white leather, marked with signs, and made during the nine days. On his head the master wore a crown of virgin paper, new paper that had never served another purpose, with the name Yod He Vau He in front, Adonai behind, El on the right and Elohim on the left. The companions wore crowns marked in scarlet. The book adds that if the linen had once been the vestments of Levites or priests and used for holy things, so much the better. Over the chest, sewn on with a consecrated needle and a thread woven by a young girl, went the pentacles he would need. Psalms were recited as each garment was put on.

Why all this? The Key gives its own answer, and it is the single most important thing to understand about it. In the Lansdowne preface, Solomon promises his son success only on condition that all his works tend to the honour of God. He tells him that if he does not mean to use these secrets for a good purpose, he should cast the Testament into the fire, for the benevolent angels, wearied by illicit demands, would carry out God's commands to his sorrow. The book also remarks, in Book Two, that exact preparation of days and hours is less necessary for adepts, who have faith from practice, than for beginners, who have little.

I will put it more boldly than the scholars would. The Key is built like a liturgy in which the spirits are the last guests to arrive: everything before them is addressed to God, and the spirits come under the authority of his names, not the magician's own.

So the magician stands washed, robed, crowned and absolved. His hands, however, are empty, and everything he will carry into the circle he must first make.

Picture a workbench on the ninth day. On it lies a blade of steel, not yet the knife it will become. According to Book Two, the magician heats it in the fire until it glows red, plunges it into a mixture of the blood of a gosling and the juice of the herb pimpernel, and does this three times. Then he fastens to it a white hilt, engraved with characters, and writes along the blade, from the point towards the hilt, the names Agla and On. That is how the knife with the white hilt is finished.

It must be made in the day and hour of Mercury, when Mars stands in the sign of the Ram or the Scorpion, and with the Moon full or increasing in light. When it is done, it is perfumed, sprinkled and wrapped in silk. With it, the book says, the magician may do every operation of the art except draw the circles.

For circles there is the second knife, the one we began with. The knife with the black hilt is made the same way, but in the day and hour of Saturn, dipped in the blood of a black cat and the juice of hemlock, and wrapped in black silk. You will see its work in Part Five.

The scimitar, the sickle, the dagger, the poniard and the short lance follow the same recipe, in Mercury's day and hour, with the blood of a magpie and the juice of the herb called Mercury. Their handles are of white boxwood cut at a single stroke from the tree at sunrise, with a new knife. The staff is of elder, cane or rosewood, and the wand of hazel or nut, in each case virgin wood, meaning wood of one year's growth only, cut at a single stroke on the day of Mercury at sunrise, its characters engraved in Mercury's hour. When the wand and staff are finished the master prays that Adonai bless and consecrate them.

The sword is made from a new blade, cleaned and polished on the day of Mercury at the first or the fifteenth hour. On one side are written Yod He Vau He, Adonai and Eheieh; on the other, Elohim Gibor. The master then conjures it by a string of names, among them the word Abracadabra, asking that it serve as strength and defence against his enemies visible and invisible. One manuscript adds three more swords for the companions, with the names of the angels Gabriel, Auriel and Raphael on their pommels.

The burin, the engraver's tool, was consecrated in the hour of Mars or Venus, with a prayer to Asophiel; the needle, the same. All steel tools, the book says elsewhere, were made in the day and hour of Jupiter.

Then come the instruments of writing, and here the book becomes most exacting. The pen was a feather: the third from the right wing of a male gosling, plucked with a prayer that deceit and error be banished from it. Or it might be a swallow's feather, or a crow's, plucked with an invocation of Michael and two other captains of the heavenly army. The inkstand was engraved with names in Mercury's hour. And for some purposes the book calls for blood: of a living bat, pierced in a vein of the right wing after an exorcism, or of a pigeon or other winged creature. You should know that Mathers, who printed all this, added a note beside the chapter of the bat saying he could not impress too strongly that the use of blood was more or less connected with black magic and should be avoided.

The writing surface is the most elaborate object of all. Virgin parchment, the book explains, comes from an animal too young to have bred; unborn parchment from one taken from its mother before its time. Either must be male, and the work is done in Mercury's hour in a place where no man may see. The magician cuts a marsh reed at one stroke, with Psalm seventy-two said first, shapes it into a knife and uses that reed-knife to flay the animal. He rubs the skin with exorcised salt and leaves it in the sun for a whole day; soaks it three days in a glazed vessel of lime and water; scrapes it; stretches it on a hoop of hazel with a cord spun by a young maiden and stones from a brook; and dries it three days in a dark and shady place. Nor must any woman who has her courses be allowed to see the parchment, or it loses its virtue. Where this is too tedious, the book gives a shorter method, "not so good," and permits the cauls of newborn children, suitably consecrated, as a substitute.

I have given you only the principal tools. The book goes on to wax candles made from virgin wax with wicks by a young girl, each weighing half a pound, to be carried in a square lantern with panes of crystal. It describes a trumpet of new wood inscribed with the names Elohim Gibor and Elohim Tzabaoth, to be sounded to the four quarters before the work. It prescribes perfumes of incense, aloes, nutmeg, gum benjamin and musk, lit in new earthen vessels with fire struck from flint and steel. It requires a water sprinkler of vervain, fennel, lavender, sage, valerian, mint, garden basil, rosemary and, yes, hyssop, bound with a thread spun by a young maiden and gathered in Mercury's hour. Each instrument, once consecrated, is wrapped in silk of any colour except black or grey, which has itself been written on, censed and kept seven days among sweet spices. And at the end of Book Two there is a small book of prayers, names of angels in litanies, seals and characters, consecrated over seven days beginning on a Saturday, laid on a table under a lamp that burns day and night behind a white curtain.

Now notice the pattern. You will see Mercury again and again. The planet of writing, eloquence and cunning stands over almost every workshop task in the book, the knives, pens, ink, parchment, wands, water and candles, and the book is not shy about why: in its list of what Mercury governs, which you will read in the next part, are writings, wonders and the answers of spirits. Notice also the vocabulary of purity: virgin, new, single stroke, spun by a young maiden. Each of these means the same thing, that the object has never touched another use.

All of these objects were made and used by the clock, and the clock has not yet been explained.

The first working rule of Book One is a warning about time. Before anything else, says Solomon, the magician must have prepared the order of the hours and the days and the position of the Moon, and without that he can effect nothing. If you want to understand why the tools were made when they were, and why spirits were called at some hours and not others, you must learn the clock the book runs on. It is simpler than it looks.

There are seven planets in the old reckoning, which counts the Sun and Moon among them. The Key lists them from the highest in the heavens to the lowest, and gives each its Hebrew name. At the top is Saturn, Shabbathai. Beneath it is Jupiter, Tzedeq; beneath that Mars, Madim; then the Sun, Shemesh; then Venus, Nogah; then Mercury, Kokav; and lowest of all, the Moon, Levanah. Saturn, Jupiter, Mars, the Sun, Venus, Mercury, the Moon. That single ladder is the whole mechanism, and I will ask you to carry it with you.

Each planet rules the day whose name it bears: Saturn, Saturday; Jupiter, Thursday; Mars, Tuesday; the Sun, Sunday; Venus, Friday; Mercury, Wednesday; the Moon, Monday. In English only three of these names are plain, but in the Romance languages of the manuscripts you can hear the rest: the French mardi, mercredi, jeudi and vendredi still carry Mars, Mercury, Jupiter and Venus.

The hours work the same way. The book says that the hours of day and night together are twenty-four, and that each is governed by one planet in regular order down the ladder. The rule begins from dawn, at sunrise, on the day that takes its name from the planet. The book gives the Saturday example itself. Saturn rules the first hour, Jupiter the second, Mars the third, the Sun the fourth, Venus the fifth, Mercury the sixth, the Moon the seventh, and then Saturn returns for the eighth, and the others in their turn, always in the same order.

With that you can work out any hour yourself. On a Thursday the first hour is Jupiter's, then Mars, the Sun, Venus, and the fifth hour belongs to Mercury. And you can see why the week runs in the order it does. On Saturday, Saturn rules the first, eighth, fifteenth and twenty-second hours; Jupiter the twenty-third and Mars the twenty-fourth; so the first hour of the next day falls to the Sun. That is how Saturday is followed by Sunday, and Sunday by Monday, and so on around the week.

Each hour also had an angel and a name. The Lansdowne introduction prints three tables, which Mathers reproduced. The first gives the planet of every hour for every day. The second gives twenty-four magical names for the hours, beginning after midnight with Yayn, Yanor, Nasnia, Salla, and continuing through the afternoon names, of which the thirteenth is Beron, to the last, Salam, and sets against each one an angel for each day of the week. The third lists, for each planet, an archangel, an angel, a metal and a colour. For Saturn: the archangel Tzaphqiel, the angel Cassiel, lead, black. For Jupiter: Tzadiqel and Sachiel, tin, blue. For Mars: Khamael and Zamael, iron, red. For the Sun: Raphael and Michael, gold, yellow. For Venus: Haniel and Anael, copper, green. For Mercury: Michael and Raphael, the mixture of metals, purple or mixed colours. For the Moon: Gabriel in both places, silver, white.

Mathers worked an example in his notes. The hour from noon to one o'clock on a Tuesday is under Mars; its magical name is Beron; its ruling angel is Zamael. Tuesday itself belongs to Mars, whose archangel is Khamael, with iron and red as its metal and colour. That is the kind of reading the book expects.

Now what is each hour for? The book is quite specific. Of the seven planets, Saturn's days and hours serve for summoning the souls of the dead, but only of those who died a natural death; for good or ill fortune on buildings; for familiar spirits to attend one in sleep; for learning; and also for destruction, death, hatred and discord. Jupiter's serve for honours, riches, friendships and health. Mars's for war, courage, overthrowing enemies and for ruin, slaughter and wounding. The Sun's for wealth, hope, gain, divination, the favour of princes, and dissolving hostility. Venus's for friendship, kindness, love, joyous undertakings and travel. Mercury's for eloquence, quickness in business, science, divination, wonders, apparitions and answers about the future, and also for thefts, writings, deceit and merchandise. The Moon's for embassies, voyages, messages, navigation, reconciliation, love and merchandise by water.

Some hours were better for particular acts. The hours of Saturn, Mars and the Moon are alike good for speaking with spirits; those of Mercury for recovering thefts by the aid of spirits; the hours of Mars for calling up the souls of those slain in battle. For conversing with spirits in general, the book says, the best day is Mercury's, in his hour, and the hour in which the work begins is always Mercury's.

The Moon needed watching too. The book forbids beginning anything while she is in conjunction with the Sun, which it calls extremely unfortunate. From new moon to full is the time for building and constructing; the waning moon is the time for war, disturbance and discord; and when she is almost without light, it is the time for invisibility and death. For invoking spirits, necromancy and recovering stolen goods, she should stand in an earthy sign, Taurus, Virgo or Capricorn; for love, grace and invisibility, in a fiery one, Aries, Leo or Sagittarius; for hatred and destruction, in a watery one, Cancer, Scorpio or Pisces; and for matters of no one kind, in an airy one, Gemini, Libra or Aquarius.

The spirits themselves are sorted the same way. Solomon tells his son that there are spirits of the Empyrean heaven and of the starry heaven; there are Saturnites, Jovial, Martial, Solar, Venerean, Mercurial and Lunar spirits; and there are spirits in the elements of fire, air, water and earth. Every person, he adds, has a guardian genius, and a man of fiery temperament would find his under the empire of a spirit called Bael.

You now have the clock. When the hour came, the magician and his companions went out to the place, carrying everything they had made.

The procession, according to the book, left the house in a fixed order. The first companion carried the censer, the fire and the incense; the second, the book, the paper, the pens, the ink and the perfumes; the third, the knife and the sickle, with the lantern and candles; and the master himself carried the staff and the wand. As they went he sprinkled the path with water and hyssop, and the companions repeated in low voices the prayers of the fast.

The place was chosen for solitude: desolate regions, the borders of lakes, forests, old deserted houses, caves, and best of all, says the book, a crossroads in the depth and silence of night. But a purified chamber would do. Arrived there, the master first sounded the trumpet of new wood to each of the four quarters, East, South, West, North, and warned the spirits that they would be summoned.

Then the circle. Book One describes it in detail. With the knife of art the master drew an inner circle, then a second circle one foot beyond it, on the same centre. In the space between he marked, towards the four quarters, the letter Tau, the sign of the cross, and four hexagonal figures, and between these four great names of God. Beyond them he drew two squares, one inside the other, with the angles pointing to the four quarters of the earth, and at the extreme angles four more circles, each with a name inside: El in the East, Yah in the West, Agla in the South and Adonai in the North. Between the squares he wrote Tetragrammaton. A different chapter in Book Two describes a circle laid out with a cord of nine feet fixed to the sickle at the centre, with openings left towards the north for entry and exit, to be closed once the master was inside. While the circle was drawn, he recited six psalms.

Then he placed his companions at the four quarters, each with a new naked sword in his hand, in addition to the consecrated sword of the art, and each with strict orders not to move from his place. The master went out once more, lit the fires in earthen pots, set censers at the four quarters and lit the consecrated taper. Then he came back in and closed the circle. He said a prayer asking that God enter the circle with a happy and eternal entry and that the angels of peace protect it. He put on his paper crown with the four names Agla, Aglai, Aglata, Aglatai written on its front, back and sides. And he had on his chest the pentacles his purpose required.

Now the summoning. The master, facing east, pronounced the first conjuration. It is a ladder of divine names, each paired with a biblical story of rescue. Eheieh, the name Adam invoked, is credited with giving him knowledge of created things; the name Elohim with saving Noah from the flood; Shaddai with the parting of the sea for Moses; and Adonai Melekh with the stopping of the sun for Joshua. It ends by invoking the ten angels who preside over the ten Sephiroth. Sephiroth are the ten emanations of the divine in Jewish mystical tradition, and Mathers notes in the margin that the conjuration is built on that scheme. Then, turning to the four quarters with the pentacles in hand, he spoke the words the book likes best: "be ye gentle and peaceable, and obey in all things that we shall command ye."

If the spirits did not come, the master moved to the second step, which the book calls stronger. He uncovered the pentacles and, holding the consecrated knife in his right hand, struck the air with it as if to begin a combat. He spoke a conjuration that recites, name by name, the plagues of Egypt, the Red Sea and the tablets of Sinai, each as the work of a divine name. He repeated it to each of the four quarters, East, South, West and North, so that it was spoken four times in all. If they still did not appear, he made the sign of the Tau on the foreheads of his companions and spoke a third conjuration, which sets out the terrors of the Last Judgment. And if they were still absent, the master and his companions knelt and called upon the angels of God to witness the disobedience of the fallen spirits.

The last in the ladder is the longest, headed "an extremely powerful conjuration." It threatens as well as commands. It lists the ten choirs of angels and the angels Metatron, Sangariel and Michael. It promises the spirits that if they come willingly they will inhale pleasant perfumes and be dismissed with thanks, and that if they do not, they will fall into disease and leprosy and be consumed by fire. Before it comes a short instruction: if they appear, show them the pentacles and say that they are to be gentle and peaceable. "They will then immediately talk with thee, as a friend speaketh unto a friend." And if they do not appear even yet, the book has advice that is almost the moral of the whole ritual: "there is nothing in the world stronger and of greater force to overawe the Spirits than constancy."

What did the spirits look like when they came? On this the book gives a vivid account. They come from all sides in haste with their princes. Those of the first order are like soldiers armed with spears, shields and corslets. Those of the second are like barons, princes, dukes, captains and generals of armies. For the third and last order their king appears, crowned with a diadem, preceded by many players of instruments and by melodious singing in chorus. Book Two adds that spirits made of different substances arrive differently: those of water with rain, thunder and hail; those of clouds in horrible forms and with great noise; those of fire with the terrible nature of fire; those of air with a gentle breeze; and those from the vapours of the sun in beautiful form, but full of pride and vanity. Wind, the book adds, keeps spirits away.

The master then uncovered the pentacles to the king, saying that every knee should bow. The king would bow the knee and ask, in the book's words, "What dost thou wish, and wherefore hast thou caused us to come hither from the Infernal Abodes?" The master, in an assured and grave voice, bade him be tranquil and keep his attendants silent, renewed the incense, and made his demand. In one chapter the emperor of the spirits answers that from the time of the great Addus no exorciser had been able to behold him, and that he and his followers had been compelled by rites derived from Solomon, which few exorcisers possess. The master wrote his petition on virgin card and passed it out of the circle to the spirit king, who returned it saying that what was asked was accomplished.

Then the dismissal. "Let each of you return unto his place; be there peace between us and you, and be ye ready to come when ye are called." The master read the first chapter of Genesis, and the company left the circle one by one, the master first, and washed their faces in exorcised water.

The book also has a last resort for the stubborn spirit: his name written on virgin paper, soiled with mud, and burned over rue and asafoetida with a curse. The spirits then come crying for deliverance, and are received with a sweeter fumigation and dismissed with thanks.

In nearly every scene I have just described, the master reached for something on his breast and uncovered it. That object is what most owners of the Key valued most.

When Mathers prepared his edition, he spent years on one problem alone: the pentacles. In the manuscripts the Hebrew letters inside them had been copied by scribes who could not read Hebrew, until, as he complained in his preface, some were so vilely scribbled as to be actually undecipherable. He restored them letter by letter, and what he gave the reader is the set of the forty-four pentacles, which the book calls the whole science of the Key.

A pentacle, in this book, is a medal or figure carrying divine names and sacred characters. It was made either of the metal suited to its planet, or drawn on virgin parchment or paper in the planet's colour. It was ringed with a verse from the Bible and often with the names of angels. The planets and their metals and colours are those you already know: lead and black for Saturn, tin and blue for Jupiter, iron and red for Mars, gold and yellow for the Sun, copper and green for Venus, mixed metals and colours for Mercury, silver for the Moon. There are seven pentacles each for Saturn, Jupiter, Mars and the Sun, five each for Venus and Mercury, and six for the Moon. Seven, seven, seven and seven, five, five and six: forty-four.

The book is not quite consistent about making them. One chapter says they should be made in the day and hour of Mercury, with the Moon in an airy or earthy sign and increasing, using gold, vermilion and azure blue. Another says they are engraved in the day and hour of the planet they belong to. A magician who read both had to choose. Either way, the pentacles were consecrated with fumigation of frankincense, mastic and aloes, held over the smoke facing East, with eight psalms and an oration asking God, who had granted them to Solomon, to give them power against all spirits. Then they were wrapped in silk. Worn on the breast, covered by a silk cloth, they were uncovered and shown to the spirits when the moment came.

What were they for? The book says the Medals strike terror into the spirits and reduce them to obedience, and have further virtue against perils of earth, air, water and fire, against poison, infirmity and sorcery. Let me take you through a selection, as the text states each one.

Saturn's first pentacle, in the book's words, "is of great value and utility for striking terror into the Spirits." Shown to them, they kneel and obey; its verse speaks of enemies licking the dust. The second is the famous square of five words, Sator, Arepo, Tenet, Opera, Rotas, a double acrostic which reads the same in every direction. It is of great value against adversities and for repressing the pride of the spirits. Mathers argues that the square has twenty-five letters, which with the unity make twenty-six, the numerical value of the name Tetragrammaton. The fourth serves for operations of ruin, destruction and death. The fifth defends those who invoke Saturn's spirits at night and drives away the spirits that guard treasure. The seventh is fit for exciting earthquakes, and carries the names of nine orders of angels.

Jupiter's second pentacle is for glory, honours, riches and tranquillity of mind, and for discovering treasure; it was to be written with the pen of a swallow and the blood of a screech owl. The fourth is for riches and honour, with the angel Bariel, and was to be engraved on silver in Jupiter's day and hour when he is in the sign of Cancer. The fifth is for assured visions, and the text adds that Jacob, armed with it, beheld the ladder reaching to heaven. Mathers observes, honestly, that the verse around it is from Ezekiel, which makes the claim about Jacob an anachronism. The seventh is against poverty.

Mars's second pentacle serves against all diseases when applied to the afflicted part; the fourth gives victory in war; the sixth, for a man armed with it, means that if attacked he is neither injured nor wounded and his enemy's weapons turn against him. The seventh, written with the blood of a bat in Mars's day and hour and uncovered within the circle, was said to bring hail and tempest.

The Sun's first pentacle bears the countenance of the great angel Metatron, whom Mathers calls the Prince of Countenances; it is the countenance of Shaddai, at whose aspect all creatures obey. The fourth enables the operator to see spirits who appear invisible; the fifth calls the spirits who can transport a man from one place to another in short time over a long distance; the sixth serves excellently for invisibility; the seventh, engraved in gold on the Sun's day and hour, would deliver anyone imprisoned in fetters of iron.

Venus's third pentacle attracts love when it is only shown to a person, and its angel Monachiel is invoked in Venus's hour, at one o'clock or at eight. The fourth compels the spirits of Venus to force on the instant any person the magician wished to come to him. The fifth, when shown to someone, incites wonderfully to love; its verse is the heart like melting wax.

Mercury's second pentacle brings things contrary to the order of nature, and the spirits written on it answer easily but can hardly be seen. The fourth gives understanding and knowledge of all created things and commands spirits called Allatori to carry out embassies. The fifth opens doors however they are closed, and is surrounded by the verse from the twenty-fourth Psalm: "Lift up your heads, O ye gates."

The Moon's first pentacle, a kind of picture of a door, also opens doors. The second is against perils by water, and, if the Moon's own spirits should raise rain and tempests about the circle to terrify the operator, showing it quiets them. The fourth defends against sorceries, and its angel Sophiel gives knowledge of herbs and stones. The fifth gives answers in sleep and, the book says, may also be used to summon the souls of the departed. The sixth, engraved on silver and placed under water, will cause heavy rain as long as it remains there.

You will notice how the pentacles differ from the knives and the circle. Those are things the magician makes in order to act. These are things he shows, so that the divine names do the acting. That is why they were worn over the heart, and why the book at one point calls them the standards and banners of God the Conqueror.

Some of the book's other operations needed nothing beyond a prayer and a figure. We turn now to the practical experiments.

Of all parts of the Key, these are the ones that startled Mathers' Victorian readers, and they are the most revealing for historians, because they show what ordinary people wanted from magic. They come, mostly, at the end of Book One, and several are taken from one manuscript only, the French Lansdowne twelve hundred and three. Here are the ones most worth your time.

First, the recovery of stolen things. The book's formal method calls on the spirits in the hour of the Moon or Mercury, and has the master say a prayer that the spirits show the thieves and where they are to be found. A child may be present to see in vision what the master seeks. But the book adds two homely methods, and these are the ones that stay in the memory. In the first, a sieve is hung from a cord that has once hanged a man, with characters written in blood in its four quarters. A brass basin of fountain water is set beneath. The operator spins the sieve with his left hand and, with his right, stirs the water in the opposite direction with a twig of green laurel. When both come to rest, he gazes in the water and sees the face of the thief. If he cuts a mark in the water's image with the magical sword, the same mark will be found on the thief's face.

In the second, called the sieve and shears, the open points of a pair of scissors are stuck into the sieve's rim, and the rings rest on the thumbnails of two people. One of them prays, naming Susanna, Thekla and Daniel, as God's rescuers of the innocent. Then the names of everyone in the household are read aloud, and after each, "By Saint Peter and Saint Paul, such a person hath not done this thing," with the other answering in the same words. The sieve, says the book, turns of itself at the name of the guilty. Mathers noted that this is an ancient divination, but that the names of the two saints show it had been reconstructed in the Middle Ages.

Second, invisibility. The book first gives a conjuration to a spirit called Almiras, Master of Invisibility, and his ten ministers. Then, from the Lansdowne manuscript, a method by image: a little man of yellow wax made in January in Saturn's day and hour, with a character written on the crown of the head with a needle, and a strip of frog or toad skin inscribed with words. The figure is hung by one of the maker's hairs from the roof of a cavern at midnight, perfumed, buried in a small box of deal, and afterwards carried in the left pocket, with the words, "Come unto me and never quit me."

Third, an operation to hinder a sportsman from killing game. A stick of green elder, hollowed at both ends, with strips of hare-skin parchment written in the blood of a black hen in each end, is fumigated on a Friday in February, buried beneath an elder tree and then laid in the path the huntsman will take. Once he has passed it, the book says, he need not hope to kill any game that day.

Fourth, the magic garters. They are made of stag's skin, written inside with the blood of a hare killed on the twenty-fifth of June, filled with green mugwort gathered the same day before sunrise, and with the eye of a barbel fish at the ends. Washed in running water before sunrise and worn above the knees, with a rod of holm oak cut that same day, they were said to let their wearer write the name of a place on the ground and arrive in a few days without fatigue. To stop, he said "Amech" and beat the air with the wand.

Fifth, the magic carpet, which is not a flying carpet but a carpet for questions. It was made of new white wool and spread out in the open country when the Moon was full in Capricorn, in the hour of the Sun. The master called on four archangels at the four points and raised each corner in turn with a prayer to Agla. For the questioning itself, on a night of full or new moon, between midnight and daybreak, he lay face down under the carpet with a censer beneath it and a strip of azure parchment against his forehead, written with a dove's feather. He prayed to God to make him discover the hidden matter, and, says the text, he would hear distinctly the answer he sought.

Sixth, the treasure possessed by spirits. The book explains that earth is inhabited by spirits called Gnomes who know where treasure lies, and who sometimes kill those who search for it. This, it says, is not through avarice, because a spirit cannot possess anything, but a warning against greed. The master, on a Sunday before sunrise between the tenth of July and the twentieth of August with the Moon in Leo, drew a circle with the sword over the spot, censed it three times through the day and hung a lamp above the opening. The oil was mixed with the fat of a man who had died in July, and the wick was made from his burial cloth. The workmen were girded with a belt of fresh goatskin written in the dead man's blood and told not to be disturbed by the spectres they would see. When the digging was done, the spirits were thanked and licensed to depart.

Seventh, favour and love. A paper with the Sator square, a string of divine names and the ten Sephiroth was placed in clean silk and buried for a day and a night where four roads meet. It was then carried in the right hand when favour was sought.

And last, the operations of mockery and deceit. The master wrote names with the blood of a bat and called on spirits who love "the times and places wherein all kinds of mockeries and deceits are practised," asking that those present might seem to see what they did not see and hear what they did not hear.

Mathers tells us in his preface what he did with this material. He left out one or two experiments, as he put it, partaking largely of black magic, and derived from two other books attributed to Solomon, the Grimorium Verum and one called a reduced Clavicula. He does not say which. He also cautioned the practical student against blood: "the prayer, the pentacle, and the perfumes, rightly used, are sufficient."

All of these recipes assume a book that was made by many hands. That brings us to the question I promised to hold until now.

Take the oldest manuscript Mathers used, Additional manuscript ten thousand eight hundred and sixty-two in the British Museum. He dates it to about the end of the sixteenth century. It is written in contracted Latin, hard to read, and its title says simply that this is the Key of Solomon translated from the Hebrew language into the Latin. That copy was made some two and a half thousand years after Solomon. The others Mathers used, in French and Italian, he places in the seventeenth century and later.

He noticed something else. All seven manuscripts, he says, agree in one thing: the execrable mangling of the Hebrew words through the ignorance of the transcribers. The people who copied this supposedly Hebrew book could not read Hebrew. Mathers took this as a defect to be repaired. Historians take it as evidence.

What the manuscripts show, and what scholars have concluded from sorting them into families, is that the Key as we know it took shape in fourteenth or fifteenth-century Italy, in Latin and Italian, and that no copy of it earlier than the Renaissance is known. It did not come from nowhere. Behind it stands a Greek handbook of the Byzantine world called the Hygromanteia, which means roughly divination by water, and which is also fathered on Solomon. It shares with the Key the planetary days and hours, the conjuring of spirits, and the habit of speaking in Solomon's voice to his son Roboam. The Italian book was in many ways a Latin descendant of that Greek tradition.

Once it existed, it multiplied. Through the sixteenth, seventeenth and eighteenth centuries copies spread in Italian, French, Latin and English, and at last in Hebrew, a Hebrew version translated back from the European text into the very language its title had always claimed. The copies differ. Chapters are classed differently, and one manuscript, as Mathers notes, divides nothing into chapters at all. One omits all of the chapters in Book One after the eighth. One is called the True Keys of King Solomon, and another the Veritable Keys. And the manuscripts bring their own authenticating stories. One family claims, on its title page, to be a translation from the Hebrew into Italian by Abraham Colorno, by order of the Duke of Mantua, and then recently put into French. Another has Rabbi Abognazar and the Archbishop of Arles. I read these as a reader's insurance. Every reader of a book that claims to be Solomon's will ask where it has been for three thousand years, and each translator named in a title is a link in a chain that answers him. No Hebrew original, however, has ever been produced, and Mathers himself admitted that it had in all likelihood been entirely lost.

And now to what the authorities did. In fifteen fifty-nine the Clavicula Salomonis was placed on the Index of Prohibited Books, the list of works the Roman Church forbade its members to read. That mattered in a world of print, where books could be seized from shops. But the Key had long since escaped print. It passed hand to hand as copies in manuscript, which is why so many survive in so many forms, and why Mathers could claim in eighteen eighty-eight that, apart from one curtailed and incomplete French copy of the seventeenth century, it had never been printed at all.

The Inquisition did not ignore handwritten books. In Venice, a city of printers, booksellers and many practitioners of folk magic, its tribunal brought to trial people found with handwritten copies of magical books, among them the Key. Federico Barbierato, working through the Venetian trial records for his book The Inquisitor in the Hat Shop, shows how these books were owned, lent, copied and sold, and how men of the church were counted among their readers. The punishments recorded in such cases were usually abjuration, penance and the surrender of the book, rarely anything worse. A man with a copy of the Key was not a hero of forbidden knowledge. He was often a minor figure in a city of small ambitions, wanting a lost purse, a lover's favour, or a buried treasure, and the book itself, as you have seen, offered all three.

That is the real history: a book of Italian Renaissance make, built on a Greek Byzantine ancestor, spread by scribes who could not read its Hebrew, dressed in a legend of kings and casket and archbishop, and read by people who believed every word of the legend and the recipes alike.

In October of eighteen eighty-eight Mathers signed the preface to an English edition, and in eighteen eighty-nine it was published in London. He had put it together, he says, from several ancient manuscripts in the British Museum, which all differ from each other in various points: seven codices in all, Additional ten thousand eight hundred and sixty-two, Sloane thirteen hundred and seven and thirty ninety-one, Harleian thirty-nine eighty-one, King's two hundred and eighty-eight, and Lansdowne twelve hundred and two and twelve hundred and three. He thanked Dr. Wynn Westcott for help with the Hebrew of the pentacles. He closed the volume with two extras: a fragment which he called ancient Hebrew, translated by Eliphas Levi, and the Qabalistical Invocation of Solomon, also given by Levi. Neither comes from the seven manuscripts. Mathers also claimed, in his preface, that Levi had taken the Key as the model for his great work on ritual magic.

Mathers was a founder of the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, and through the Order the Key's equipment and ritual structure passed into modern ceremonial magic: the wand, the sword, the circle, the divine names, the idea that the magician prepares himself before he calls upon anything. And there is a second descendant. The black-handled and white-handled knives of the Key turn up in the ritual tools of the witchcraft that Gerald Gardner made public in the nineteen fifties, and the usual route runs through Mathers's printed pages.

One distinction needs making once, plainly. The Key of Solomon is not the Lesser Key of Solomon. That other book, the Lemegeton, is full of the seals of seventy-two spirits, and it is a different and later work. Mathers said as much, and warned his reader away from the black-magic books that had got mixed up with the Key. The Key you have just heard about has no catalogue of demons. It has a clock, a workshop, a circle, forty-four pentacles and a few homely recipes.

What was it, then, for the people who copied it by hand? It was a handbook, and it was also a discipline. A man who copied it learned to write a divine name with a clean pen, to wait for the right hour, to confess his faults, and to ask for what he wanted in the language of prayer. Whether the spirits came is not a question history can answer. What history can say is that for some three hundred years, in cities such as Venice, ordinary men and women preferred to spend nine days fasting and a month cutting wands and flaying parchment rather than do nothing about the things that frightened or tempted them.

Now return to the casket. Solomon asked his son to bury the book, lest it fall into the hands of the wicked. The Babylonian philosophers dug it up and could not read it. Iohe Grevis wept until an angel opened it, then laid a curse on every unworthy reader and shut it up again. The legend is a story about a book that wanted to be hidden, and the manuscripts are the proof that nobody let it stay so. Every scribe who copied it opened the casket again.

And the casket was opened again, and again.
