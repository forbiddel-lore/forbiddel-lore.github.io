The Secret of the Ba, Ka and Akh: The Hidden Parts of the Egyptian Soul

The seven parts of the Egyptian person, from the Book of the Dead and the Papyrus of Ani: the khet, the body that had to last; the ka that had to be fed; the ba that flew home at night; the shut, the shadow; the ren, the name that could be killed; the ib, the heart that could testify; the akh, the shining spirit; and the second death the Egyptians feared more than any hell.

Full script

The scribe Ani walks into the Hall of the Two Truths with his wife Tutu at his side, and the first thing either of them sees is the balance.

It stands in the middle of the hall, a great beam on a post, with a pan hanging from each end. The floor is cold stone. The incense has been burning longer than anyone can say, and the light is the low gold light of lamps, because no sun has ever reached this place. Ahead, in two long rows, sit forty two judges. At the far end, on a throne, wrapped like a mummy and holding the crook and the flail, sits Osiris, lord of the dead.

Ani and Tutu wear white linen. Their arms are raised, palms open, the old gesture of a person who comes before power with nothing in his hands. Ani was a scribe, a man who spent his life setting names and numbers on papyrus. Now something is about to be written down about him.

In one pan of the balance lies his heart. In the other stands a single feather, the feather of Maat, goddess of truth, of order, of the way things are meant to be. A feather is the lightest thing an Egyptian could think of. That is the point.

Anubis, the jackal-headed god who guides the dead and watches over the embalmers, kneels at the balance and lays his hand to it. He checks the beam. He checks the cord and the little plumb, so that no one can say afterward that the weighing was crooked. On the other side stands Thoth, the ibis-headed god of writing, with a palette in one hand and a reed pen in the other. He does not speak. He is waiting to write a sentence, and the sentence has not been decided.

And beside the scales, low against the foot of the post, crouches a creature that no living eye has seen and every Egyptian eye knew. Ammit. Her head is a crocodile's. The front of her body is a lion's and the back of it is a hippopotamus, the three most dangerous animals along the Nile, stitched into one. Her jaws are a little open. She is not guarding the scale. She is waiting at it, the way a hungry dog waits beside a table.

Anubis takes his hand away. The beam shivers. The pan that holds the heart dips, a hair's breadth, and the pan that holds the feather lifts, and for one long breath the whole hall watches the cord of the plumb swing, and swing, and begin to slow.

That moment has been held in paint for more than three thousand years, on a papyrus roll from the thirteenth century before the common era. It is the most famous picture in Egyptian religion, and almost everyone who looks at it thinks it shows something it does not.

They think it shows a soul on trial: one immortal self, standing before a court, to be sent up or sent down. It is a good story. It is not the Egyptian one. Ani, standing in that hall, is not one soul. The Egyptians had no single word for a soul and no single thing to put in its place. To them a person was a gathering of parts, and every part had its own nature, its own hunger, its own dangers, and its own fate when the body died. One part had to be fed. One could fly. One was a shadow. One was a name, and a name could be murdered. One was the heart, which could speak, and which in this hall was about to be made to.

That is what the Egyptians' own texts say: the lines carved on the walls of pyramids, painted on coffins, and written on rolls like Ani's. The Pyramid Texts, the Coffin Texts, the Book of the Dead. And what they say is that the afterlife was not one journey but seven, made at the same moment by seven different parts of one person, and that a person lived on only if every part was kept alive.

Seven parts. The khet, the body. The ka, the double that must eat. The ba, the bird with a human face. The shut, the shadow. The ren, the name. The ib, the heart. And the akh, the shining spirit that the justified dead became. We will take them one at a time, in that order, and each has its own story.

And there is one thing more. It is not an eighth part. It is a fate, and the Egyptians feared it more than death itself, because it came after death. It is the reason Ammit crouches beside the scale. I am keeping it for the end. It is the darkest idea in the whole Egyptian tradition, and you cannot understand it until you know what each of the seven parts was, and what it would mean to lose them one by one.

Seven parts. One fate. A feather on a scale. Let us begin with the body.

Seventy days is a long time to be dead and not yet buried.

For seventy days the body lay in the hands of the embalmers, in a workshop on the west bank of the river that stank of salt and resin and cedar oil, while the family waited for it to come out changed. A Greek traveler named Herodotus, who came up the Nile around the middle of the fifth century before the common era, wrote down what the embalmers told him. He tells us that when a family brought a body in, the embalmers showed them wooden models of corpses, painted to look real, in three grades of work and three prices, and let them choose.

Then the work began, and it began with the head. First, Herodotus says, they draw out the brain through the nostrils with a crooked iron hook. Then, with a sharp stone, they cut open the flank and take out the whole of the intestines. They rinse the empty cavity with palm wine and pack it with ground spices, and they sew it shut. Then they bury the body in natron, the dry salt of the desert, and leave it there for seventy days, and not a day more. When the time is up they wash it and wrap it from head to foot in strips of fine linen, smeared with the gum that the Egyptians use instead of glue.

The Egyptians had a name for what lay on that table. They called it the khet, the physical body, the flesh. It was the first of the seven parts and the most fragile, because it was the only one that began to die the moment life stopped. A second word, the sah, was used for the body once the embalmers had finished with it: the body made whole, wrapped, spoken over, given dignity. The khet is what dies. The sah is what the living build out of it. Sah was also the Egyptian name for the stars we call Orion, the great striding figure of Osiris in the sky, and the Pyramid Texts send the dead king up to join him.

That was the reason for all the salt and linen, and it was the foundation of everything that follows. Every other part of a person needed the body. The ka returned to it to be fed. The ba flew out of it by day and had to find it again at night. The shining spirit could only rise from a body that was still there to rise from. A person without a body was like a house without a door: nothing could come in and nothing could go out. So the Egyptians called a tomb the house of eternity, and they built it to outlast the stone it was made of.

The pattern came from a god. Osiris, a king of Egypt, was murdered by his brother Seth, who tore the body to pieces and scattered it across the land. His wife Isis and her sister Nephthys searched the country and gathered the pieces, and Anubis bound them together in linen, and breath came back to Osiris long enough for him to father a son, Horus. Then he went down to rule the dead as their king. Every Egyptian who was buried properly hoped to be treated as Osiris had been treated, and in the texts the dead are given the god's name as a title. In the papyrus of Ani he is not simply Ani. He is the Osiris Ani.

Not everything went back inside the body. The liver, the lungs, the stomach and the intestines were taken out, dried, and sealed in four jars, whose lids were carved in the shape of four gods, the sons of Horus. Imsety, with a man's head, guarded the liver. Hapy, with the head of a baboon, guarded the lungs. Duamutef, with the head of a jackal, guarded the stomach, and Qebehsenuef, with the head of a falcon, guarded the intestines. The four jars stood beside the coffin like four small sentries. The brain, which the Egyptians seem to have thought of no use to anyone, was thrown away. The heart was left exactly where it was. Remember that. It will matter.

Between the layers of linen the embalmers laid amulets: an eye of Horus, a pillar for stability, a beetle. The face was covered with a mask, painted or gilded, so that the ba and the ka, returning in the dark, would know whose body they had come to. Then the body was sealed in a coffin, and the coffin in another, until it lay like the heart of a nest.

On the day of burial the sah was stood upright at the door of its tomb for the ceremony called the Opening of the Mouth. The papyrus of Hunefer, another scribe, paints the scene: the mummy held upright by a priest in the mask of Anubis, mourning women crouched at its feet, priests with their tools and their offerings reaching to touch the face. By that ceremony the senses were given back. The dead could breathe again, speak again, eat again. The silent, wrapped thing at the door was not a corpse any longer. It was a person waiting.

We know all this from the Egyptians' own words, and we know it from three great bodies of text. The first is carved inside the pyramid of Unas at Saqqara, built about twenty three fifty before the common era. From the outside it is a low, crumbling heap. Inside, the walls of the burial chamber are covered from floor to roof in columns of hieroglyphs, cut into the limestone and filled with blue, and the ceiling is painted with stars. These are the Pyramid Texts: spells for the dead king, for his body and his food, for his name and his rising into the sky. James Allen's translation of them appeared in two thousand five.

When the age of the pyramid builders ended, the spells did not die with it. Within a few centuries, officials who were not kings began to have them painted inside their wooden coffins, in a flowing hand, so that the words lay around the body on every side. Those are the Coffin Texts. And in the age of Ani the spells moved again, onto rolls of papyrus, with pictures: the Book of the Dead, which the Egyptians themselves called the spells for going forth by day. There was never one fixed book. Each roll was a selection, made for one person, and Ani's runs to more than seventy feet.

So the khet was kept. The sah was made. The lid went down and the priests went home. But a body, however well it is wrapped, is only a thing. It cannot be hungry. And somewhere in the dark of the tomb, something was.

In the shadow of the Step Pyramid at Saqqara, against its northern face, stands a small stone box with no door. It is called the serdab, the Arabic word for a cellar, and in the front wall of it, at about the height of a standing man's eyes, two holes have been cut. Behind the holes, in the dark, someone is looking out.

It is a statue of Djoser, the king who built the pyramid, about twenty six hundred before the common era. He sits upright in the heavy striped wig and the long ceremonial beard, with his hands on his knees. The inlaid eyes are gone. He stares through the two holes anyway, north, toward the part of the sky where the stars that never set turn around the pole. Priests burned incense outside the little box, and the smoke drifted in through those holes to a king who was not breathing.

That statue was a home for the ka.

The Egyptians wrote the ka as a pair of upraised arms, bent at the elbows, reaching or embracing. It is the second of the seven parts, and the hardest to put in a sentence. John Taylor, whose books on the Egyptian afterlife are standard reading, describes it as the life force: the power that sustains a person, the difference between a body that lives and a body that does not. It was also a double, an unseen twin who stood beside the person from the beginning.

The beginning is exactly where the Egyptians put it. On the walls of Hatshepsut's temple at Deir el-Bahari, the ram-headed god Khnum sits at his potter's wheel and shapes the future queen out of clay, and beside her, identical, he shapes her ka. Two children from one wheel. The ka was not acquired at death. It was born with you, it went through life beside you, and when your body failed it did not fail. It simply went on needing what it had always needed.

What it needed was food. The Egyptian word for the ka is close to their word for sustenance, and the ka lived on bread, beer, beef, fowl, water, linen and incense. It did not eat the way a living mouth eats. It took the life out of the offering, and the priests who served the tomb ate what was left. But it had to be given something, every day, forever. That was the terrible arithmetic at the center of every Egyptian tomb. A person had died once, and the ka would need to be fed for all the time there was.

So a wealthy man built the meals into his tomb. Above ground, attached to the burial shaft, stood a chapel. In it was an offering table of stone, sometimes carved with loaves of bread in relief. He endowed a priest, a servant of the ka, with land whose harvest paid for the daily offerings. And on the western wall of the chapel he set a false door.

The false door was a slab of carved stone made to look like a doorway, with a lintel above, jambs on either side, and a panel at its center that showed the dead man sitting before a table piled with bread. It opened onto nothing. The stone behind it was solid. But it was the real entrance, the threshold between the living world and the world of the dead, and the ka came and went through it. Offerings were laid before it. The ka passed through the stone, took what it needed, and returned to the tomb.

The words spoken over those offerings were set in stone too, and they are among the most common lines in all Egyptian writing. The offering formula begins by calling the offering a gift that the king gives, to Osiris or to Anubis, so that the god may in turn give invocation offerings of bread and beer, beef and fowl, alabaster and linen, every good and pure thing on which a god lives, for the ka of the dead man, who is named. The king gives to the god; the god gives to the ka. It is a chain of generosity, and the whole chain is held up by a single spoken sentence.

That sentence mattered so much that tomb owners begged strangers for it. Inscriptions at the doors of tombs address anyone walking past. They ask the living to stop and say the formula, and if the passer-by has no bread or beer to give, then to say it with his mouth, because the breath of the mouth is enough. A sentence, spoken aloud, became a loaf. The Egyptians did not leave their dead to depend on bread alone. They decorated the chapel walls with butchers and brewers, bakers and herdsmen, harvesters bringing in grain, so that if the real servants failed, the painted ones would go on working.

And the servants did fail. Estates were sold. Priests died with no sons to take their place. Families moved on. The ka at the back of the chapel waited, and nothing came. The texts say plainly what the Egyptians feared would happen next. Spells in the Coffin Texts and the Book of the Dead beg that the dead not be made to eat excrement and drink urine in the realm of the dead, and the dead man declares that such filth is his abomination, that he will not touch it. A spell would not exist unless somebody feared the thing. The ka, left hungry, would take what there was.

The Egyptians were so used to thinking of the ka as the self that stays behind that it became the way they spoke about death. When a man died, the Egyptian might say that he had gone to his ka. It was a gentle phrase, the kind we use when we say someone has passed on. The person has gone to join his double, the other half of him, at the place where it has always been.

And so the second part of the person was known, and its fate was clear: it stayed. It stayed by the tomb and the false door and the little stone box with its two holes, and it lived or starved on the kindness of those who came after. It was not free. It was bound to one place, as a servant is bound, or a child.

But the Egyptians knew that the dead were not all bound. There was another part of the person that could leave the tomb and go where it wished. It could look up at the sun. It had wings.

In the papyri, the picture comes again and again. A mummy lies on its bier in the dark of the burial chamber, and above it, with wings spread, hangs a bird. It is not an ordinary bird. Its body is that of a falcon or a stork, but its head is a man's, with the face and sometimes the very wig of the dead person below. It is hovering, caught in the act of coming down. That is the ba.

The ba is the third of the seven parts, and the one most often mistaken for the soul. The word has been translated as soul since the nineteenth century, and the translation has stuck, but the Egyptians used the ba in a way that no soul ever was. Gods had bas. The ba of Ra was the power of the sun made visible. The ba of a person was not a thing hidden inside him, waiting to be released. It was his manifestation, the shape he took when he moved, acted, and made himself known. Above all, it was mobile. The tomb held the body, and the ka sat by the false door. The ba could go anywhere.

By day it left the tomb. The spells of the Book of the Dead, whose Egyptian title means the spells for going forth by day, were above all spells for the ba: to come out of the tomb into the light, to have power over its legs, to take whatever form it wished. The ba could become a falcon, a heron, a swallow, a lotus. It could sit in the branches of a sycamore and drink from a pool. It could join the sun god in his boat at dawn and cross the sky. And when evening came it had to go back. The ba returned to the tomb at night to rest beside the body it belonged to, and the Egyptians drew the reunion with tenderness: the bird swooping down to the mummy's face, sometimes holding the symbol of life in its claws.

The sun god did the same. The night texts of the underworld, the Duat, the realm the sun passes through between sunset and dawn, show Ra descending into the dark to meet the body of Osiris, and the two uniting. Ra is the ba; Osiris is the corpse. Each night the ba of the sun joins the body of the dead god, and by that union the dead god is renewed and the sun is born again at dawn. The Egyptians read the daily pattern of the sky as a ba returning to its body. What the sun did every night, the dead hoped to do too.

But a ba could be shut in. The spells say so. There is a spell in the Book of the Dead that the ba not be shut in, and another for opening the tomb to the ba so that it may come out by day. To be a bird that could not leave its cage, that watched the daylight through a crack, was among the fates the Egyptians dreaded. The ba without its body was lost, and the body without its ba was nothing but meat.

Louis Zabkar, who wrote the major study of the ba concept in nineteen sixty eight, drew attention to something easily missed. The ba was not another person inside the person. It was the person, acting. That is why a man could speak to his ba as to himself, and why the best-known Egyptian text about the ba is a conversation.

It survives on a single papyrus now in Berlin, catalogued as Papyrus Berlin three thousand twenty four. It was written in the Middle Kingdom, nearly four thousand years ago, and its modern title is the Dispute between a Man and his Ba. The beginning is lost. We come in at the middle of an argument.

A man is weary of life. The world around him has gone bad, and he wants to die, and his ba will not agree. The two of them have been quarrelling. The man pleads that the ba must not abandon him; he needs it at his side if he is to cross into the west. The ba pushes back. Are you not a man, it asks, and are you not alive? Death, it says, is not a cure for weariness. It paints the grave as a gate of weeping, a place where a man is carried from his house and left on a hill, and never again goes up to see the sun.

Then the ba tells him about the builders. There were men, it says, who raised pyramids of granite, who cut fair chambers and set up offering stones, and their offering stones are bare now, as bare as the stones of the poor who died on the riverbank with no one left to mourn them. Their monuments did not save them. Their names are gone, and where are they? So what is the point of a tomb? Hold to the happy day, says the ba, and forget your sorrow.

The man does not take the advice. He answers with poetry, and some of it is as bleak as anything the Egyptians ever wrote. His name, he says, stinks. To whom can he speak today? His brothers are evil. The friends of today do not love. Hearts are greedy, and every man seizes the goods of his neighbor. Gentleness has perished and violence strikes everyone; no one remembers yesterday, and no one repays a good deed. Each stanza begins with the same question, and the answer is always the same: no one.

And then the poem turns. Death, the man says, is in his sight today like the recovery of a sick man, like going out of doors after a long illness. Death is like the scent of myrrh, like sitting under an awning on a breezy day. It is like the fragrance of lotus, like a well-trodden road, like a man coming home from war. It is like the sky clearing, like a man who longs to see his home after years held in captivity. In the end, he says, whoever is yonder will be a living god, punishing the wrongdoer, standing in the boat of the sun, and no one will keep him from speaking to Ra.

It is one of the most beautiful passages that has come down from ancient Egypt. And the ba answers it, in the last speech of the papyrus, with something very simple. Cast your complaint aside, it says. My companion. My brother. Make your offerings, cling to life, as you said. Love me here. Put the west aside for now, and yet wish to reach the west when your body touches the earth. I will alight after you have died, and then we shall make a home together.

That is the ba. It is not the part that goes to heaven while the body stays behind. It is the part that stays with you through the weariness, argues with you, and promises, in the end, to share the house. It could fly. But it chose to come home.

Yet even a bird that flew in the sun trailed something on the ground, something dark and quiet that followed the man and followed the bird, and fell across every wall he passed.

At noon in Upper Egypt the shadows almost vanish. The sun stands so nearly overhead that a man walking across a courtyard in Thebes trails only a dark puddle at his feet, and a column casts hardly more. Then the afternoon wears on, and the shadows lengthen, and by sunset every standing thing has a long dark twin lying beside it on the ground.

The Egyptians watched shadows the way other peoples watch clocks. They measured the hours by them, with shadow clocks of stone and wood, and they felt the difference between shade and glare in their bodies the way only people who live under a hard sun can. Shade was the first mercy of the day. A tree's shadow was a refuge. A wall's shadow was the place a child slept in the afternoon. And so, when the Egyptians counted the parts of a person, they counted the shadow. They called it the shut.

The shut is the fourth of the seven, and the quietest. It is not a double like the ka or a traveler like the ba. It is simply there. It goes where the person goes, from the day he is born, and it is as much a part of him as his arm. In the painted tombs and in the pictures that accompany the Book of the Dead, the shut is drawn as a small human figure, filled in solid black, sometimes standing beside the ba at the door of the tomb, sometimes following its owner like a little silhouette. It is the plainest visual joke in all Egyptian art, and it is perfectly serious. The shadow is a person. He is just a flat one.

What the shadow does in the texts is protect. In the Egyptian way of thinking, a shadow was a covering, and to be in someone's shadow was to be sheltered. A good king was spoken of as a shade over his people, a cool place in the heat of the day. The shadow of a god was a refuge and a danger at once: sheltering to the faithful, and fearful to those who stood outside it. The shut of a dead man, then, was his shelter in the next world, a part of him that covered him, hid him, and stood between him and whatever came out of the dark.

It could also be taken.

The Book of the Dead is full of small anxieties like this. It has spells for the heart not to be taken away, for the head not to be cut off, for the dead man not to be made to walk upside down. It has a spell for opening the tomb to the ba and the shadow, that they may go out and come back, and others that ask that the shadow not be shut in or held captive in the realm of the dead. The shadow was something that could be seized. It could be trapped in the dark and kept there, a prisoner, while the rest of the person waited above. A man who lost his shut had lost his covering. He was exposed, and in the Duat, exposure was fatal.

In the vignette that goes with the spell for opening the tomb, the ba and the shadow are shown together, the bird and the black figure, coming out of the doorway into the day. The dead man needs both. The ba is how he moves; the shut is how he is protected while he moves. One flies, the other follows. And at night, the texts imply, both return to the same dark house where the body lies.

The Egyptians also took pleasure in shade. The vignettes of the Book of the Dead show a tree goddess in a sycamore, reaching out from the branches to give water and bread to the dead and to the ba, and the cool shadow of the tree falls over them. That is the afterlife of a people who have lived their whole lives under a hot sun. A shaded place, a pool of water, a loaf. The shut under the tree is as comfortable as it is protected.

One Egyptian ruler built a religion on the other thing, the thing the shadow is cast by. Akhenaten, who reigned in the fourteenth century before the common era, set aside the old gods and made the disk of the sun, the Aten, the one object of worship. He abandoned the old temples, founded a new capital in the desert, and called it Akhetaten, the horizon of the Aten. We know it as Amarna. And there, among the temples to the sun, he raised a set of open-air precincts that Egyptologists call sunshade temples. The Egyptian name tied them to the shut: to the shadow, the shade, of Ra.

It is an odd thing at first, a religion of the sun naming its holy places for shade. But Egyptian thought had never separated the two. A shadow is only cast where light falls, and the shut was the proof that the sun had touched the person. To raise a shrine called the shadow of the sun was to say that the god's light had a shape on the ground. The sun, in other words, was not only a burning disk. It was something that could shelter.

There is a small irony there too, because Akhenaten's own shadow, in the sense the Egyptians meant it, did not last. His buildings were taken down after his death. We will come to that. But the shut itself, the part that was a black figure on a tomb wall, was never meant to be destroyed by anything but darkness. It was the one part of a person that could not be broken in a quarrel or stolen by a rival. It could be shut in, but not erased.

The next part could be erased. It lived on other people's lips and on the faces of stone walls, it needed no body at all, and a single man with a chisel could kill it.

Somebody walks along a tomb wall, and his lips are moving. He has stopped in front of a column of hieroglyphs, followed them with his finger, and is reading them aloud. He does not know the man who is buried here. He does not know the family. But the inscription asked him, in words as clear as a hand on the shoulder, to speak the name of the dead, and he has done it.

That is the ren, the name, and it is the fifth of the seven parts.

To the Egyptians, a name was not a label. It was a piece of the person, as real as the body or the shadow. A child received a name at birth, and that name was a part of him from that moment until the end of time. The gods themselves came into being by being named; the Egyptians told stories of creation in which the world is spoken into existence, thing by thing. To say a name was to make that thing exist. To say the name of the dead was to keep them alive.

There is a story in a magical papyrus of the New Kingdom that shows how far this was taken. The sun god Ra, grown old, has a secret name that no one knows, and in the name lies his power. Isis, who wants that power, makes a snake out of a little earth mixed with Ra's own spittle and sets it in his path. The snake bites him. The god, who has never known pain, burns with the poison and cannot be healed. Isis offers to cure him if he will tell her his name, his true and hidden name, and at last, in agony, Ra gives it up. The poison leaves him. And Isis, who now holds his name, holds something of him too.

That was how the Egyptians understood names. They were handles. A name was a way of taking hold of a person, and so a name had to be guarded. The kings of Egypt wrote theirs inside a loop of rope, an oval with a bar at one end, which we call the cartouche, and which the Egyptians called the shen, the thing that encircles. The loop is a protective ring, the circuit of the sun, drawn around the king's name so that nothing could touch it. A name in a cartouche was a name behind a wall.

For ordinary people the walls were stone. Tomb owners carved their names on every surface they could: on the false door, the offering table, the statues, the lintels, the coffin, the shabti figures. Children were named after their grandfathers, so that a dead man's name would go on walking about the village. And the offering formula we met in the last part has a clause of its own about this, asking that the name of the dead be made to live, and that it be spoken aloud for as long as the tomb stood. The Egyptians could imagine nothing worse for a dead person than to be left without a name that anyone said.

Which is why they knew exactly how to murder one.

In the long reign of Thutmose III, some twenty years after the death of Hatshepsut, the stonecutters went to work on her monuments. Hatshepsut had ruled Egypt as king for more than two decades, and she had covered the land in her images and her cartouches. At her great temple at Deir el-Bahari, the carved figures of the king were hacked off the walls, leaving blank shapes in the stone. Her cartouches were chiselled out, and in some places replaced with the names of other kings. Her statues were pulled down, smashed, and thrown into pits near the temple. The man or men who gave the order knew what they were doing. They were not just altering a record. They were trying to make sure no one would ever say her name again, and that without the saying, she would not live.

It is hard to look at the blank spaces on those walls and not feel it. The shapes are still there, the outline of a body that was once carved into the stone and is now gone.

Akhenaten met a similar end. When his reign was over, the new capital was abandoned. His temples to the Aten were dismantled, and the carved blocks were reused as rubble in the foundations of other buildings. His name was cut from the monuments. When later kings had the royal lists carved at Abydos, listing each ruler by name, the list passes straight from the father of Akhenaten to a later king, as though the intervening years had not been. He is simply missing. The man who had once ordered the name of the god Amun hacked from temple walls had his own name hacked from the walls in turn.

That was the point of the erasure. It was a campaign against the dead. If a person's name was no longer spoken, the ren was dead, and a dead ren meant a dead person. No priest would pour a libation to a name that was not there. No stranger would pause at a blank wall and read.

And the Egyptians had a still more direct way. In the centuries after the Old Kingdom, a ritual developed in which the names of enemies, foreign rulers, rebels, and sometimes people closer to home, were written on clay figurines of bound prisoners or on red bowls. Then the figures and bowls were smashed and buried. Archaeologists have found hundreds of them, called execration figures, at Saqqara, Giza, and elsewhere. The writing and the breaking were meant to do one thing: to break the name, and by breaking the name, the person.

You may think of it as superstition. But look at what it assumed. It assumed that a person existed in the world as much through other people's mouths as through his own body. If the mouths fell silent, the person did too.

Here is the part I want to say plainly, and it is a reading of my own. The Egyptians built, over three thousand years, a vast machinery for keeping the dead present: names carved, formulas spoken, tombs visited, statues fed. And they built, alongside it, the opposite machinery: the chisel, the hammer, the pit. It was a war over memory, and it was fought with the same weapons on both sides. Think of how many people today exist only as a name on a screen, a name that someone else could take down. The Egyptians would have understood that fear at once. They knew that to be erased was to be killed, and that murder by chisel needed no blood.

But the name, for all its power, was an outer part. It hung in the air between people. The next part was inside the body, and it had never been outside, and on the day of judgment it would be made to speak for itself.

The dead man is whispering, and what he is whispering is a plea.

He has reached the hall. He stands before the scale, and the scale is about to take what is in his chest and lay it on a pan against a feather, and he knows what is in there. He knows every unkind word, every small theft, every lie he told his wife. So he does what a person does when he is terrified of the one person who knows all his secrets: he begs. And the person he begs is himself.

That is the ib, the heart, and it is the sixth of the seven parts.

To the Egyptians, the heart was not just a pump. It was the seat of thought, will, emotion and memory. It was where a person decided, and it was where a person remembered what he had decided. The brain, which Herodotus tells us was hooked out through the nose and thrown away, was a mass of nothing. The heart was the mind. Egyptian writings speak of a man's heart as his guide and his counselor, the thing that tells him what to do and, afterward, whether he did it well. They had two words for it: one for the heart as the organ in the chest, and one, the ib, for the heart as the self.

That is why it was left in the body. Every other organ could be taken out and set aside, but the heart stayed where it was, wrapped in linen on the chest. If, by accident, the embalmers removed it, they sewed it back. A person without a heart was a person without a self. And over it, in the wrappings, they laid a stone beetle, the heart scarab, usually carved from a dark green stone, with a spell cut into its flat underside.

That spell is number thirty B of the Book of the Dead, and it is the most unusual speech in the whole collection, because it is addressed to a part of the speaker. The dead man calls the heart his mother, the heart he had at his coming into being. He tells it not to stand up against him as a witness. He tells it not to oppose him in the tribunal, not to turn against him before the keeper of the scales. He tells it that it is his own ka within his body, the god Khnum who knit his limbs together, and then reminds it of this closeness, as one reminds a friend of long loyalty before asking for a favor. He asks it not to make his name stink before the gods. He asks it not to speak lies against him in the presence of the god.

Read it slowly and the hair rises on the back of the neck. The man knows that the heart is a witness, and he knows that it cannot be bribed with gold or silenced with force, so he talks to it as a man talks to a judge who happens to be a relative. He pleads. He flatters. And the Egyptians carved this speech on a stone, put the stone on his chest, and trusted it to do the work.

Raymond Faulkner's translation of the Book of the Dead, published in nineteen eighty five, gives the spell in plain English, and its flatness only sharpens the terror. A man is trying to persuade his own memory not to remember.

What the heart would testify to is spelled out in the next great text, spell one hundred twenty five, the spell for entering the Hall of the Two Truths. The dead man walks in and greets the god. He says he knows the names of the forty two judges who sit there, and he addresses each of them by name, and each name is stranger than the last. One is called Bone-breaker. Another is called Eater of Blood. To each, the dead man makes a declaration, and the declarations are a catalogue of everything a decent person was supposed to avoid.

I have not done wrong, he says. I have not robbed with violence. I have not stolen. I have not killed man or woman. I have not told lies. I have not diminished the food offerings in the temples. I have not taken the milk from the mouths of children. I have not driven cattle from their pastures. I have not trapped the birds of the gods. I have not polluted the water, and I have not stopped it from flowing in its season. I have not been deaf to words of truth. I have not made anyone weep. I have not been quick to anger. I have not listened at doors. I am pure, he says, and again, I am pure, and again.

It is a strange list. Some of it is what we would expect: murder, theft, lying. Some of it is strangely particular: the child's milk, the water, the birds. But all of it is about the same thing, Maat, the right order of the world. A sin, to the Egyptians, was a disturbance of that order: a theft that took from the balance, a lie that bent it. The dead man's declaration is his claim that he left the order as he found it.

Jan Assmann, whose book on Egyptian death and salvation appeared in two thousand five, reads this judgment as the place where Egyptian ethics and the afterlife are joined. A life lived according to Maat was the passport. It is a mistake to think the Egyptians cared only about magic. They cared about what a man did to his neighbor.

And yet the spell is also a formula, and the dead man says it whether he is innocent or not. That is what the scarab was for. The heart knew. It had been there for every unkind word, and it could not be fooled. So the dead man did the only thing he could, which was to talk to it, and to ask for mercy from the one witness who could not be argued with. In the papyrus of Ani, we see him do it. His heart sits in the pan, a small vase-shaped thing, quiet. It is the loneliest picture in the whole scroll.

The ib was a part of the person that could testify against him. The Egyptians did not think of conscience as a whisper inside the head. They thought of it as a witness, with a voice, in a court. And the court was about to open.

What the court would do with a heart that spoke against its owner is the question that hung over every Egyptian life. But to understand what was at stake, we need to see the best that could happen first, the part that comes at the end of the road for those who passed: the shining spirit, the akh.

Ani is plowing. In one scene near the end of his papyrus, the scribe stands behind a pair of oxen with the plow in his hands, the furrow turning behind him, in a green field beside a river that runs the way the Nile does. A little further on he is reaping. The grain stands taller than a man. The water is blue. There is no master, no overseer, no weariness, and the sun is warm.

That is the Field of Reeds, and it is where the justified dead went.

It was, in fact, Egypt again. The Egyptians pictured the afterlife as the best of what they already knew: a country of islands in a slow river, with fields of barley and emmer so tall and heavy that the stalks bowed under the grain. The dead farmed there as they had farmed in life. They reaped, they ate, they drank, they sat in the shade. The Book of the Dead has a whole spell, number one hundred ten, devoted to getting a person there, with the names of the gates, the ferryman, and the fields, and the answers a man must give on arrival.

The person who walked in the Field of Reeds was no longer simply a ka, a ba, a body and a shadow. He was something new. He was the akh.

The akh is the seventh of the parts, and the last. The word means something like the bright one, the effective one, the one that is transfigured. The Egyptians drew it as a crested ibis. It was the form the dead took when everything had gone right: when the body had been preserved and the Opening of the Mouth performed, when the ka was fed, the ba reunited with the body, the name spoken, the shadow free, and the heart weighed true. Then all the parts came together, and out of them a single shining being rose. It belonged to the sky and the stars and the light of the sun. The Pyramid Texts speak of the dead king as an akh who climbs to the horizon.

Jan Assmann's reading is useful here. The akh was not something a person had. It was something a person became, by ritual, by justification, by the care of the living. Nobody was born an akh. You had to be made one.

In the texts the dead man who has passed is called true of voice, which means that his words had been tested and had held. He was justified. And as an akh he was free of the one thing that had filled the afterlife with dread: he could go in and out. He could rest in his tomb or cross the sky, and he could feast and farm in the Field of Reeds.

But even in the Field of Reeds there was work, and the Egyptians, who knew what work was, found a way around it. In the Duat, the gods could call on the dead for labor, as the king could call on any Egyptian to dig a canal or haul stone. So the dead were buried with servants. Small figures, in wood or stone or glazed faience, made in the shape of a mummy and carried hoes and baskets in their hands, were placed in the tomb. These are the shabtis, the answerers. A spell, number six in the Book of the Dead, addresses the figure directly: if the dead man is summoned to do work in the realm of the dead, whether to make the fields arable, to flood the banks, or to carry sand from east to west, the shabti is to answer for him, and say, here I am.

The figures multiplied. In earlier tombs there might be one or two. By later periods some tombs held a figure for every day of the year, with overseers over the workers, so the dead man's fields were tilled by a small silent army and he himself lay back in the shade. It is a perfect Egyptian joke, and it carries a serious hope, that even the next world could be run by good management.

But the akh was not only a creature of the next world. The Egyptians believed that the akh could reach back across the line between death and life, and act on the living.

The evidence is a strange and moving group of documents known as the Letters to the Dead. They are written on pottery bowls, on linen, on jars, in the same cursive script the living used to write to each other, and they are addressed to people who have died. They were placed at the tomb, often on offering bowls, so that the dead would read the words while eating the food. The Qau bowl is one example, a clay vessel inscribed in ink and left at a cemetery. Others come from the centuries after the pyramid age and down into the Late Period.

The writers are ordinary people with ordinary troubles. They write because a child is sick, because a lawsuit is going wrong, because they are childless and want a son, because a servant is cheating them, because they have had a nightmare. And they ask the dead to do what the dead can do from where they are: to intercede with the gods, to stand in court before the great tribunal and plead their case, to chase away a hostile spirit. In one such letter a widower, who has tended his wife's tomb faithfully, asks her what he could possibly have done to deserve her torment. The dead, in other words, could also be a nuisance. An akh could send illness, nightmares and misfortune to the household, if it was angry, or neglected, or wronged. The letters read like a family quarrel carried on across the grave. Please, they say, you were good to us in life. Be good to us now. Fight for us over there. And stop whatever it is you are doing to us here.

That is the akh at its fullest: a dead person who was still a member of the family, still owed bread and beer, still able to help and still able to harm. The Egyptian dead did not drift away into a cloud. They stayed close, and they stayed involved.

But every one of those letters rests on one assumption. The dead person had passed. The heart had been weighed, and the scale had held, and the ka, the ba, the shadow and the name had come back together into one shining spirit. The akh was what you became if the verdict went your way.

Which brings us back to the hall, to the balance, and to the creature crouched beside it. Because the Egyptians knew there was another verdict.

The plumb is still swinging.

We left Ani standing in the Hall of the Two Truths with his arms raised and his heart in a pan, and the beam of the balance trembling, and the whole hall waiting. Anubis is on one knee at the scale. Thoth has his reed pen lifted over the palette. And beside the post, with her jaws a little open, Ammit is watching the pan that holds the heart.

Now you know what is in that pan. It is not just an organ. It is the ib, the self, the witness that remembers every unkind word and every small theft. And you know what is riding on it: the ka that must be fed, the ba that must fly home at night, the shadow, the name, the body lying in its wrappings far away in a tomb at Thebes. All seven parts are waiting on one beam of wood and one feather.

So here is the question the papyrus never quite answers in paint. What happened if the heart was heavier?

The texts do not linger on it. That is part of what makes it frightening. The Egyptians wrote spell after spell to make sure it would never happen, and almost nothing about the thing itself, the way people will speak for an hour about how to avoid a disease and not once say its name. But the pieces are there, in the vignettes and the captions and the spells, and when they are set side by side the answer is plain.

Look again at Ammit. In the Papyrus of Ani a short line of hieroglyphs is written above her, giving her name and her nature. Wallis Budge, who translated it in eighteen ninety five, called her the Devourer of the Dead. Her front is a crocodile, her middle is a lion, her hind part is a hippopotamus. She has no other job in the whole of Egyptian religion. She does not guard a gate. She does not punish. She does not torment anyone for eternity. She eats. And what she waits to eat is the heart in the pan.

In the papyrus of Hunefer, a royal scribe of the same age as Ani, she is there again, crouched on a little stand beside the balance, her eyes fixed on the scale. Across dozens of Books of the Dead the same creature appears in the same place, in the same posture. She is not an ornament. She is the alternative.

If the heart was heavy, if it testified against its owner, if the forty two declarations of innocence turned out to be forty two lies, then the heart did not go back into the chest. It went to Ammit. And the moment it was eaten, everything built on it fell.

Think about what that means, part by part, the way the Egyptians would have.

The heart was the self. Without it there was no one left to remember, to decide, to be justified. The ka would come to the false door of the tomb looking for its bread and beer, and there would be no person for it to belong to. The ba, the bird with the human face, would have no body to fly home to at dusk. The shadow would have no one to follow. The name, the ren, could go on being carved on the walls of a tomb, but it would be the name of nobody. And the akh, the shining spirit, would never be born at all. The seven parts would not be punished. They would simply come apart, and stop.

That is the fate I promised at the beginning. It is not hell. The Egyptians had no hell in the later Christian sense. Their underworld books do show pits of fire for the enemies of the sun god, but those fires were for destroying, not for keeping a sinner alive inside them for ever, still feeling it. What they feared was worse than pain, because pain at least requires someone to feel it. They feared ceasing to exist. Not dying, because death was only a door, and the whole of Egyptian religion was built to walk people through it. They feared dying a second time, on the far side of the door, in the place where there was no further door to go through.

And they had a phrase for it. It appears in the Coffin Texts, painted inside the wooden coffins of the Middle Kingdom, and it appears again in the Book of the Dead: not to die again in the realm of the dead. Spell forty four of the Book of the Dead is titled with exactly that wish, a spell for not dying a second time. Other spells ask that the dead man's heart not be taken from him, that his name not be forgotten, that he not be made to walk upside down or eat filth in the Duat, that he not be destroyed. Read them together and you hear a whole civilization saying one thing over and over. Let me not be unmade.

That is why the heart scarab carried its plea. That is why the forty two judges were named and answered one by one. That is why Ani paid for a roll of papyrus more than twenty meters long, painted in colour by the best artists in Thebes. Every line of it was insurance against Ammit.

Here is the boldest reading of the whole tradition, and I will put it plainly. The Egyptians did not invent the afterlife to comfort themselves. They invented annihilation. Every other people around them imagined the dead going on somewhere, dimly, in a house of dust. The Egyptians were the first to imagine that a person could be erased, completely and for ever, and then they spent three thousand years building the most elaborate machinery in the ancient world to make sure it never happened to them.

And now look at the scale one last time.

In the Papyrus of Ani, the beam does not tip. The plumb comes to rest, hanging straight down the post, and the two pans hang level, heart and feather in perfect balance. Anubis reports that the weighing is true. Thoth writes it down: the heart of the Osiris Ani has been weighed, and it has been found righteous, and there is no wickedness in him. The gods declare him true of voice. Ammit does not move.

In the next scene, Horus, the falcon-headed son of Osiris, takes Ani by the hand and leads him forward, past the judges, to the throne. Ani kneels before Osiris with offerings piled at his feet, and he is presented as one who has been found true.

It always goes that way. In every Book of the Dead that shows the weighing, the scale is level and the dead person walks on. Ammit is painted waiting, and she is never painted eating. Nobody would pay a scribe to paint his own annihilation. The papyrus was not a record of a trial. It was the verdict, written in advance, rolled up and laid in the coffin, so that when the real moment came the outcome would already exist in ink.

That is the last secret of the seven parts. The Egyptians did not leave the second death to chance. They painted their way past it.

For about three thousand years the roll lay in a tomb near Thebes. Then, in the winter of eighteen eighty eight, it was bought.

The buyer was Wallis Budge, a young curator from the British Museum, sent to Egypt to acquire antiquities. He came back to London with the Papyrus of Ani, one of the longest and finest Books of the Dead ever found. And to make it easier to study and to display, the roll was cut into sheets and mounted under glass. The papyrus that had been made to stay whole for eternity was sliced into panels so that visitors could walk past it. You can still see it in the British Museum today. Ani is still standing at the scale, behind glass, in London.

Budge published the papyrus with his translation in eighteen ninety five, and for a long time his was the version most of the world read. He did not always understand the parts of the person the way later scholars would. He often translated the ba as soul and the ka as double, and those English words carried a great deal of Christian freight that the Egyptians never put there.

Over the next century Egyptologists slowly took that freight off. Louis Zabkar, in his study of the ba published in nineteen sixty eight, argued that the ba was not a soul at all in our sense. It was not a piece of the person that left the body and lived on in its place. It was the person himself, in another mode of being, the dead man as he could move and act and appear. Raymond Faulkner's translation of the Book of the Dead, in nineteen eighty five, gave English readers the spells in plain, careful language. John Taylor, at the British Museum, set out the whole system of belief in his book on death and the afterlife in two thousand one, and in two thousand ten led an exhibition there called Journey Through the Afterlife, built around the Books of the Dead in the museum's own collection. And Jan Assmann argued that an Egyptian person was a constellation, a gathering of parts held together in life by the body and in death by ritual, by the care of the living, and by the judgment.

The scholars still do not agree on everything. How many parts there were depends on which text you read and which century it comes from. The list of seven is a gathering of the most important, not a creed. Some of the parts mattered more in one age than another, and the ideas changed over three thousand years, as any people's ideas do. But on the shape of the thing they agree. There was no single soul. There was a person made of many pieces, and the pieces had to be kept together.

And that tells us what the Egyptians were really afraid of. Not death. They had made peace with death, and more than peace: they had turned it into a journey with a map, a ferryman, and a field of barley at the end. What they feared was coming apart. Being forgotten. A name chiselled off a wall. A tomb with no one to bring bread to its door. A heart that spoke against its owner. Every part of the person was a way of holding on, and every spell was a hand reaching out to keep the pieces from drifting away.

Perhaps that is why the picture of the weighing still stops people in a museum hall more than three thousand years later. It is not a picture of punishment. It is a picture of a man finding out whether he will be held together.

So go back one last time to the Hall of the Two Truths.

The lamps are low. The forty two judges sit in their rows. Osiris waits on his throne with the crook and the flail. Tutu stands beside her husband. Anubis kneels at the post, and Thoth holds his pen, and Ammit crouches with her jaws a little open.

Ani's heart lies in one pan. The feather of Maat stands in the other.

The beam trembles. The plumb swings, and slows, and hangs straight.

The scale is level.

Ani walks on.
