Full script

The Shams al-Ma'arif, the Sun of Knowledge, is a book of letters, numbers, the names of God and squares of figures. Across much of the Muslim world it has long had the name of a book that should not be kept in the house. What is inside it is stranger, and far more orderly, than that reputation suggests.

Start with the smallest thing in its pages. It is a square of three rows of three cells. In the top row stand four, nine and two. In the middle row, three, five and seven. In the bottom row, eight, one and six. Add any row, any column, or either diagonal, and the answer is fifteen. The book does not treat such an arrangement as a pastime. It treats it as a pattern in which number, letter and divine name are held in balance. It directs its readers to write squares like this at chosen hours, in a chosen state of purity, for protection, for healing and for the easing of trouble. Beside it stand larger squares of four, five, six and more. There is a table of the ninety-nine Beautiful Names of God, and an account of which planet rules which hour.

The reputation is easy to state. Many Muslims have held that a copy in the house invites trouble, and that the book is too strong for anyone who has not prepared for it. Where that belief comes from, and how far it is justified, is part of what you will learn.

I will tell you at the start how the road runs. The book is attributed to Ahmad al-Buni, a Sufi teacher from the Algerian coast who taught in Cairo and died there in the early thirteenth century. So we begin with the man. After him come seven teachings, in the order a student would have met them.

The first is the science of letters, which is the study of the Arabic alphabet, the number each letter carries and the nature each letter was given. It is the grammar of everything else. The second is the Beautiful Names and the long discipline of reciting them. The third is the squares. The fourth is the sky, with its planets, its hours and the twenty-eight mansions of the Moon. The fifth is the angels. The sixth is the kings of the jinn, the unseen beings that Islamic belief places beside humankind, and the oaths by which the tradition says they were addressed. The seventh is different in kind. It is the argument: the long quarrel among Muslim scholars over whether a book like this is devotion or sorcery. You will hear both sides.

By the end you will know what the book teaches, in what order, and what it meant to the people who copied it, wore it and condemned it. One thing I am keeping for last, because it changes how every earlier part is heard. The famous book, the one printed and sold today under the name Shams al-Ma'arif, is not quite the book its author wrote. You will understand that better once you have seen what is in it.

A word on how this will be told. The book sits inside Islamic history, not outside it. Its author was a devout man and its subject is the names of God. It has been defended by learned Muslims and condemned by other learned Muslims, and I will tell it that way: what the book says, what its users did, and what its critics answered. We begin, then, with the man from Buna.

Ahmad al-Buni lies in the Qarafa, the great cemetery that spreads beneath the Muqattam hills on the edge of Cairo, where the city buried its scholars, its judges and its saints for centuries. His death is placed in six hundred twenty-two of the Hijra, which is twelve twenty-five, or in the few years after. The notices of his life are brief and were written by later men, and they do not agree to the year. That is nearly all the biographers hold firmly. It is worth knowing how little there is, because an enormous book now stands on top of it.

His name tells you where he began. Buna was a port town on the coast of what is now eastern Algeria, in the land the Arabs called Ifriqiya, and today it is called Annaba. It lay a short way from the ruins of Hippo Regius, where Augustine had been bishop some eight centuries earlier. The old Roman city had faded, and the Arab town had grown up beside it. In al-Buni's lifetime this was the western edge of the Islamic world, a land of its own schools of law, its own Sufi lodges and its own devotion to the Arabic language. He was formed there, in the later twelfth century, and then he travelled east.

He settled in Egypt. Cairo was then ruled by the Ayyubids, the family of Saladin, and it was a city of colleges and Sufi lodges. Saladin had turned a former palace into a lodge for Sufis, the Sa'id al-Su'ada, in eleven seventy-three, and scholars from the west who came east found patrons and listeners. Al-Buni was one of them. He taught in Cairo until his death.

He was a contemporary of Ibn Arabi, who was born in Murcia in Spain in eleven sixty-five and died in Damascus in twelve forty. The two men came from the same western world of Sufism, and both lived their mature years in the east. I do not say they were friends, because no good record says so. I say that they breathed the same air. Ibn Arabi wrote at enormous length about the letters of the alphabet as the structure of reality, and al-Buni's teaching on letters belongs to the same current. When you meet the science of letters in the next part, you are meeting an idea that was alive among the best Sufi minds of the age, not a fringe curiosity.

Al-Buni taught small circles. A Sufi master in his day had a few disciples around him, and teaching was personal. Knowledge passed from a teacher to a student who had been tested, and the student received permission to hand it on. His writings were composed for such circles. They address initiates, they warn the reader to guard what he learns, and they assume a reader who is already living a devout life. His securely authentic works include the Lata'if al-Isharat, the Subtle Indications, a treatise on the letters. You will hear its name again, because it stands closer to the man himself than the famous book does.

Now the idea his teaching starts from. You need to hold three principles, and the rest will rest on them.

The first is that God created by speech. In the Islamic understanding, God brought the world into being by His command, and the world is therefore, in a sense, spoken. The second follows from it. Speech in Arabic is made of letters, and the divine names are made of letters, so the letters of the Arabic alphabet and the names of God are the fabric from which the world is woven. A person who understood the letters would understand something of how creation is ordered. The third principle is the one al-Buni's readers would have put first. Such knowledge is a path of devotion before it is anything else. The student did not approach the letters as a craftsman approaches a tool. He approached them as a servant approaches the words of his Lord, with fasting, purity and a sincere intention, and whatever power the letters had was understood to belong to God and to be given or withheld by Him.

Notice what this does to the book's later reputation. A reader who meets the squares and the spirits first, without these three principles, may think he has found a manual of tricks. A reader who meets them in al-Buni's order meets a Sufi's theory of language, and the practices are its consequences.

So the teaching begins where the world begins, with the letters. The first thing a student learned was that each of them is also a number.

A lesson in the science of letters might have begun with a single word spoken by the teacher and a single question: what is its number? Every student in the circle was expected to answer without writing anything down. To see how, you have to learn the system the way they learned it.

The Arabic alphabet has twenty-eight letters. In the system called abjad, named for the first four letters in the old order, alif, ba, jim and dal, each letter carries a number. The first nine letters are the numbers one to nine. The next nine are the tens, from ten up to ninety. The next nine are the hundreds, from one hundred up to nine hundred. The last letter, ghayn, stands for one thousand. Because every word is made of letters, every word has a sum, and so does every name.

Here is the name Allah, worked as a student was taught to work it. It is written with four letters: alif, lam, lam and ha. Alif is one. Lam is thirty, and there are two of them, which makes sixty. Ha is five. Together they come to sixty-six. That number then belonged to the name, and a master of the science could do things with it. He could compare it with the numbers of other names, fold it down to a smaller number by adding its digits, or use it to decide how many times a recitation should be repeated. Numbers drawn from words in this way were treated as a kind of second voice of the word.

One complication is worth knowing, because it explains something you will meet later. In the western Islamic lands, the Maghrib, the order and values of a few letters differed from those used in the east. Al-Buni was a man of the west who taught in the east. Two copyists, one trained in each tradition, could therefore reckon the same name differently. When the numbers in two manuscripts do not agree, the cause is sometimes no more sinister than that.

The second layer of the system concerns nature. The twenty-eight letters were divided among the four elements, seven to each of fire, air, water and earth. The division ran in rotation through the alphabet. Alif, the first letter, belonged to fire. Ba, the second, belonged to air. Jim, the third, belonged to water. Dal, the fourth, belonged to earth. Then the cycle began again with the fifth letter, and so on to the end. From the elements came the familiar qualities of the physician's world. Fire is hot and dry, air is hot and moist, water is cold and moist, and earth is cold and dry. So each letter was hot or cold, dry or moist, and a word made of letters had a temperament that could be read from its spelling.

The logic here was a physician's logic. In the medicine of the time, illness was an imbalance of qualities, and the remedy was something of the opposite nature. The tradition of the letters applied the same thought to names and requests. A condition thought to be too cold called for letters that were warm, and a name whose letters balanced the trouble was the name to pray with. How strictly the book applied this varies from chapter to chapter, but the habit of thought is everywhere.

Now the strangest and most honoured part of the system, the detached letters. Twenty-nine chapters of the Qur'an open with single letters, spoken separately: the Muslim tradition counts fourteen different ones among them, half the alphabet. Sometimes there is a single letter, sometimes a group of three, four or five. Muslim commentators gave many explanations of what they mean, and a large body of them held that their meaning is known to God alone. The science of letters took them as its warrant. If God Himself opened parts of His book with letters standing alone, then letters had a standing of their own, beyond their work in making words. Teachers of the science treated these letters with great reverence and wove them into their treatises on the grounds of that standing.

Put the three layers together and you see what a student was acquiring: a way of tying any word or name to a number, to an element, and to a quality, so that the whole language became a web of correspondences. The planets and the zodiac were soon added to the web, as you will see in Part Five.

I would put one thing more boldly than the scholars would. The system is the same kind of act as the Muslim sciences of grammar and law: an attempt to find the regular order inside something given. Its extra step was to believe that the order was not only in the meaning of the words but in their very shapes and sums.

What the system was for is the subject of the next teaching. The student who could number a name could be sent to recite it.

A disciple in a Cairo lodge in the early thirteenth century might have been given a single name of God and told that it was his work for the coming weeks. The name might be the Opener, for a man whose way seemed shut. It might be the Provider, for one in need of livelihood, or the Forgiving, for one in need of repentance. This was the second teaching, and it was the heart of devotional practice in al-Buni's circle.

The Beautiful Names are the names by which God is called in the Islamic tradition. The usual count is ninety-nine. They name qualities: the Merciful, the Compassionate, the Provider, the Opener, the Subtle, the Loving, the Forgiving, the Just, and so on down the list. The book assigns to each name a quality and a kind of petition for which it was thought fitting. The Opener was invoked when a door of any kind had closed. The Provider was invoked in want. The Subtle, whose meaning includes gentleness and fine understanding, was invoked in a hard matter that needed an easy resolution. The principle is one that devout Muslims will recognise. To ask for something is to ask by the name that fits it.

Here the numbers of the first teaching came into play. The book directs the reciter to take a name's number from its letters and to repeat the name that many times, or a multiple of that number. Al-Latif, the Subtle, is an example: without the article, its letters add to one hundred and twenty-nine, so a reciter who took the number from the letters would have said the name that many times in a sitting. Each repetition was a dhikr. That word means remembrance, and it names the central practice of Sufism, the repeated recitation of God's name or of a phrase in His praise, with the whole attention fixed on Him.

For the serious student there was the retreat, called khalwa. It was the discipline in which Sufis shut themselves away from the world for a fixed period, often forty days, though shorter ones are described. The picture the books give is of a small, closed room, kept dark or dim. The man in retreat fasted by day and ate little at night. He kept himself in ritual purity, with ablutions before prayer and between recitations. He kept the obligatory prayers and added to them. He spoke to no one but his teacher and he slept little. And he recited, hour after hour, the name or names that had been assigned to him, in the number his teacher had fixed.

What did he hope for? Here the sources speak in the language of Sufism. The retreat was meant to empty the heart of everything except God. In the course of it, the Sufi writers say, the retreatant might receive dreams or visions or a sense of opening, and these were to be reported to the teacher and weighed by him, not trusted by themselves. Many Sufi masters were careful to warn that wonders were a distraction and that the goal was nearness to God, not marvels. That warning appears again and again in the literature, and it was addressed to the very kind of student who was drawn to books like this.

Now the idea that crowns the teaching, the Greatest Name. The Islamic tradition holds that among the names of God there is one that is greatest, and that whoever asks by it is answered. Muslim scholars never agreed on what it is. Some said it was the name Allah itself, some said it was one of the Beautiful Names, and some said that it was never made known and could only be given by God to those He chose. The book treats the Greatest Name as the object of its whole discipline. The long recitations, the fasting and the calculations were meant, among their other purposes, to bring the practitioner nearer to a prayer that could not be refused. This is the meaning of a phrase you will meet again in Part Six, the seal of the Greatest Name.

It is important to be exact here. For al-Buni and his readers this teaching was worship. The names were God's, the petition was made to God, and the power of the names was God's power, held by no man. A reciter did not command a name. He asked by it. The book's whole structure of numbers and hours was understood as a way of making the asking more fitting and more sincere, never as a way of compelling the answer. Whatever later readers made of the book, the first teaching about the names in this tradition is a teaching about prayer.

Notice, too, where it leaves the student. A man who has spent forty days in recitation has learned the names by living in them. Such a man was ready for the next teaching, in which the names and letters were not only spoken but written, and arranged in figures. The most famous of those is a square.

Some hundred years before al-Buni, the great theologian al-Ghazali, who died in eleven eleven, wrote his short autobiography of the mind, the Deliverer from Error. In it he discusses the strange properties of things, powers that reason cannot derive but experience confirms. As one example he mentions a figure of numbers written for a woman in a hard labour, to ease it. Later readers recognised it as this same square of three rows of three, the one you met in the opening. A scholar of the first rank was putting such a figure in the middle of a serious argument, without embarrassment, a century before al-Buni taught in Cairo.

That is the third teaching, and the one the book is best known for. The square is called a wafq in Arabic, a word that means a harmonious arrangement. The simplest is the three-by-three square, with five at its centre. Its top row reads four, nine, two. The middle row reads three, five, seven. The bottom row reads eight, one, six. Every row, every column and both diagonals add to fifteen, and the whole square, the numbers one to nine, adds to forty-five. If you look at the centre you will notice that every pair of cells across it adds to ten.

The four corners hold the even numbers: four, two, eight and six. In the abjad system these are the letters dal, ba, ha and waw, and when read together they make a word, buduh. The square is called the buduh square for that reason, and the word buduh was itself written as a charm beside it. In the tradition, then, this is a square that is also a word, and a word that is also a number. That is the whole science of letters folded into nine cells.

The squares did not begin as amulets. Mathematicians of the ninth and tenth centuries had studied how to build them, as a branch of number theory. By al-Ghazali's day they were being written for the sick, and the science of letters built a system on top of them. Larger squares were made, of four rows, five, six, seven, eight and nine. In the usual scheme, each was matched to a planet, and each had its own total in every line. The three-by-three belonged to Saturn and made fifteen. The four-by-four belonged to Jupiter and made thirty-four. The five-by-five belonged to Mars and made sixty-five. The six-by-six belonged to the Sun and made one hundred and eleven. The seven-by-seven belonged to Venus and made one hundred and seventy-five. The eight-by-eight belonged to Mercury and made two hundred and sixty. The nine-by-nine belonged to the Moon and made three hundred and sixty-nine. You will notice that the number of cells rises with each planet in the order of their heavenly speed, from the slowest to the fastest. Each planet was also tied to a metal, so that a square might be engraved on the metal of the planet it served: lead for Saturn, tin for Jupiter, iron for Mars, gold for the Sun, copper for Venus, silver for the Moon.

Then the tradition went further and built squares for particular names of God. The method started from the number of the name. From that number the maker worked out a square whose lines gave the name's total, or whose cells held the letters of the name in place of figures. The result was a figure that was at once a name, a number and a pattern, and the more cleverly it was constructed, the more it was prized. The books give long rules for doing this, and they are the part of the tradition that comes closest to mathematics.

How was a square used? The book directs the maker to write it with care. The material might be paper, parchment, or a plate of the proper metal. The ink was often perfumed with saffron and rose water. The hour was chosen from the planet's rule over the day, as Part Five explains. The maker was in ritual purity, had made his ablutions, and generally burned incense, and the writing was begun and ended with the name of God. When it was finished, the square might be folded into a small case of leather and worn on the body, or kept under a pillow, or set at a threshold. Another practice the tradition records is writing a figure on a bowl, washing it off with water, and drinking the water, a custom widely attested in Islamic healing.

I will make one bold claim here, and you should weigh it as a reading, not as settled fact. The square was the book's whole outlook made visible. It said that the order of God's creation could be set down in number, and that a person who wrote it down in purity and in His name was bringing a little of that order to a place where there was trouble. Whether any particular square worked was something only its user could say. That it was a statement of confidence in divine order is something the structure itself shows.

Squares were fixed to times, and times belong to the heavens. So the fourth teaching leaves the page and turns to the sky.

A practitioner following the chapters of the book would have waited for the right hour, and the right hour was counted from sunrise. Everything in this part of the tradition begins with the fact that time is not empty. It is a succession of rulers.

Seven planets rule the seven days. The old astronomers counted the Sun and the Moon among the planets, with Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn. Sunday belongs to the Sun, Monday to the Moon, Tuesday to Mars, Wednesday to Mercury, Thursday to Jupiter, Friday to Venus and Saturday to Saturn. The astrologers placed Friday under Venus, though for Muslims it is first the day of the congregational prayer.

Each day and each night is then divided into twelve hours, from sunrise to sunset and from sunset to the next sunrise. Because these hours are measured by the sun, they are unequal. A summer day-hour is long and a winter day-hour is short. The first hour of a day belongs to the day's own planet. The hours after it pass through the planets in a fixed order: Saturn, Jupiter, Mars, the Sun, Venus, Mercury and the Moon, and then round again. On a Friday, for example, the first hour after sunrise belongs to Venus, the second to Mercury, the third to the Moon, the fourth to Saturn, and so on through the day. A person who wanted to write a Venus square needed to find a Venus hour, and a person who wanted a Mars square needed a Mars hour. This is why so many instructions in the tradition begin with a calendar.

The second great scheme is the mansions of the Moon. The Moon moves through the sky in a little more than twenty-seven days, and the Arabs, long before Islam, had marked out its path by twenty-eight groups of stars, one for each night. The first is called al-Sharatan, the two horns of the Ram. The tradition of the letters made one more pairing, and I think it the most beautiful in the whole book. Each mansion was matched with one of the twenty-eight letters. As the Moon passed from mansion to mansion, it passed from letter to letter, so that a month in the sky was an alphabet being recited. The same twenty-eight that organised the language also organised the night.

What work did the book assign to which mansion and hour? Here I will be careful. The lists differ between manuscripts, and I will not give you a particular mansion for a particular purpose from memory. The general pattern is clear. Mansions and hours were sorted into the favourable and the unfavourable. Some were held to suit beginning a journey, building, seeking the goodwill of a ruler, healing, or reconciling people. Others were thought to suit separation and harm, and the astrological literature as a whole, outside al-Buni's circle, does record works of that kind. A practitioner's first task was to know which hour he was in.

None of this was peculiar to al-Buni's book. The sky-lore of the Shams is part of a shared inheritance, drawn from the astrologers' books of the same centuries. The most famous of them is the Ghayat al-Hakim, the Goal of the Sage, written in Arabic in al-Andalus in the tenth century. It was translated into Castilian at the court of Alfonso the Tenth of Castile around twelve fifty-six, and later into Latin, where it became known as the Picatrix. It also teaches the planetary hours, the mansions of the Moon, and the making of talismans under the stars. Liana Saif's study of the two traditions, whose title runs from the Ghayat al-Hakim to the Shams al-Ma'arif, follows how the older astral learning flowed into the later letter-learning, and how the two differ in their ways of seeing the cosmos.

They did differ, and the difference is the point. The astrologers' books worked by drawing the powers of the planets into an image. Al-Buni's tradition looked to the names of God and the letters of speech, and it used the planets chiefly as a calendar of fitting times. A reader who knew both would have recognised the same hours, the same metals and the same squares, set to different music.

There is one more difference of fate. The Ghayat al-Hakim crossed into Latin Europe and became a famous book of Western magic. The Shams never did. It stayed inside the Muslim world, and in a great many languages it was read and copied among people to whom its names of God were not strange.

The planets and the hours gave the practitioner his time. They did not give him anyone to address, and the next two teachings are about the beings the tradition set over each day.

The seven kings of the jinn belong to the part of the tradition that earned the book its darkest name. Their names are written in the lists of the wider tradition that gathers under al-Buni's name. Maymun, Burqan, Shamhurish and Murrah are among the names given in those lists, and each of the seven days of the week was assigned its king, with the hosts that served him. I cannot tell you with certainty that every list stands in the Shams itself. The honest statement is that they belong to the wider tradition collected under al-Buni's name, and Part Eight will explain why that distinction matters.

First, the jinn. In Islamic belief, the jinn are beings created by God before humankind and living alongside it. They are unseen, they have free will, and they are divided, like humans, into believers and unbelievers. The Qur'an speaks of them often, and one of its chapters bears their name. Muslim law and theology treated them as real and discussed what could be asked of them. Islamic tradition, following the Qur'anic account, holds that Solomon was granted authority over the jinn by God. That story is the pattern behind the whole literature. It says that command over such beings is possible, but only by a gift from God, and never by a man's own strength.

So the fifth teaching concerns the angels. The tradition lists angels set over each day and each planet, ranks of servants of God, with the great angels at their head. The book directs the practitioner to know their names and to call on God by the names of the angels who served the day. Here the angels are less addressed than named, as part of an appeal to God. They were guardians of the time.

The sixth teaching concerns the kings of the jinn. Each king ruled over a host, and in the tradition each was linked to his own day. The practitioner who dealt with them was working in a state the books describe as one of purity: ablution, fasting, a clean place, incense, garments kept clean, and a mind turned to God. The seal of the Greatest Name, a series of seven signs, belongs here. The books describe it as seven figures written in a row. They look nothing like the letters of the alphabet, being strokes and small loops, and the tradition gave them a place of honour as a kind of seal of authority.

The tool of this teaching was the oath. The tradition calls it an adjuration, a long formal address in which the practitioner called on a being in the name of God. The texts are long. They pile name upon name, and in them the practitioner invoked God by His names, by His angels, by His books and by His prophets. In close paraphrase, such an oath summoned the king to appear, to submit, to do no harm, and to carry out what was asked of him, and it threatened him, if he refused, with the power of the names invoked against him. The practitioner did not claim authority for himself. The structure of the oath is that of a petitioner who stands behind a higher authority: I speak, but by the name of God, and you are bidden by that name.

That is how the users understood it. Command was exercised through the names of God, by a person in a state of purity, and the person was only the voice. It is an idea you can trace back to the story of Solomon. It is also the idea that made the critics most uneasy. Many of the oaths contain strange or unintelligible words, and critics asked how a man could be sure that such words were truly the names of God and not something else. You will meet that objection in the next part.

It is here, I think, that the popular reputation of the book was made. Stories told of readers who tried these oaths without preparation, and of copies that brought trouble to a house. These tales are the likely source of the idea that the Shams was the most feared book of its kind in the Muslim world, a description of its popular standing that does not need to be believed to be understood. A book that deals with unseen beings and with the greatest of names invites a certain fear in anyone who takes its claims seriously, and its being taken seriously is the point.

Whether the book's claims were true is not a question history can answer. What history can answer is how learned men responded, and some of them responded with alarm. The seventh teaching is the argument.

Late in the fourteenth century a North African scholar sat down to explain the sciences. Ibn Khaldun was a Tunisian by birth, from a family that had come from Seville. He finished the Muqaddimah, his great introduction to history, in thirteen seventy-seven, and he spent the last decades of his life in Cairo, teaching, serving as a judge and revising his book. In it he describes the science of letters. He names al-Buni among those who wrote on it, and he describes the way its practitioners assigned natures to letters, drew up squares and used the names. His judgement is plain. He took the science to be a form of sorcery, though its practitioners clothed it in the language of piety.

You can hear the force of the charge. Ibn Khaldun is not mocking the learning. He is a careful historian describing a practice he knows well, and he is saying that its effects, if there are any, belong to the family of magic, and that the attachment of divine names and Sufi devotion to it does not change its nature. A man in Cairo reading the Muqaddimah in thirteen eighty could have taken it as the verdict of the age's best mind.

Half a century earlier, and over in Damascus, the jurist Ibn Taymiyya, who died in thirteen twenty-eight, had condemned practices of this kind more sweepingly. He held to a strict principle: worship and petition must be limited to what the revealed law permits, and anything that appeals to unseen beings, uses words nobody can understand, or claims to compel what only God can grant, falls outside it. On that principle, the oaths of the seven kings and the writing of numbers and characters for effect were forbidden, and he said so. He wrote in a time when such practices were common and he wrote to stop them.

Now the other side, for it was not a small one. The Mamluks, who ruled Egypt and Syria from twelve fifty to fifteen seventeen, were soldiers who had been brought up as slaves and who became the sultans and emirs of the age, and they presided over a great flourishing of scholarship. Noah Gardiner's study of al-Buni's readers through the Mamluk period shows that his works were copied and read by learned men in these courts and cities, not only by obscure mystics. Under the Ottomans, who ruled the Arab lands from fifteen seventeen, the copying continued. Ottoman collections still hold talismanic shirts covered in verses, divine names and squares, worn by men of rank. Learned people did not regard this as a disgrace.

How did the defenders answer the critics? They drew a line, and you can state it simply. The names of God and the verses of the Qur'an are not appeals to any other power. A person who writes the Throne Verse for protection, or the Opening for healing, or one of the Beautiful Names for a need, is asking God by His own words. The letters are God's creation, the book is God's book, and the asking is directed to God alone. Writing the words in a fitting place, at a fitting hour, in a state of purity, was an act of respect, not of rebellion. Many scholars who would never have gone so far as the seven kings accepted that Qur'anic writing for healing had a place in devotion, and defended al-Buni's learning on that ground.

The critics drew their line elsewhere. For them, the moment the practice reached for something other than a plain appeal to God, the line was crossed. Strange names with no meaning that anyone could verify, oaths that commanded rather than asked, and the calling up of jinn went beyond what the law allowed, whatever pious words were wrapped around them. And the claim that numbers and letters had efficacy in themselves, apart from God's will, seemed to critics a dangerous confusion of creation with Creator. Their position was not that the letters were meaningless. It was that a Muslim has been given what he needs for worship, and that the rest is a road with a known destination.

I would like you to hold both lines in mind. The defenders were not cynics hiding sorcery behind piety, and the critics were not enemies of learning. They were arguing, in good faith and at the highest level, about where the boundary lies between asking and commanding, between devotion and technique. The book sat on that boundary, and for most of its history it has been read from one side or the other.

This much is the argument. But all this while, I have been speaking of the book as though it were one thing. It was not, and the next part tells what it was.

Noah Gardiner counted the manuscripts. He studied the surviving copies of al-Buni's works, hundreds of them, scattered through libraries across the world. What he found changes how you must hear everything I have told you.

The works that can be traced to al-Buni himself circulated in a limited way, among small circles of initiates, much as the man himself had taught. They are the Lata'if al-Isharat and others like it, and they deal with the letters, the names and the discipline of the Sufi. They are shorter than the famous book and quieter in tone.

The huge book now printed as the Shams al-Ma'arif al-Kubra, the Great Sun of Knowledge, is not that. It is a vast compilation in forty chapters, and Gardiner's work indicates that it was assembled long after al-Buni's death, probably in the early seventeenth century. Its compilers drew on his writings and on a great deal of later material. The evidence is in the chains of teachers. Such chains were the way Sufi and scholarly knowledge was authenticated: a list of the masters through whom a text had come down. In the large book, some of the chains name men who lived after al-Buni. A man who died in twelve twenty-five could not have written the names of men born after him. The book therefore contains the work of other hands, and that is how the scholars know it.

Think what that means for what I have told you. The teaching on the letters and the names, with the discipline of the retreat, belongs closest to the man. The squares and the astral hours belong to an inheritance far older than he was, which he and others passed along. And the long lists of the seven kings and the great oaths, as I said in Part Six, belong to the wider tradition that gathered under his name in the centuries after he died. The book grew by accretion, with each generation of copyists and compilers adding what they thought belonged. Jean-Charles Coulon's survey of magic in the Islamic lands in the Middle Ages, and Edgar Francis's study of al-Buni's writings on protection and healing, are among the works that help you see the layers.

The name itself attracted material. A famous teacher's name is a magnet for the unattributed, and in a manuscript culture, where every copy was made by hand, a copyist who had a good treatise and no author could easily give it one. The compilation did not require deceit. It required only the ordinary habit of a world in which a book was a living collection, enlarged as it travelled.

Then came print, and with print the book's fate changed. In the nineteenth century the great Muslim presses took up lithography, a method of printing from a stone on which the text is written in the copyist's own hand, which suited Arabic script very well. Lithographed editions of the Shams al-Ma'arif were produced in Cairo and in Bombay, one of the great centres of Arabic lithography, and cheap copies went out through every market. A book that had once passed between teacher and student was now on sale to anyone who could pay. The hand-copied versions had been protected by the need to find a teacher. Print removed that need.

The reputation followed the book into the modern world. In some places it has been restricted, and in others it has been sold on the pavements. It is the kind of book that people lower their voices to describe. Stories grew around it that the manuscripts never told: tales of those who read it carelessly, of copies that could not be kept, of a book that, in a sense, reads its owner. A thick printed volume, with its forty chapters, its seals and its tables, looks as formidable as its reputation, and it is easy to assume that the whole of it is the work of a single thirteenth-century hand.

It is not, and now you know why the scholars take such pains to say so.

Gather what you have now. A Sufi from Buna taught in Cairo in the early thirteenth century that God created by speech, that the letters and the names are the fabric of creation, and that to know them is a path of devotion. Around his teaching lay an inheritance far older than he was: the numbering of the alphabet, the elements and their qualities, the squares that mathematicians had studied and physicians' patients had worn, and the planetary hours and mansions of the Moon that the astrologers also used. And over four centuries more came additions, the angels, the kings of the jinn, the great oaths, the later chains of teachers and the vast compilation that now carries his name.

Hold two readers side by side. A reader in Cairo in the year thirteen hundred, three-quarters of a century after al-Buni's death, would have held a manuscript copied by hand, perhaps a treatise like the Lata'if al-Isharat. He would have received it from a teacher who judged him fit, and he would have read it as part of a life of fasting, prayer and recitation. Whatever he made of the squares, he would have read them inside a devotion to the names of God. A reader now, perhaps in a bookshop in Cairo or Bombay or elsewhere, holds a printed book of forty chapters that is not the work of one author, and may hold it with fear, or curiosity, or pious suspicion. The words are in large part the same words. The setting could hardly be more different.

This is why the scholars have given so much labour to telling the layers apart. Noah Gardiner's work on the manuscripts shows when the large book was assembled and what it was assembled from. Jean-Charles Coulon's work on magic in the medieval Islamic lands places the book in the long history of such writing and helps to separate what belongs to al-Buni's circle from what came later. Their work does not make the book smaller. It makes it more intelligible. You can now see a teaching of devotion, an old inheritance of number and star, and a late harvest of spirit-lore, where the pavement seller sees one forbidding object.

And you can say something fairer about the argument as well. A book that has been defended by learned men who saw in it a way to ask God by His own names, and condemned by learned men who feared that it led from asking to commanding, is not a book to be sorted at once into good and bad. It is a book at the edge of a boundary that Muslims themselves have drawn and redrawn for eight hundred years.

Return, then, to the square you were shown at the start. Four, nine, two. Three, five, seven. Eight, one, six. It was in the hands of a theologian before al-Buni was born, in the hands of mathematicians before that, and in the hands of the physicians and soldiers who wore it after. Its meaning changed with every person who wrote it. The figure itself did not change at all.
