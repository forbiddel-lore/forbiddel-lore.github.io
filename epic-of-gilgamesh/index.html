Full script

Gilgamesh is the king of Uruk, a powerful ruler whose demands wear down the city's people. The gods answer their complaints by making Enkidu, a wild man intended to match the king's strength. Shamhat brings Enkidu from the animals into human society, and Gilgamesh meets him in a struggle that becomes friendship. Together they travel to the Cedar Forest and kill Humbaba, its fearsome guardian. Gilgamesh then rejects Ishtar, and the goddess sends the Bull of Heaven against Uruk. Gilgamesh and Enkidu kill the bull, but the gods decree that Enkidu must die. Gilgamesh mourns him and searches for Utnapishtim, the survivor of the great flood, hoping to learn how a human being might escape death. Utnapishtim tells the flood story, and Gilgamesh fails the tests that follow. He finds a plant said to restore youth, but a snake takes it while he is bathing. Gilgamesh returns to Uruk and presents its walls as the enduring work of a king. Sumerian poems about Gilgamesh were composed around two thousand one hundred before the common era. An Old Babylonian epic belongs roughly to eighteen hundred before the common era, while the twelve-tablet Standard Babylonian version is known mainly from the library of Ashurbanipal at Nineveh in the seventh century before the common era. This episode first follows how the tablets were recovered, then tells the epic from its first tablet to its last, and closes with what historians can establish about how it was composed and how it relates to other flood stories.

The Epic of Gilgamesh survived because its words were impressed into clay. Mesopotamian scribes used a cut reed to press wedge-shaped signs into damp tablets. The tablets could record contracts, school exercises, royal inscriptions, prayers and long literary compositions. Once dried, clay remained durable. A tablet that was accidentally baked in a building fire could become harder rather than disappear. This is one reason ancient destruction sometimes preserved documents. Fire damaged buildings and broke tablets, but it could also protect the clay from later decay.

The epic is known in several languages, periods and forms. Its central figure first appears in Sumerian literature. Sumerian was the language of the southern Mesopotamian city-states and was still used by scribes long after it ceased to be the ordinary spoken language of the region. Several Sumerian poems tell separate episodes in the career of a ruler called Bilgames, the Sumerian form of the name later written Gilgamesh. They include conflicts with enemies, a journey to the Cedar Forest, and encounters with death and the underworld. These poems are not simply the twelve tablets of the later epic translated into Sumerian. They are independent compositions, and the later Babylonian narrative drew on some of their stories.

The Sumerian King List counts Gilgamesh among the early kings of Uruk and assigns him a reign of a hundred and twenty six years. The figure belongs to the traditions preserved by that list, not to an independently dated royal archive. Some historians think Gilgamesh may preserve the memory of an early ruler of Uruk, perhaps in the early third millennium before the common era. No contemporary inscription has established the details of his reign, and the question remains debated. The king list is evidence for how later scribes classified ancient kings. It is not a straightforward biography.

The earliest literary references to Gilgamesh are found in Sumerian material from around two thousand one hundred before the common era. The poems were copied in different places and periods, and their tablets are often incomplete. One poem tells of Gilgamesh and Huwawa, the Sumerian counterpart of the later Humbaba. Another concerns the Bull of Heaven. Others describe Enkidu and the underworld or the death of Gilgamesh. Their existence shows that the stories circulated separately before a Babylonian editor or editors brought related episodes into a larger narrative.

The Babylonian versions used a different language and a different scribal tradition. Akkadian was written in cuneiform signs borrowed from the Sumerian system, but the language belonged to another branch of the ancient Near Eastern language family. The Old Babylonian epic, copied around eighteen hundred before the common era, survives in fragments from several sites. Its wording is not identical to the later Standard Babylonian form. Some episodes are arranged differently, and some lines that appear in the later poem are absent or damaged in the earlier witnesses.

Two important Old Babylonian witnesses are commonly identified by their present locations. The Pennsylvania tablet, now associated with the University of Pennsylvania, preserves part of the meeting between Gilgamesh and Enkidu. The Yale tablet preserves another section of the early epic. These tablets entered modern collections at different times; the Pennsylvania tablet was bought in nineteen fourteen. Their importance lies in their language and age. They show that the epic had a substantial form centuries before the library of Ashurbanipal, while also showing that the poem continued to change.

Jeffrey Tigay, in The Evolution of the Gilgamesh Epic, argued that the poem developed through the expansion, combination and reshaping of earlier stories. The evidence is textual. Different tablets preserve different lengths, episodes and formulations, and the later version sometimes joins material that once circulated independently. Andrew George has likewise treated the epic as a work with a long history rather than a composition created all at once. The surviving witnesses do not allow a complete account of every stage, but they establish that the poem was repeatedly copied and revised.

The version most often read today is called the Standard Babylonian version. It is written in a relatively consistent literary form and was arranged as a sequence of twelve tablets. The opening line gives the work its ancient title, usually translated as He Who Saw the Deep or He Who Saw the Abyss. The poem moves from Gilgamesh's rule in Uruk through his friendship with Enkidu, their adventures, Enkidu's death, and Gilgamesh's search for Utnapishtim.

The title "Standard Babylonian" describes a language and textual form, not a single surviving manuscript. The text is known from copies made at different places and dates. The most extensive group comes from Nineveh, where tablets were collected for the library of Ashurbanipal in the seventh century before the common era. Other fragments come from sites in Mesopotamia and from later copies. The twelve-tablet arrangement is therefore a scholarly reconstruction based on the surviving evidence.

Ancient tradition associated the editing of the epic with Sin-leqi-unninni. A later catalogue names him in connection with the composition, and he may have been remembered as a learned Babylonian scribe who shaped the Standard Babylonian form. The surviving tablets do not provide a signed manuscript proving that he personally wrote every line. The attribution is a later tradition, and modern scholars use it cautiously. The text itself is the result of a long process of copying and composition, whether or not one particular editor gave the final form its structure.

The library of Ashurbanipal provides the principal evidence for that form. Ashurbanipal ruled the Assyrian empire from around six hundred sixty eight to around six hundred thirty one before the common era. He presented himself as a learned king who could read and write, and his agents gathered tablets from temple and palace collections. The library was not a modern public library. It was a royal collection containing lexical lists, rituals, omens, medical texts, royal records, myths and literary works. Scribes copied tablets, requested exemplars and stored related compositions together.

Nineveh stood on the eastern bank of the Tigris, in the region now associated with northern Iraq. The mound of Kuyunjik contained the remains of the royal palaces and libraries. The city was destroyed in the late seventh century before the common era, when an alliance of Babylonians and Medes defeated the Assyrian kingdom. Buildings collapsed, tablets broke, and many rooms burned. That destruction left thousands of clay fragments in the ground.

European excavation began there in the nineteenth century. Austen Henry Layard excavated at Nineveh in the late eighteen forties, uncovering palace reliefs and cuneiform tablets. Hormuzd Rassam continued major work in the area, including excavations in eighteen fifty three. Their efforts sent large quantities of material to the British Museum. The movement of these objects was part of the imperial archaeology of the period, and the tablets were separated from their original rooms, groups and archaeological contexts. Even so, the collections preserved written evidence that could not yet be read.

Cuneiform decipherment had begun through royal inscriptions from other sites. Scholars compared repeated signs, proper names and parallel versions, gradually identifying the languages and writing system. The process was difficult because the signs could represent syllables, words or grammatical markers, and because a single sign could have several values. Akkadian texts could be read only after scholars had established the language's grammar and vocabulary through many inscriptions.

George Smith worked at the British Museum as an engraver and studied cuneiform tablets in his spare time. He became especially interested in texts concerning creation, the gods and a great flood. In the tablet fragments from Nineveh, he recognised a narrative in which a man survived a flood after receiving instructions to build a large vessel. On the third of December eighteen seventy two, Smith read his account of the discovery to the Society of Biblical Archaeology in London.

The report drew attention because the flood narrative resembled the story in the Book of Genesis. Smith's work did not establish that one text simply copied the other. It showed that flood traditions existed in several ancient Near Eastern languages and periods, and that the Nineveh library preserved a major Akkadian version. The episode later became part of the eleventh tablet of the Gilgamesh epic.

Smith believed that a missing portion of the flood account might still be found. In eighteen seventy three, he travelled to Mesopotamia with support connected to the Daily Telegraph and searched among the ruins and collections. He found additional fragments, although the modern story of the search has sometimes been told more dramatically than the evidence permits. The important result was the expansion of the known text and the confirmation that damaged tablets could be joined through repeated phrases, matching edges and the sequence of the narrative.

The recovery of the epic continued after Smith. Scholars compared fragments in the British Museum with tablets in other collections. A broken edge could match a fragment excavated far away or acquired decades earlier. Repeated formulas helped establish the order of columns and lines. The language of one fragment could also reveal its place in the poem through grammar, vocabulary and narrative sequence. Reconstruction therefore depends on physical joins, textual parallels and knowledge of scribal practice.

The tablets from Nineveh were themselves incomplete. Some had been broken in the ancient destruction of the city. Others had lost their edges, and some lines were damaged by erosion or earlier handling. The modern twelve-tablet text is consequently a critical reconstruction. Andrew George's editions place the surviving manuscripts beside one another and distinguish the readable lines from restorations. Restored words are based on grammar, parallel passages and the space available on the tablet, not on unrestricted invention.

New discoveries have continued to alter the picture. Tablets found or identified after two thousand are often small and fragmentary, but they can preserve lines missing from older editions or clarify readings already proposed. The Gilgamesh Dream Tablet, an Old Babylonian fragment about thirty five hundred years old in which Gilgamesh tells his dreams to his mother Ninsun, was returned by the United States to Iraq in twenty twenty one. The Museum of the Bible had bought it at auction in twenty fourteen, and American authorities seized it in twenty nineteen on the ground that it had been brought into the United States illegally. Its return belongs to the continuing history of Mesopotamian archaeology, in which the reconstruction of a text is connected not only to philology but also to the movement and legal status of antiquities.

The recovery is therefore not a single event. It began with the survival of fired and dried clay, continued through nineteenth-century excavation and the decipherment of cuneiform, and remains incomplete because new fragments can change a reading. George Smith recognised the flood account in eighteen seventy two, but the epic itself was not restored in a single act. It was assembled from Sumerian poems, Old Babylonian witnesses, Standard Babylonian tablets and later discoveries.

The first tablet opens with a prologue about a man who saw everything. The poem calls attention to Gilgamesh's knowledge of distant places, his experience of human affairs and his account of what existed before the flood. He travelled widely, endured hardship and brought back knowledge. The poem directs attention to the walls of Uruk, presenting them as the work of Gilgamesh and as evidence of his rule. Uruk is described through its wall, its outer defenses and its sacred precinct. The city itself becomes the first physical object associated with the king.

The prologue also tells its audience how to check what it says. It orders the listener to go up on the wall of Uruk and walk along it, to examine the foundation and inspect the brickwork, and asks whether the masonry was not made of baked brick. It then points to a box of cedar wood fastened with bronze clasps, and says that inside lies a tablet of lapis lazuli on which Gilgamesh recorded everything he went through. The poem thus presents itself as a written record kept in the city, and invites the listener to test the claim against the city wall. The lapis tablet is a literary device. No such object has been found, and the tablets that carry the epic are ordinary clay.

Gilgamesh is introduced as the king of Uruk. The text gives him extraordinary status, saying that he is two parts god and one part man. He is powerful, beautiful and formidable, but his strength has become a burden to the city. The people of Uruk complain to the gods. Their complaint concerns Gilgamesh's conduct as ruler. He does not leave the young men of the city in peace, and his demands affect the families of Uruk. The poem also presents a custom or royal privilege connected with marriage, though the damaged condition and interpretation of some lines have produced discussion among translators. The central point is clear: the people regard the king's power as excessive and appeal to the gods for relief.

The gods hear the complaint. Anu, the sky god and chief authority in the assembly, receives the people's appeal. The goddess Aruru is told to create a counterpart to Gilgamesh. She forms Enkidu from clay. The poem presents Enkidu as a being made to balance the king's strength, not as a soldier raised by an army or a rival from another city. Enkidu is created outside human society and placed among the animals of the steppe.

The poem gives the making of Enkidu a few concrete details. Aruru washes her hands, pinches off a piece of clay and throws it down in the open country, and Enkidu is formed there. His hair is long, and his locks grow thick like grain. He knows nothing of people or of settled land. He eats grass with the gazelles and jostles with the herds at the watering place. When the trapper first sees him at the water, the trapper is afraid. He goes home silent and dismayed and tells his father that a man of great strength has come down from the hills, that this man fills in the pits the trapper has dug and pulls up the traps he has set.

Enkidu has a hairy body and lives with wild creatures. He eats and drinks with them and moves through the watering places. His presence interferes with a trapper's work. The trapper finds that the animals escape from his snares because Enkidu has been opening the traps and freeing them. The trapper reports this to his father, who advises him to go to Uruk and speak with Gilgamesh. The king tells him to take Shamhat, a woman associated with the temple and with sexual power, to the watering place.

The trapper and Shamhat return to the wilderness. They find Enkidu among the animals. Shamhat approaches him and remains with him for six days and seven nights. The poem states the duration directly. Afterward Enkidu rises and seeks the animals, but they no longer accept him as one of their own. His strength has changed, and his relation to human society has begun. The animals flee from him. Enkidu has gained understanding, but he has lost his former place among the herds.

Shamhat tells Enkidu that he is handsome and that human society contains houses, temples and the king of Uruk. She takes him toward the shepherds' camps. The shepherds give him bread, but Enkidu does not know how to eat it. They set beer before him, but he does not know how to drink it. Shamhat instructs him. He eats until he is full and drinks seven cups of beer. The beer changes his mood, and he washes his hairy body and anoints himself. He takes up a weapon and protects the shepherds from predators.

The poem marks this as a change in Enkidu's identity. He has been brought from the animal world into a human community. He receives clothing and begins to learn human customs. He also hears about Gilgamesh. The king's authority reaches beyond the palace, and his behavior has become part of the speech of ordinary people. Enkidu now has a reason to travel to Uruk.

Before Enkidu reaches the city, Gilgamesh has two dreams. In the first, he sees an object fall from the sky or appear in his city. The people of Uruk gather around it, and Gilgamesh struggles to lift it. He embraces it and treats it as something precious. In the second dream, another object falls or appears, and the people again gather. This time the object is an axe, and again Gilgamesh loves it as a man loves a wife. In both dreams, Gilgamesh's mother Ninsun interprets the object as a sign of a powerful companion.

Ninsun is described as a wise goddess and the mother of Gilgamesh. Gilgamesh reports the dreams to her, and she explains that the man represented by the falling object will become his equal and companion. He will be strong enough to challenge Gilgamesh, and the king will love him as a companion. The dreams prepare the narrative for Enkidu's arrival. The man made by Aruru to oppose Gilgamesh will not remain only an enemy.

The sequence is preserved most fully in the Standard Babylonian opening, but several parts are damaged in the Nineveh tablets. The episode concerning Enkidu's transformation also has earlier versions, and the Old Babylonian wording differs in details and arrangement. The text's knowledge of Gilgamesh's dreams depends on the surviving tablet lines and on restorations based on parallel passages. Scholars agree about the broad sequence, while some individual images and translations remain disputed.

The first tablet therefore moves from the city's walls to the king's abuse of power, from the gods' response to the making of Enkidu, and from the steppe to the dreams that announce friendship. Its opening prologue claims knowledge gained through travel and suffering, while the narrative itself begins with a ruler who must be confronted by an equal.

Enkidu travels toward Uruk with Shamhat. Along the way, he meets people who tell him about Gilgamesh. The king is preparing for a wedding, and Enkidu learns that Gilgamesh intends to enter the bride's chamber before the bridegroom. The poem presents this as part of the conduct that has angered the people. Enkidu decides to stop him.

When Enkidu reaches Uruk, the city's inhabitants gather around him. They see a man whose body and strength resemble the descriptions of Gilgamesh's equal. Some compare him to the king. Enkidu has been formed in the wilderness, but his encounter with Shamhat and the shepherds has made him capable of entering the city. He now directs his strength against the king's abuse of authority.

Gilgamesh approaches the wedding house. Enkidu stands in the doorway and blocks him. The text describes the entrance as a place where the king's progress is stopped. Gilgamesh and Enkidu begin to wrestle. They seize one another and fight in the street. Their struggle is not a brief contest. The poem gives it weight and force, describing how the doorposts shake and how the two men test each other's strength.

Gilgamesh gains the advantage, though the damaged text leaves some details uncertain. Enkidu recognises that Gilgamesh possesses exceptional power. Gilgamesh also recognises that Enkidu is his equal. The contest ends without the death of either man. Their opposition changes into respect. The king who has ruled without a rival has found one, and the wild man created to balance him has found the person who can match him.

In the Old Babylonian Pennsylvania tablet the fight ends when Gilgamesh bows to the ground and his anger subsides. Enkidu then speaks to him. He says that Gilgamesh was born unlike other men, that his mother Ninsun, the wild cow of the cattle pens, raised his head above the rest, and that the god Enlil has decreed kingship over the people for him. That speech is the first statement of the friendship, and it comes from the man who has just stopped the king at the door.

The two men embrace and become friends. The change is immediate in the narrative, but it follows the purpose of Enkidu's creation. Aruru formed him to oppose Gilgamesh, and the opposition has produced companionship instead. The people of Uruk now have a different relationship to the king's strength. Gilgamesh remains powerful, but his strength is joined to Enkidu's.

The poem then turns toward a new undertaking. Gilgamesh proposes a journey to the Cedar Forest. He wants to cut down the cedars and make his name known. The forest lies beyond the ordinary world of Uruk, and Humbaba guards it. Gilgamesh describes Humbaba as a formidable being appointed by Enlil to protect the forest. The guardian's roar is compared to a flood, his mouth to fire and his breath to death. The exact imagery varies in the tablets, but the poem consistently presents Humbaba as a dangerous opponent whose power is tied to the forest.

Enkidu warns Gilgamesh. He knows the region beyond the settled lands and has heard about Humbaba's strength. The guardian's seven terrors or radiances surround him, and the god Shamash protects the forest's boundary. Enkidu tells Gilgamesh that Humbaba will hear even a distant approach. He advises caution and reminds the king that Enlil gave Humbaba his commission. The journey is not simply a hunt for timber. It is an attack on a protected place and on a figure appointed by a major god.

Gilgamesh answers that ordinary human life is short. He wants to accomplish an act that will preserve his name. The text does not present this as a modern theory of fame. It places the desire within the world of kingship, heroic reputation and the limits of human life. Gilgamesh argues that the people of Uruk will remember a ruler who entered the forest and defeated its guardian. Enkidu remains cautious, but the friendship has already bound him to the king's undertaking.

The Old Babylonian fragments preserve part of this debate, though not every line survives. In one important passage, Gilgamesh expresses fear of death and the desire to make a lasting name. The wording is older and sometimes more direct than the Standard Babylonian form. The later version expands the meeting and gives the forest journey a larger place within the poem's movement from kingship to mortality.

Gilgamesh brings the proposal before the elders of Uruk. In the Standard Babylonian text, the elders advise him. Their response is practical and protective. They tell the king not to rely only on youth and strength. They recommend that Enkidu guide him because Enkidu knows the roads and the danger of Humbaba. The elders do not forbid the expedition. Their counsel recognises both Gilgamesh's authority and the need for Enkidu's knowledge.

Gilgamesh and Enkidu then prepare for the journey. The text gives the king's mother, Ninsun, an important role. She hears of the plan and understands the danger. She goes to the temple of Shamash, the sun god, and asks for protection for Gilgamesh. Ninsun changes clothing or performs the actions appropriate to prayer in the surviving account, though some lines are damaged. She raises her hands and addresses Shamash.

Ninsun asks why Gilgamesh has been given a restless heart and why he has been moved to undertake a distant journey toward Humbaba. She places Gilgamesh under Shamash's protection. She also takes Enkidu into the household of the gods as Gilgamesh's companion. The precise ritual language is uncertain, but the narrative function is clear. Ninsun recognises that Enkidu has become part of Gilgamesh's life and treats him as more than an outsider.

In this episode Ninsun gives Enkidu a maternal and sacred status. The text later calls him the companion and brother of Gilgamesh in poetic language. That relationship begins here, before the journey. Gilgamesh has acquired a rival, then a friend, and now a companion accepted within the king's divine family. The next tablet will show the practical preparation for the expedition.

The Standard Babylonian second tablet is damaged at several points, and its arrangement is not identical to every earlier version. The broad order is established by the surviving tablets: Enkidu enters Uruk, blocks Gilgamesh at the wedding house, wrestles with him, and becomes his friend; Gilgamesh proposes the Cedar Forest; Enkidu warns him; the king's elders and Ninsun respond. Andrew George's edition distinguishes the preserved lines from restorations, while Jeffrey Tigay's study places the episode within the longer development of the poem. The details of some customs and speeches remain debated, but the narrative sequence is secure.

The journey to the Cedar Forest begins with preparation in Uruk. Gilgamesh and Enkidu gather weapons and equipment for a distant expedition. The poem lists objects made for the king, including an axe of great weight and a sword with a heavy blade. The weapons are not described as ordinary tools. Their size and weight emphasise Gilgamesh's heroic strength and the scale of the battle expected.

The elders advise the two companions again. They tell Gilgamesh to trust Enkidu's knowledge of the route and to let Enkidu go first into dangerous places. The instruction gives Enkidu a practical role that matches his origin. He has lived beyond the city and knows the terrain, while Gilgamesh possesses the authority and force of the king. Their cooperation is necessary because neither man has every kind of knowledge required for the expedition.

Ninsun goes to the temple of Shamash. She asks the sun god to protect Gilgamesh during the journey. In the Standard Babylonian account, her prayer includes a complaint about the unrest in her son's heart and the distance of the road. She invokes Shamash as the deity who crosses the heavens and sees the lands below. The prayer connects the human expedition to divine oversight.

Ninsun also adopts Enkidu into Gilgamesh's family. She entrusts him to Gilgamesh and treats him as a companion bound to the king. The act does not erase Enkidu's origin among the animals, but it gives his new social identity a place within the household of Uruk's ruler. The two men leave as friends under divine protection, although the outcome of the journey remains uncertain.

The road to the Cedar Forest takes several days. The poem describes the companions travelling through open country and stopping at intervals. At each stopping place Gilgamesh prepares for sleep, and Enkidu watches over him or helps interpret what occurs. Gilgamesh has dreams during the journey. The dream episodes are difficult because the tablets are damaged and the images are compressed, but the pattern is clear: Gilgamesh sees alarming signs, Enkidu interprets them as favorable, and the companions continue.

The poem compresses the distance. In three days the two men cover a journey that would ordinarily take a month and a half. At every stage they eat and rest, and at each stopping place Gilgamesh climbs a mountain, pours out an offering of flour, and asks the mountain to bring him a dream. The dreams that follow are the ones that frighten him and that Enkidu reads as good omens.

One dream concerns a mountain that falls or threatens to overwhelm Gilgamesh. Enkidu explains the image as a sign that they will defeat Humbaba. Another involves a storm, a dark object or a powerful being. Again Enkidu gives a reassuring interpretation. The exact correspondence between each image and its meaning differs among copies. The dream sequence functions as a dialogue between Gilgamesh's fear and Enkidu's confidence.

The journey lasts several days, and the companions reach the forest. The text marks the change in place through the cedars themselves. The trees are tall, fragrant and associated with the domain of the gods. Their timber is valuable, but the forest is not represented merely as a supply of wood. It is a protected sacred landscape, and Humbaba's presence gives the expedition the character of an assault on divine order.

Humbaba hears the approach. The poem describes his voice and the terror surrounding him. He speaks from the forest or appears before the companions, and he challenges their right to enter. He knows that Gilgamesh is a king of Uruk, but he treats the expedition as an act of trespass. Enkidu and Gilgamesh face him with their weapons.

Shamash intervenes on behalf of the two men. The sun god sends powerful winds against Humbaba. The surviving lines name several winds or describe a storm that weakens and immobilises the guardian. The winds prevent Humbaba from moving freely and make it possible for Gilgamesh and Enkidu to overcome him. Divine assistance is therefore part of the battle. The victory is not presented as the result of human strength alone.

Humbaba is terrified and pleads for his life. He addresses Gilgamesh and offers to serve him. He promises access to the forest and offers to cut its trees for the king. In some passages he appeals to the fact that Gilgamesh is young or inexperienced. He asks the king to spare him and presents himself as a servant who can provide timber. His speeches show that the guardian is not silent or mindless. He understands the danger and attempts to bargain.

Gilgamesh hesitates. The two companions discuss whether Humbaba should live. Enkidu urges immediate action. He warns that Humbaba's words are meant to delay them and that the guardian might regain his strength. Enkidu's advice is shaped by fear of the consequences. He knows that the forest's protector is dangerous and tells Gilgamesh not to trust the plea.

Humbaba continues to appeal. He invokes the god Enlil, who appointed him to guard the forest. He presents the killing as an offense against the order established by the gods. The text does not make his claim false within the narrative. Humbaba is genuinely the guardian of the forest, and Enlil genuinely granted him that role. The conflict therefore places Gilgamesh and Enkidu against a being whose authority comes from a god.

The companions kill Humbaba. The exact sequence of blows is damaged in the surviving tablets, but the result is certain. The guardian's head is cut off, and the men take possession of it. In some versions Humbaba's seven terrors are removed or fall away. The victory gives Gilgamesh the fame he sought, but it also creates a debt to the divine order that will shape later events.

After Humbaba's death, Gilgamesh and Enkidu enter the forest. They cut down cedars and select a great tree. The poem connects the timber to the building of a door or an object for a temple. The exact destination differs across versions, and some lines are missing. The men do not merely kill Humbaba and leave. They harvest the forest, making the expedition a material act as well as a heroic contest.

Enlil reacts to the death of Humbaba. The surviving text is damaged, but the god's anger is clear. The companions have killed the guardian appointed to protect the forest and have taken its trees. The Standard Babylonian tablet does not preserve every detail of Enlil's judgment, and the earlier Sumerian poem differs in its treatment of the event. The narrative nonetheless establishes that the victory has offended a god whose authority matters.

The episode's wording is known chiefly from the Standard Babylonian tablets associated with Nineveh, supplemented by Old Babylonian and Sumerian witnesses. The Sumerian Huwawa poems preserve an earlier form of the forest adventure, while the Akkadian tablets expand the journey, dreams, prayer and debate with Humbaba. Scholars disagree about how much of the later sequence derives from a single early poem and how much was assembled from separate traditions. Andrew George and Jeffrey Tigay both treat the differences among versions as evidence for continued composition and revision. The Cedar Forest episode is therefore preserved as a layered narrative whose sequence can be followed even where individual lines remain damaged.

After Gilgamesh and Enkidu returned to Uruk from the Cedar Forest, Ishtar saw Gilgamesh and proposed marriage. The tablet presents her speech as a list of royal gifts and privileges. She offered a chariot of lapis lazuli and gold, with gold fittings and precious stones. Kings, princes and nobles would bow before Gilgamesh. His sheep and goats would bear abundant young, and his asses would surpass mules in strength. The tablet describes the wealth that Ishtar promised, but Gilgamesh rejected her.

His answer is not a general refusal of marriage. It is a catalogue of Ishtar's earlier lovers and the fates that followed them. He recalls Dumuzi, the lover of her youth, for whom she established yearly mourning. He recalls a bright-winged bird that she struck and broke, leaving it in a thicket. He recalls a lion that she trapped and left with a mutilated body. He recalls a stallion that she condemned to the whip, the spur and a long journey. He recalls Ishullanu, the gardener of her father, who brought her baskets of dates. When Ishtar invited him to enter her dwelling, Ishullanu refused her and compared her to a wall that did not protect its builders. Ishtar then changed him into a frog, according to Gilgamesh's speech.

Gilgamesh's reply begins with a question. He asks what he could give her if he took her as his wife: oil for her body, clothing, bread and drink. He says that she is a brazier that goes out in the cold, a door that keeps out neither wind nor storm, a palace that crushes its warriors, a shoe that bites its owner. Then he asks which of her lovers she ever loved forever, and the list that follows is his answer. One more figure belongs to it, a shepherd who baked loaves for her every day and slaughtered kids for her. Ishtar struck him and turned him into a wolf, and his own herd boys drove him away and his own dogs bit his thighs.

Gilgamesh asks what would be different for him. He says that he would suffer the same fate as these former lovers. The speech names Ishtar as a woman whose affection brings destruction. The text does not give a separate reply from Gilgamesh after the catalogue. Ishtar turns to her father Anu and her mother Antum. She weeps before them and demands the Bull of Heaven. She threatens that, unless Anu gives her the bull, she will break open the gates of the Netherworld and raise the dead. The dead would then outnumber the living.

Anu asks what would happen if he granted the bull. He says that famine would follow. Ishtar answers that she has already secured provisions for the people and animals of the land. Anu gives her the Bull of Heaven. The tablet then moves from the divine assembly to Uruk. The bull descends, and its first breath opens a great pit. One hundred men of Uruk fall into it. When it breathes again, two hundred more are swallowed. With the third breath, Enkidu is thrown toward the pit but catches hold of the bull by its horns.

The bull's head is described through its physical force. Enkidu seizes it, and the beast lashes with its tail. He calls to Gilgamesh and tells him that the bull is overpowering him. Gilgamesh answers that the pair must bring it down. Enkidu takes the bull's measure and tells Gilgamesh to strike behind the horns, between the neck and the tendons. The two heroes attack together. Enkidu holds the animal, while Gilgamesh drives his sword between its neck and horns. The Bull of Heaven falls.

Gilgamesh and Enkidu then cut out the heart and present it to Shamash. The text gives the offering as part of the heroes' immediate response to the kill. The horns are treated as objects of value. Gilgamesh fills them with oil and gives them to his father Lugalbanda. He also dedicates them to the goddess Ninsun. The tablet then turns to Ishtar. She stands on the wall of Uruk and pronounces a lament over the dead bull. She calls for prostitutes, courtesans and women of the city to mourn with her. Enkidu hears her.

Enkidu tears off the bull's right haunch and throws it toward Ishtar. He tells her that, if he could reach her, he would do the same to her. He also says that he would hang the bull's entrails beside her. Ishtar gathers the women who serve her and mourns over the haunch. Gilgamesh summons the craftsmen of Uruk. They admire the horns, which the tablet says are six measures in thickness. Their capacity is described in terms of a large quantity of oil. Gilgamesh dedicates the horns to his god Lugalbanda and places them in the temple.

The episode ends in celebration. Gilgamesh and Enkidu appear before the young men of Uruk. Gilgamesh asks which man is the most glorious among them. The answer identifies Gilgamesh as the most glorious and Enkidu as the strongest. The two heroes then go to the palace. The tablet's conclusion places their victory within the city and its public institutions. The bull has been killed, the weapons and horns have been dedicated, and Uruk has recognized the pair.

This tablet is known mainly from the standard Babylonian version preserved in the library of Ashurbanipal at Nineveh, although the epic also survives in other manuscript groups. Andrew George's edition places the sequence in Tablet Six and translates the long catalogue of Ishtar's lovers from damaged cuneiform witnesses whose wording is not complete everywhere. The story's order is secure: Ishtar proposes, Gilgamesh rejects her, the Bull of Heaven is released, and the two companions kill it. The exact number and wording of some damaged lines remain matters of reconstruction.

The immediate consequence is stated at the opening of the next tablet. The gods meet because Gilgamesh and Enkidu have killed Humbaba and the Bull of Heaven. The story has moved from victory to judgment, and the judgment falls on Enkidu.

The seventh tablet begins with Enkidu recounting a dream. In the dream, the gods sit in council. Anu, Enlil, Ea and Shamash are present, and the killing of the Bull of Heaven and Humbaba is discussed. Enlil declares that Enkidu must die. Shamash objects that the heroes acted under his protection when they killed Humbaba. Enlil's decision stands. In some readings of the damaged passage, the debate assigns the death to Enkidu while Gilgamesh is spared. The tablet therefore connects Enkidu's illness directly with the divine verdict.

Enkidu becomes ill. He lies on his bed and addresses his companion. The text gives a long curse against the trapper who first saw him in the steppe and against Shamhat, the woman who brought him into human society. Enkidu curses the trapper's livelihood. He wishes that the trapper would have no success in the fields, that wild animals would avoid his snares, and that his work would fail. He then curses Shamhat. He wishes that her dwelling would be without a family, that men would use her and then leave her, and that the rewards of her work would be taken from her.

Shamash hears this speech and rebukes Enkidu. The god reminds him of the life that Shamhat gave him. She clothed him, fed him, brought him to the city, and introduced him to Gilgamesh. Through her, Enkidu received bread, beer and the company of human beings. Shamash tells him that Gilgamesh became his friend and that the king granted him a place of honor. Enkidu accepts the correction. He does not withdraw the entire account of his suffering, but he changes the curse on Shamhat into a blessing. He says that she will be honored and that men will desire her company. Her earnings will be abundant, and she will receive jewelry and fine clothing.

The episode makes Shamash's reply part of the moral structure of Enkidu's final days. The same encounter that removed him from the animals gave him friendship, language and a place in Uruk. The text does not present the transition as simple gain. Enkidu's illness and approaching death lead him to speak against the human world, and Shamash answers by recalling what that world made possible.

Enkidu then has another dream. He sees a figure who seizes him and takes him to the Netherworld. The being is described with features of a terrifying creature: its face resembles a lion, its feet resemble an eagle's talons, and its hands are like claws. Enkidu struggles, but the figure changes him into a dove. The dream then opens onto the house of dust.

There Enkidu sees kings and officials who once wore crowns. He sees priests and attendants, musicians and servants. They sit in darkness, and dust is their food. The queen of the Netherworld, Ereshkigal, sits among them. The dream describes the dead according to rank, but rank has lost its power. Those who once ruled and those who once served share the same underworld condition. Enkidu also sees a man who has no funerary offering and another who has no one to care for his corpse. The dream presents burial rites and offerings as matters that affect the condition of the dead, but it does not offer a doctrine of reward and punishment in a later heavenly sense.

The house of dust, the dream says, is a house that no one leaves once he has entered it. The road to it is a road with no return. Its dwellers are clothed like birds, in garments of feathers, and they see no light. They sit in darkness, and dust lies on the door and on the bolt. The dead eat dust and clay. The picture is not one of punishment. It is a description of what happens to everyone, and the comfort of the dead depends on whether the living remember them with offerings.

Enkidu wakes and tells Gilgamesh what he has seen. His illness continues. The text then records his address to the sun god Shamash. He speaks of his former life in the steppe, when the trapper and Shamhat changed his condition. He recalls the companions of the wilderness and the animals among which he lived. His speech returns to the threshold between the open country and the city. The tablet's damaged lines make portions of the exchange difficult to restore, but its direction is clear. Enkidu is dying, and the dream has shown him the realm to which he will go.

On the twelfth day, Enkidu's sickness becomes severe. Gilgamesh is present. Enkidu describes the goddess and the house of dust in further words, then falls silent. The text states that Enkidu's fate is completed. He dies.

Gilgamesh responds with a lament that occupies much of Tablet Eight. He calls to the elders of Uruk, to the people of the city, and to the inhabitants of the land. He asks them to mourn Enkidu. He calls for the animals of the steppe, the rivers, the roads and the mountains to share in the grief. The companions of Enkidu are named through the places and creatures associated with his former life. Gilgamesh's lament is both personal and public. The king's grief is announced to the whole community.

He then addresses Enkidu directly. He calls him his companion and his beloved friend. He says that Enkidu was his protection and his strength. He compares the death to the loss of an axe, a weapon and a cloak. The comparisons are concrete objects of work, combat and travel. The text presents grief as the disappearance of the things through which a person acts in the world.

Gilgamesh orders craftsmen to make an image of Enkidu. The tablet describes the materials used for the funeral gifts. Gold and precious stones are shaped into objects, and gifts are prepared for the gods of the Netherworld. The exact sequence is damaged in places, but the ceremony is extensive. Gilgamesh gives offerings to Shamash, to the divine judges and to the queen of the Netherworld. He also orders food and drink to be placed before Enkidu.

The city's people participate in the mourning. The text names women, men and officials, and it sets the lament within the institutions of Uruk. Gilgamesh's grief is not resolved by the funeral. The final lines of the tablet turn from the rites for Enkidu to Gilgamesh's altered condition. The death has made mortality personal to the king. The next tablet begins with Gilgamesh leaving the city and going into the steppe.

The account is preserved chiefly in the standard Babylonian tablets from Nineveh, with significant damage and supplementary evidence from other sites. The dream of the house of dust belongs to the Mesopotamian picture of the Netherworld in which the dead continue in a diminished condition below the earth. Jeffrey Tigay argues that the epic's present form grew through the reshaping of earlier episodes, including the transition from independent Sumerian poems to a connected Akkadian narrative. The wording of the curses, dreams and funeral lists varies where tablets are broken, but the sequence from divine council to illness, death and lament is established.

After Enkidu's burial, Gilgamesh leaves Uruk. He goes into the steppe wearing a lion's skin. His grief has become fear. He asks whether he too will die as Enkidu died. The tablet gives no account of a royal council ordering Gilgamesh's death. Instead, his awareness of death follows from the loss of his companion. He abandons the city and begins searching for Utnapishtim, the man who survived the flood and received life without end.

Gilgamesh travels through the wilderness. He encounters lions and kills them. At night he pours water from a skin and looks toward the moon. The exact wording of these lines differs among manuscripts, but the image is consistent: the king moves through uncultivated land while thinking about death. He speaks of the darkness within himself and of the road to the edge of the world. He is not seeking a weapon, a kingdom or a rival. He is seeking the person who, according to the story, crossed the boundary that no ordinary human crosses.

At Mount Mashu, Gilgamesh reaches the passage guarded by scorpion people. The man and woman who guard the gate have bodies that rise from the earth to the sky. Their radiance is described as fearful, and their appearance causes Gilgamesh to stop. The male guardian asks where Gilgamesh is going. Gilgamesh answers that he is seeking Utnapishtim, who lives at the mouth of the rivers, and that he wishes to ask him about life and death.

The scorpion man tells him that no human has crossed the mountain's interior. The road through Mashu runs beneath the mountain, in the path of the sun. Gilgamesh says that he will go. The guardian opens the gate. Gilgamesh enters the tunnel.

The tunnel is completely dark. For a long distance he sees no light ahead and no light behind. The text measures the journey in double-hours. After several measures, the darkness remains total. Near the end, a faint light appears. The journey through the mountain takes twelve double-hours. Gilgamesh emerges at the far side, where the sun rises into another region.

He reaches a garden in which the trees bear precious stones. The branches hold carnelian, lapis lazuli and other colored materials. The garden is not described as an ordinary orchard. Its plants and fruits are made of valuables, and the scene marks the threshold of a divine or otherworldly landscape. Gilgamesh passes through it until he reaches the seashore.

There he finds Siduri, the tavern keeper. She is associated with a dwelling at the edge of the waters, and she sees Gilgamesh approaching in a lion skin with a face marked by travel and grief. At first she bars her door. Gilgamesh threatens to break it, and Siduri asks who he is. He tells her his name and recounts the deeds that have made him famous. He says that he killed Humbaba in the Cedar Forest, killed lions, crossed mountains and defeated the Bull of Heaven. He then explains why he has come. Enkidu, his companion, has died, and Gilgamesh fears the same fate.

Siduri tells him that the life he seeks he will not find, and that human beings are mortal. She says that the gods assigned death to humanity and kept life in their own hands. Her speech then turns toward the ordinary pleasures and duties of human existence. It refers to food, drink, clothing, bathing, family and celebration. The lines are often read as advice to accept the human condition, but the tablet does not make Gilgamesh settle for that advice. He asks where Utnapishtim is.

The Old Babylonian version gives the tavern keeper a longer and better known speech. She tells Gilgamesh that the life he is looking for he will not find. When the gods made mankind, she says, they set death aside for mankind and kept life in their own hands. She advises him to fill his belly, to be glad day and night, to make every day a day of rejoicing, to wear clean clothes, to wash his head and bathe in water, to look on the child who holds his hand, and to let his wife be happy in his embrace, because that is the task of mankind. Scholars debate how much of this advice the Standard Babylonian version keeps, since the later text is damaged at this point.

Siduri directs him to Urshanabi, the boatman of Utnapishtim. She says that Urshanabi is with the stone things. Gilgamesh finds Urshanabi in the forest or at the shore, with these objects near him. Their exact identity is uncertain. The text names them, but the surviving descriptions do not permit a secure modern identification. Gilgamesh attacks them and breaks them. In some manuscripts, the stone things are associated with the boat's operation; in others, the wording has been interpreted differently.

Urshanabi asks why Gilgamesh destroyed the stone things. Gilgamesh explains his grief and his need to cross the waters. Urshanabi tells him that the damage has made the crossing more difficult. He orders Gilgamesh to cut poles, each sixty cubits long. The number is repeated in the tablet. Gilgamesh cuts the poles and brings them to the boat. Together they set out across the waters of death.

The crossing cannot be made by touching the water. The poles are used once and then discarded, so that the hands do not touch the deadly surface. When the poles are exhausted, Gilgamesh removes his garment and uses it as a sail. The journey lasts three days. The text gives the distance in a conventional measure, and the waters finally end. Urshanabi and Gilgamesh reach the shore where Utnapishtim lives.

Utnapishtim sees Gilgamesh and asks why he has come. The visitor appears unlike a king returning from victory. His clothing is damaged, his body is marked by travel, and grief has changed his appearance. Gilgamesh says that he has come because Enkidu died. He says that the fear of death has driven him across the wilderness. He asks Utnapishtim how he obtained life that does not end.

The tablet has brought Gilgamesh to the limit of the human world. The tunnel under Mashu, the jewel garden, the seashore and the waters of death form a sequence of thresholds. Each is crossed through an object or a guide: the scorpion guardian opens the mountain, Siduri gives directions, Urshanabi provides the boat, and the poles make the crossing possible. The text does not yet explain Utnapishtim's fate. That explanation begins in Tablet Eleven with the account of the flood.

These chapters belong to the standard Babylonian version, especially the Nineveh manuscripts, and their order is among the most stable parts of the epic. Some details are reconstructed from parallel witnesses. The meaning of the stone things remains disputed, and the measurements of the journey use ancient units whose exact modern equivalents cannot be stated with confidence. Siduri's speech is often treated as one of the epic's clearest statements about mortal life, although the damaged tablet leaves parts of its wording uncertain.

Utnapishtim answers Gilgamesh by telling him that the gods once decided to send a flood. The decision is associated with the city of Shuruppak. The text names the gods Anu, Enlil, Ninurta, Ennugi and Ea in the divine deliberation. The reason for the flood is not fully explained in the surviving passage. The gods agree to destroy humanity, while Ea finds a way to warn Utnapishtim.

Ea speaks to a reed wall rather than directly to the man. He tells the wall to listen and tells Utnapishtim to abandon his house and build a boat. He gives the dimensions: the boat is to have equal length and width, and its height is to correspond to its length. The instructions divide the vessel into decks and compartments. Bitumen is used to seal it. Utnapishtim is told to bring into the boat the seed of all living things, his family, craftsmen and animals of the steppe.

Utnapishtim describes the work in the first person. He loads the boat with silver and gold, with his family and kin, with the animals of the steppe and with craftsmen of every kind. The boat is sealed with bitumen and pitch, and the tablet gives its decks and compartments in measures that translators render differently because the lines are damaged. Utnapishtim then waits for the sign that Shamash had set, a storm to be rained down at evening. When the evening comes, he looks at the sky and finds it fearful, goes into the boat and shuts the door. The boatman Puzur-Amurri is given charge of the structure and everything in it.

Utnapishtim asks what answer he should give when the people of Shuruppak ask why he is building such a vessel. Ea provides a public explanation. Utnapishtim will say that he is leaving the land because Enlil is angry with him, and that he is going down to the realm of Ea. The boat is constructed, supplied with food and water, and loaded with living creatures and skilled workers.

The storm begins. The black cloud rises, and Adad thunders. The gods bring wind and flood. The storm is so violent that the gods themselves become afraid and retreat to the upper heaven. Ishtar laments that she agreed to the destruction of humanity. The flood covers the land for six days and seven nights. On the seventh day the storm breaks, and the sea becomes quiet. Utnapishtim opens the hatch and sees that the entire world has become a flat expanse of water.

The boat comes to rest on Mount Nimush. After seven days, Utnapishtim releases a dove. The bird goes out and returns because it finds no place to land. He releases a swallow, and it also returns. He then releases a raven. The raven finds the receding water, eats, circles and does not return. Utnapishtim opens the boat and releases the living creatures.

He makes an offering on the mountain. He arranges vessels and pours out the sacrifice. The gods smell the sweet savor and gather like flies over the sacrifice, because during the flood they had been deprived of offerings. Ishtar arrives and raises the necklace that Anu had made for her. She says that she will remember the days of the flood and never forget them. Enlil then sees the boat and becomes angry. He asks who allowed a human being to survive.

Ea answers Enlil. He does not deny the destruction, but reproaches Enlil for sending a flood without distinguishing among the people. Ea says that a lion or a wolf could have been sent, or that famine or plague could have been used. He then explains that he did not reveal the decision directly. He sent a dream to Utnapishtim and allowed him to infer the warning through the reed wall. Enlil approaches the boat and takes Utnapishtim and his wife by the hand. He blesses them and grants them a distant dwelling at the mouth of the rivers.

Before the test, Utnapishtim answers the question Gilgamesh asked. He says that the gods gave him life without end only after the flood, in a council that cannot be called again, and he asks who will assemble the gods on Gilgamesh's behalf. He then speaks about the limits of human endeavor. He asks whether people build a house that lasts forever, whether they seal a contract that holds forever, whether brothers divide an inheritance forever, whether hatred lasts forever in the land, whether the river rises and brings flood forever. He says that the great gods, together with the goddess of fate, appoint death and life, but that they do not reveal the day of death. The surviving lines are damaged, and translators differ over the exact order of the questions.

Utnapishtim's flood account is followed by Gilgamesh's test. Utnapishtim asks how a human being seeking lasting life could defeat sleep, the ordinary companion of death. Gilgamesh sits down, and sleep overtakes him. Utnapishtim tells his wife to bake a loaf of bread on each day that Gilgamesh sleeps. The loaves show how long he has been unconscious. When he is shaken awake, they tell the story in order: the first is dried hard, the second leathery, the third moist, the fourth has a white crust, the fifth has gone gray with mold, the sixth is freshly baked, and the seventh is still on the coals. Gilgamesh has slept for a week. After six days and seven nights, Utnapishtim wakes him. Gilgamesh insists that he has only just fallen asleep, but the loaves prove otherwise. Utnapishtim tells Urshanabi to take him back.

Before Gilgamesh leaves, Utnapishtim reveals another possibility. He tells him about a plant at the bottom of the waters. Its name means The Old Man Becomes a Young Man. Gilgamesh ties stones to his feet, sinks into the water and cuts the plant. He brings it back and says that he will take it to Uruk and test it on an old man. He intends to eat it himself after seeing its effect.

On the return journey, Gilgamesh stops at a pool. He removes his clothing and enters the water. A snake smells the plant, comes out, takes it and sheds its skin. Gilgamesh sits down and weeps. Urshanabi tells him that the plant is gone and that his effort has failed. The snake's renewed skin supplies the story's explanation for its ability to shed an old outer layer, while Gilgamesh remains mortal.

The plant, in Utnapishtim's description, is thorny like a bramble, and it pricks the hand that takes it. Gilgamesh sits down and weeps. He asks for whom his hands have labored and for whom his heart's blood has been spent. He says that he has gained nothing for himself, and that the benefit has gone to the lion of the ground, the snake.

Gilgamesh and Urshanabi reach Uruk. Gilgamesh points to the city walls and asks Urshanabi to examine their masonry. The ending returns to the works of the king and the city. Gilgamesh has not gained unending life. He has returned with knowledge of death, the flood and the limits of human effort. The walls of Uruk stand as the final visible achievement named by the tablet.

Tablet Twelve differs from the preceding tablets. It begins with an episode in which Gilgamesh has a pukku and a mikku, objects whose names are not securely understood. They fall into the Netherworld, and Enkidu goes after them. Gilgamesh warns him not to behave in ways that would prevent his return, but Enkidu does not follow the instructions. The Netherworld retains him. Gilgamesh appeals to the gods, and in the end Ea, the god of the deep, intervenes, and the underworld god Nergal opens a hole in the earth so that Enkidu's spirit can rise. Gilgamesh asks him about the condition of the dead. Enkidu describes the different fates of those who have children, offerings or no one to care for them.

This tablet is a loose Akkadian translation of part of the Sumerian poem Bilgames and the Netherworld. It does not continue the flood story and does not provide a final adventure after the return to Uruk. Its vocabulary and structure differ from the standard Babylonian epic. Ancient scribes attached it to the collection, but its attachment does not make it a sequel in the same narrative design.

The epic is therefore a layered work. Jeffrey Tigay argued that its development can be traced from earlier Sumerian poems about Bilgames and from Old Babylonian Akkadian versions toward the longer standard Babylonian composition. Andrew George's edition gathers manuscripts from different periods and places, showing how the text was copied, expanded and repaired across centuries. The name Sin-leqi-unninni is associated with the standard Babylonian version in later tradition and manuscript catalogues, but the surviving tablets do not provide a signed author's copy. The epic is better understood as a work shaped by scribal transmission than as a modern book produced at one moment.

The epic did not stay in Mesopotamia. Fragments of Gilgamesh in Akkadian have been found at Hattusa, the Hittite capital in Anatolia, together with Hittite and Hurrian versions of the story, and a small tablet from Megiddo in the Levant carries lines from the epic's seventh tablet. Other fragments come from Emar in Syria and Ugarit on the Mediterranean coast. These finds, which date to the second half of the second millennium before the common era, show that scribes outside Babylonia copied and translated the story, and that it was being read in several languages long before the library at Nineveh was assembled.

The flood tablet belongs to a wider Mesopotamian tradition. The Sumerian poem about Ziusudra tells of a flood survivor, and the Akkadian Atrahasis epic gives a fuller account of divine decisions, the warning, the boat, the flood and the survivor's special fate. The Gilgamesh version shares major elements with Atrahasis, including the warning through Ea, the construction of the boat, the birds, the offering and the dispute among the gods. Most historians explain the relationship through a shared Mesopotamian literary tradition, with the Gilgamesh compiler adapting material known from Atrahasis. Some scholars discuss direct literary borrowing, while others stress inherited motifs and repeated scribal rewriting.

The flood narrative in Genesis shares several structural features with these Mesopotamian accounts: divine judgment by water, a chosen survivor, a large vessel, animals, birds, a landing place and an offering. The main scholarly positions are not identical. Some historians argue that the biblical account reflects knowledge of Mesopotamian flood traditions, perhaps through forms of contact during the first millennium before the common era. Others emphasize that both traditions reshape older material for different theological and literary purposes. The similarities are substantial, but the stories differ in their divine explanations, measurements, chronology and narrative conclusions.

Irving Finkel's study of a cuneiform tablet concerning the ark adds a Mesopotamian witness to the history of the flood tradition. The tablet describes a circular boat made with a large quantity of rope, a feature different from the rectangular vessel in Gilgamesh Tablet Eleven and Genesis. Finkel identifies its survivor as Atrahasis in the relevant tradition and dates the tablet to the Old Babylonian period, roughly seventeen hundred before the common era, a thousand years before the library of Ashurbanipal. It shows that scribes could preserve more than one form of the ark story; it does not turn every difference among the traditions into a single fixed account.

The modern recovery of the epic began with the excavation of Nineveh. Layard excavated at Kuyunjik in the eighteen forties, and Rassam continued work there. Cuneiform tablets from the library of Ashurbanipal entered European collections. In eighteen seventy two, George Smith recognized a flood narrative among the fragments and read it publicly. His work, published in The Chaldean Account of Genesis in eighteen seventy six, helped establish the importance of the Nineveh tablets and led to further searches for missing pieces. The text was not recovered as one intact manuscript. It was assembled from fragments, duplicates and later comparisons.

Translations have continued to change as new tablets have appeared and damaged passages have been re-edited. Stephanie Dalley presented the epic within a wider collection of Mesopotamian myths. George Smith's early readings were pioneering but depended on a smaller corpus and less developed knowledge of Akkadian. Andrew George's editions and translations use a much larger manuscript base, including fragments from different periods, and identify where the wording is restored rather than directly preserved.

English readers met the epic through a series of translations. Reginald Campbell Thompson published a verse translation in nineteen twenty eight. Alexander Heidel's study of the epic and its parallels with the Old Testament appeared in nineteen forty six, and N. K. Sandars's prose version for Penguin in nineteen sixty brought the poem to a general audience. Andrew George's Penguin translation of nineteen ninety nine, built on his own edition of the cuneiform, is the one most readers use now.

The historical record establishes a long process: Sumerian stories about Bilgames, Akkadian retellings, a standard Babylonian composition, copies in the library of Ashurbanipal, excavation at Nineveh, decipherment in the nineteenth century and continued reconstruction from cuneiform fragments. The epic ends with Gilgamesh back at the walls of Uruk, and its tablets survive because scribes copied them across many centuries.
