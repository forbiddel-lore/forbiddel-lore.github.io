Full script

The wind on the summit of Mount Hermon had snow in it. It came down off the ridges in the dark and moved across the bare rock, and where it passed the loose stones ticked and settled. Far below, in the black folds of the land, a few lamps burned in the houses of men. The book says it was in the days of Jared. It gives no hour. But a deed like this is done at night.

Hermon is the great mountain at the northern edge of the country, white at the top through much of the year. From the valleys to the south a man could see it every morning, a pale shape standing over the land like a watchtower. Nobody looking up at it that night saw anything. The Watchers had been looking down for a long time. This night they were coming.

They came onto the summit one after another, until the whole crown of the mountain was crowded with them. Two hundred. The book calls them the angels, the children of the heaven, and it calls them the Watchers, because for a long age their work had been to watch. They had watched men multiply. They had watched daughters born to men, beautiful and comely. And the angels saw them and lusted, and said to one another, "Come, let us choose us wives from among the children of men and beget us children."

Not all of them spoke so lightly. Semjaza, who was their leader, stood among them and said what none of the others had said. "I fear ye will not indeed agree to do this deed, and I alone shall have to pay the penalty of a great sin."

Listen to what he is afraid of. He does not say the deed is wrong. He does not say it is forbidden. He says that if the others lose their nerve, he will be the one left holding the guilt. And the answer comes from all of them at once, like one breath in the cold. "Let us all swear an oath, and all bind ourselves by mutual imprecations not to abandon this plan but to do this thing."

So they swore. Two hundred voices, one after another, each calling down a curse on all the rest, and on himself, should he ever turn back. By the end there was no angel on that mountain who could go home without damning every other. The book gives the mountain its name from that hour. They called it Hermon, it says, because they had sworn and bound themselves by mutual imprecations upon it. In Hebrew the name sounds like the word for a ban, a thing devoted and cursed.

Then they went down. Down through the thin air and the cold, past the snowline, toward the valleys where the fires were, carrying the oath behind them like a weight. And they did not go down empty-handed.

Most people who know this story know it as a story of angels, women and giants, and then of a Flood that washed the whole thing away. The Book of Enoch tells all of that, and tells it at length. But it tells something the four verses of Genesis never mention. These angels came down as teachers.

The book names them, one by one, and beside each name it sets the art that angel gave to the children of men: how to forge the sword, how to paint the eye, how to cut the root, how to read the moon. And the book is plain about what it thinks of these lessons. They are not the gifts of a golden age. They are the cause. The weapons, the jewels, the charms and the star charts are, in the logic of this book, the reason the earth cried out and the reason the water was sent. Civilization, as it first appears on these pages, is a stolen inheritance, handed over by traitors, and every gift in it comes with a price that somebody else pays.

Here is how we will go. In chapter eight there is a short list, nine teachers in a few verses, each with an art. We will take them one at a time, in their order, each with whatever story the book gives him. Then, sixty one chapters further on, in the part of the book called the Parables, there is a second roll of the fallen. Different names. Different gifts. Most readers never find it. We will take that one in order too.

And one art from that second list I am holding back for the end. It is the one the book says human beings were never created for. By the book's own judgement it is the worst of them all, and it is the art this very book depends on to exist. I say it once, and now we begin, on the mountain, with the names of the two hundred.

Before the Watchers go down, the book stops to take their names, the way a clerk stops at the gate of a camp to count an army.

Two hundred of them, and each ten under a chief, so that the roll runs to twenty captains. Semjaza stands at the head. After him come names that the Ethiopian copyists spelled in their own way: Kokabel, Ezeqeel, Baraqijal, Armaros, Sariel, and others besides. Keep those few in your ear. Each of them is carrying something down the mountain, and each will have a verse of his own before the story is done.

Then they go down, and chapter seven, which is short and has no mercy in it, tells what followed.

Each of them chose one woman for himself, and went in to her, and defiled himself with her. They taught the women charms and enchantments, and the cutting of roots, and made them acquainted with plants. The women conceived, and they bore giants. Great giants, the book says, whose height was three thousand ells. An ell is the length of a man's forearm. Three thousand of them is a height beyond measuring, and that is the point of the number. The children of this marriage were not the right size for the world they were born into. Genesis remembers them by a Hebrew name, the Nephilim, and the King James Bible renders it simply as giants.

They were hungry. That is the next thing the book tells us. The giants consumed all the acquisitions of men: the grain, the flocks, the work of the hands. Think of a village at the end of summer, the threshing floors heaped, the storehouses sealed, and then something comes down out of the hills that can eat the whole harvest in a night. The men worked, and the giants consumed the work. And when men could no longer sustain them, the book says, the giants turned against them and devoured mankind.

The sin does not stop with men. It spreads outward to everything that lives. The giants sinned against the birds and the beasts, against the reptiles and the fish. They devoured one another's flesh and drank the blood. And then comes the sentence that closes the chapter, the sentence the rest of the book leans on. "Then the earth laid accusation against the lawless ones."

Not the men. The earth. The ground itself bringing a charge, the way a victim brings a charge. Hold that image: a lawsuit brought by soil and blood, and heard, as we will see, in heaven.

Now a pause, because you should know how you are able to hear this at all.

The man at the center of the book is Enoch. Genesis gives him a handful of verses and a vanishing. "And Enoch walked with God: and he was not; for God took him." That is Genesis, chapter five, verse twenty four. Walked with God. Was not. Readers in the last centuries before the common era wanted to know where he went and what he saw, and the book that carries his name is the answer. It is his own report, in his own voice, of a man carried up through the heavens and shown what the angels had done. He is the son of Jared, the father of Methuselah, and the great-grandfather of Noah.

It is not one book but five, gathered under his name. The Book of the Watchers, the first thirty six chapters, is the one we are standing in. After it come the Parables, the Astronomical Book, the Dream Visions, and the Epistle of Enoch.

And then, for most of the world, it vanished. In the Greek and Latin churches the book faded out of use, and only quotations and scraps remained. But in the highlands of Ethiopia the church kept it whole. It was translated into Ge'ez, the old sacred language of that church, and copied by hand onto parchment, generation after generation, among the books of its scripture.

In seventeen seventy three a Scot named James Bruce came home to Europe. He had gone up into Ethiopia to look for the source of the Blue Nile, and he came back with Ethiopian manuscripts, and among them were copies of the Book of Enoch. One went to the king's library in Paris. One went to the Bodleian at Oxford. A book the West had lost was sitting on a shelf in the West, and nobody could read it, because nobody in Europe could read Ge'ez. It took until eighteen twenty one for Richard Laurence, an Oxford professor who later became an archbishop, to publish the first English translation. In nineteen twelve R. H. Charles produced the English that I am quoting through this whole episode.

Then came the caves above the Dead Sea. At Qumran, in the cave the scholars number Four, among thousands of torn fragments, there were pieces of the Book of Enoch in Aramaic: the Watchers, the giants, the Astronomical Book. They had been copied in the last centuries before the common era, more than a thousand years before any Ethiopian copy. The story of Semjaza's oath was being copied by hand beside the Dead Sea before there was a New Testament.

And the New Testament knows it. In the letter of Jude, verse fourteen: "And Enoch also, the seventh from Adam, prophesied of these, saying, Behold, the Lord cometh with ten thousands of his saints, To execute judgment upon all, and to convince all that are ungodly among them of all their ungodly deeds which they have ungodly committed, and of all their hard speeches which ungodly sinners have spoken against him." Those words stand in the Book of Enoch, chapter one, verse nine. A letter in the New Testament quotes the book and calls the speaker a prophet.

So that is the book, and that is how it came down through the hands that carried it. Now back to the roll of the two hundred, and to a name that is not among the twenty captains, a name that opens chapter eight and goes first. Azazel. And what Azazel brought up out of the ground was iron.

Before the sword there was only a hillside, a stain of green in the rock, and a fire.

Men had seen such stains for a long time. They had cracked the stone, carried a piece home, and wondered at it. What they did not have was the art: the heat, the patience, the secret order of things that turns a green stone into a bright liquid and the liquid into an edge. The book says that art came down with the Watchers, and that the first to give it was Azazel.

Chapter eight opens on him. "And Azazel taught men to make swords, and knives, and shields, and breastplates, and made known to them the metals of the earth and the art of working them."

Read the order of that verse slowly. First the things that cut: swords, knives. Then the things that stop the cut: shields, breastplates. Once one man has a blade, every other man needs a shield, and then the first man needs a better blade. The verse holds a whole arms race in one breath.

And then the root of it, said almost in passing. He made known to them the metals of the earth and the art of working them. This is not only weapons. This is the knowledge of the ground itself, what lies in it, what can be got out of it, and what the fire will do.

Think what that meant in the world where this book was read. Bronze is copper joined with tin, and tin was scarce, carried over great distances by those who could afford the carrying. Bronze meant a palace to gather the metal, and workshops in the king's service, and soldiers armed from the king's stores. Later came iron, harder to work, but the ore lay in more places than tin ever did, and once the art was learned, edges came into more hands than any king could count. Either way the lesson was the same. Whoever held the metal held the army. Whoever held the army held the people. A king was buried with his dagger beside him, and the dagger said what the king had been.

Picture the first morning it changed. A town on a river, a watchman on the wall, and down the road a column of raiders coming in the early light. Their spearheads catch the sun. Their shields are rimmed with metal. The townsmen go out with the tools of the field, with sticks fire-hardened and blades of flint, and they hear the sound that is new in the world, the ring of a hard edge on a soft one. After that morning no town was safe without a smith.

The Bible keeps its own memory of this. In the days of Saul, the first book of Samuel says, "there was no smith found throughout all the land of Israel," and the reason is given in plain words: the Philistines said, "Lest the Hebrews make them swords or spears." A whole people disarmed by keeping the forge out of their hands. The one who held the art held the land.

Genesis has a different account of where the art began. It places it with a man, Tubalcain, in the line of Cain, "an instructer of every artificer in brass and iron." Genesis chapter four, verse twenty two, gives you a craftsman and a family. The Book of Enoch gives you a fallen angel and a crime. Two stories of the same fire. In one, the smith is a man who learned. In the other, the smith is a pupil of the Watchers.

And think of the prophet Isaiah's great dream of peace. "They shall beat their swords into plowshares, and their spears into pruninghooks: nation shall not lift up sword against nation, neither shall they learn war any more." Read it again with Azazel in your mind. The highest hope in the whole prophetic tradition is that the nations will unlearn it. It does not say that they will stop. It says they will not learn war any more. The prophet is asking for Azazel's lesson to be taken back.

The book does not pretend the lesson was entirely unwanted. In chapter nine, when the archangels bring their complaint to heaven, they say of Azazel that he "hath taught all unrighteousness on earth and revealed the eternal secrets which were preserved in heaven, which men were striving to learn." Those last words matter. Men were striving to learn. They were reaching for these things, and a teacher was waiting who would hand them over before their time and without their cost. The crime is not that men wanted metal. The crime is that the secrets of heaven were given away by one who had no right to give them.

And when the sentence is finally spoken in chapter ten, it does not fall first on Semjaza, who led the Watchers down. It falls on Azazel. "The whole earth has been corrupted through the works that were taught by Azazel: to him ascribe all sin." All sin. The one who taught takes more of the blame than the one who led. In this book the cause of corruption is not desire alone. It is instruction.

That name was not invented for the book. Azazel is the name in Leviticus, chapter sixteen, where on the day of atonement two goats are brought forward. Verse eight: "And Aaron shall cast lots upon the two goats; one lot for the LORD, and the other lot for the scapegoat." In the Hebrew, the word the King James renders scapegoat is the name Azazel, and the goat is sent out into the wilderness bearing the sins of the people. Readers have long heard an echo. The one to whom the Book of Enoch ascribes all sin is a figure of the empty desert, and in chapter ten, as we will see, that is exactly where he is sent.

But the verse that gave him swords is not finished. The same sentence that hands men the sword goes on to hand them something that looks, at first, like its opposite. Bracelets. Ornaments. Paint for the eyes.

In a courtyard in the morning, before the heat, someone sits down with a flat stone and a pinch of dark powder.

It could be Egypt, where such stones lie in the graves with their little pots. It could be Mesopotamia, where queens went down into the earth wearing more gold than a village could earn in a century. The hand is steady. A thin black line is laid along the edge of the eyelid, the eye widens and darkens, and the face that looks out is not quite the face that went in. It looks like a person who knows who she is, and who expects to be obeyed.

That is the second half of Azazel's verse. "And bracelets, and ornaments, and the use of antimony, and the beautifying of the eyelids, and all kinds of costly stones, and all colouring tinctures."

Go through it piece by piece. Bracelets and ornaments: worked metal again, the same metals of the earth, hammered thin and bent into shapes that did not cut but gleamed. Antimony: a dark mineral ground fine, the old paint for the eye, valued against sun and dust and valued more for how it made a face look. The beautifying of the eyelids: the book says it twice over, as if the author could see the black line and wished to be sure no listener missed it.

Then the costly stones. The deep blue stone prized in the cities of the Euphrates was carried there from the mountains of what is now Afghanistan, a journey of months. The red stones came from farther still. In the royal graves of Ur, from about twenty five hundred before the common era, women lay in headdresses of gold leaf and beads of that blue and red, and attendants lay down beside them to serve in the next world. That is what costly stones were. They were a way of carrying distance on the body, and a way of showing that a household could command it.

Then the colouring tinctures: the dyes. Cloth drawn through a vat the colour of a sunset in a storm, the purple that came out of the shells of the sea so dear that it became the colour of kings. The verse closes the list there. Swords at the start, purple at the end.

So here is the question the verse puts to us. Why does the book count the paint on a face among the weapons?

Because it is the same verse. Because it is the same earth. The stones and the metals that make a blade are the stones and the metals that make a bracelet. The same hand that learned the art of working them for war learned the art of working them for show. And because they are the same kind of power.

A sword says: fear me. A jewel says: want me. Both are ways of standing out in front of other people and making them respond. Both turn a person into something to be seen and ranked and taken. The sword rules by what it threatens. The ornament rules by what it promises. One takes a body by force and the other takes a gaze. The Watchers had come down from heaven to take wives, and they taught the arts of taking, and they taught them in two forms: the edge that frightens, and the shine that draws.

The Bible shows how deep that memory ran. When Jehu came to Jezreel, the second book of Kings says, "Jezebel heard of it; and she painted her face, and tired her head, and looked out at a window." Her last act of power was the eyes. And Isaiah, chapter three, the prophet turning on the daughters of Zion, walks through their wardrobe like a man taking inventory of a house he means to burn. "The chains, and the bracelets, and the mufflers." The list goes on for verses.

Now see what the book says followed. Chapter eight, verse two. "And there arose much godlessness, and they committed fornication, and they were led astray, and became corrupt in all their ways." The sequence is plain. Swords. Shields. Metals. Bracelets. Paint. Stones. Dyes. Then godlessness, then fornication, then men led astray, then corruption in all their ways. In the book's own mind the weapons and the jewelry are links in one chain, and the chain ends in a ruined people.

Be careful what the verse does and does not say. It does not say that women wore these things and men fell. It says the angel taught, and people were led astray, and the corruption was in all their ways. Later readers sharpened it. In Carthage, around the year two hundred, the church writer Tertullian took up this very passage against the fashion of the women of his city, and argued that the fallen angels had given such arts to women. That is his reading, not the book's. The book itself, speaking to the Watchers in chapter sixteen, says that through these mysteries "women and men" work much evil on the earth. The blame stays with the teachers. The ones who do the wearing and the wielding are the ones who have been taught.

That is the second half of Azazel's lesson, and with it Azazel is done. The next two names on the list do not teach anything you can pick up in your hand. Semjaza, the leader, who feared he would pay alone, teaches something stranger. And his lesson begins not in the forge or the courtyard but at the edge of a field, with a knife, a plant, and something spoken over it.

A woman goes out at dusk to the edge of the field, where the cultivated ground gives way to scrub and stone. She kneels. She has come for something that grows there, and she knows what she wants and where it hides. When she comes back her hands are stained and she is saying something under her breath.

The book does not tell us that scene. It tells us what the Watchers taught, and the teaching is spoken of in two places. In chapter seven, as the angels take their wives, they teach them charms and enchantments, and the cutting of roots, and make them acquainted with plants. And in chapter eight, verse three, the verse is more exact about who taught what: "Semjaza taught enchantments, and root-cuttings, Armaros the resolving of enchantments."

Two names, two arts, and one trade between them.

Look first at what the verse puts side by side. Enchantments and root-cuttings. Words and plants. In the world of the ancient Near East, those were not two different crafts. The one who knew which plants healed and which killed was the same person who knew what to say over them. The Greeks had a word for the root-cutters, the people who made a living in the hills with a blade and a basket, and another word, pharmakon, that meant a drug and a poison and a charm all at once. A single small plant could turn a fever or end a life, depending on who held it and how much and why. The Watchers did not make that knowledge. They revealed it. And the book is not describing the plants. It is describing the power over a neighbour's body that this knowledge gave.

I will not tell what the text does not tell. It gives no recipes and I will not make any. What it gives is a sequence. First the knowledge of what lies hidden in the field. Then the words that make it do more than it would do alone. And then, in the mouth of the second angel, the undoing.

Armaros, the resolving of enchantments. Think about what that means. The Watchers did not only teach men and women how to lay a curse. They sent a second teacher to show them how to lift one. The same heavens that supplied the harm supplied the remedy, and both arrived together. Here is the oldest shape of a protection racket: fear is sold in the morning and relief in the evening, and in between there is a person with a reputation who stands at the door of every house.

This is why the book reads the arts as corruption rather than neutral craft. A village in which everyone knows that someone could ruin a neighbour with a root and a murmur is a village in which no one eats quite safely. Illness becomes an accusation. A death in the night begins a search for the hand behind it. Once the art exists, it need not even be used. It is enough that it might have been.

The Bible's law knew exactly this fear. Exodus, chapter twenty two, verse eighteen: "Thou shalt not suffer a witch to live." Deuteronomy gives a longer catalogue of those who practise divination, enchantment and charming and says none of them shall be found among the people. The old law is not describing a harmless folk custom. It is describing something it considers a weapon.

Notice the irony in the one who teaches it. Semjaza stood on the summit of Hermon and spoke the sentence that started the oath: "I alone shall have to pay the penalty of a great sin." He was the leader who feared the cost. And on the other side of the oath, in the book's list, it is Semjaza who teaches the arts of the unseen, the arts that make a person afraid of a thing he cannot see. The one who was afraid is the one who teaches fear.

And yet notice also that of all the Watchers' teachings, these are the ones the book connects most closely to the women and the marriages. Chapter seven puts the charms and roots in the very moment of the unions. It is the teaching of a household, passed in the dark between a husband who is not of this world and a wife who has been given his secrets. The sword and the jewel belonged to the public square. These belonged to the bedchamber and the kitchen, and the ill they did went unseen.

Think what a reader of the book in its own time would have thought on hearing these verses. A healer might be a mother or a neighbour. A person with a pouch of dried roots might be the village's only help in sickness. The book draws a hard line through that world and says that the line has an origin: a night, a mountain, and a teacher who should not have taught. Everything that looks like help may have the Watchers' fingerprints on it.

So far every art we have met has been a thing of the earth. The ground gave the metal. The ground gave the stones, the dyes, the dark mineral for the eye. The ground gave the root. And a book which began with the earth laying its accusation has now spent three teachers on the earth's own treasures.

But the Watchers were angels. Their home was not the ground. They had lived their whole existence under the stars, and they knew the sky from inside. The next six names on the list do not reach down into the soil. They reach up. And what they hand to men is the heavens.

On a flat roof in Babylon, a man waits in the last light with a clay tablet and a reed. He is a scribe of the temple. The sun has gone. He is watching the western sky for the thin sliver of a new moon, and when he sees it, or fails to see it, the month will begin, and a ritual will move, and a record will be written. He has done this since boyhood. He knows what the omens say about each shape in the sky, because the omens have been written down, tablet after tablet, in a series of some seventy, kept by scribes in Babylon and Nineveh long before the Book of Enoch was composed.

That is the world in which a Jewish writer, in the third century before the common era, sat down to tell the story of the Watchers. Everywhere around him the sky was being read. And he says: who taught that?

Chapter eight, verse three, gives the answer in six names. "Baraqijal astrology, Kokabel the constellations, Ezeqeel the knowledge of the clouds, Araqiel the signs of the earth, Shamsiel the signs of the sun, and Sariel the course of the moon."

Take them as the book gives them. Baraqijal teaches astrology: the reading of human fortunes in the positions of the stars. Kokabel teaches the constellations, and his name carries the word for star in it. The book did not need to explain; the name did. Ezeqeel teaches the knowledge of the clouds: the signs of weather, the face of the sky before rain. Araqiel teaches the signs of the earth, the omens in tremors and landscape and the ground itself. Shamsiel teaches the signs of the sun, and his name carries the word for the sun. Sariel teaches the course of the moon.

Notice the word that returns again and again. Signs. The Watchers did not give men the sky for its own sake. They gave them what the sky said about them. The signs of the earth, the signs of the sun: a language written across the heavens, and a promise that whoever learned it could know what was coming and perhaps turn it.

Take the whole catalogue together and look at it with a modern eye. Weather. Geology. Mapping the stars. The cycles of the sun and the moon. These are not the arts of a wicked people. They are the first subjects of every science that came after, and the book has chosen to place them in the mouths of fallen angels. If that sounds extreme, remember that the Watchers' list as a whole is a catalogue of what a civilization is built from: the metal, the ornament, the drug, the omen. The author has taken the foundations of the world he lived in and shown them to us as stolen goods.

And then the verse that follows. It is short, and it is the hinge of the whole chapter. "And as men perished, they cried, and their cry went up to heaven."

There is no sky teacher in that sentence. There is no art. It is the sound of the result. Men were perishing, because of everything the Watchers had taught them, the blades, the poisons, the curses, the fortunes. And they cried. And the cry did not fall on the ground and die. It went up to heaven, through the same sky the Watchers had once looked out from, and it reached someone.

But we should not take from this that the book is hostile to the heavens or to those who study them. Far from it. A large part of the Book of Enoch is devoted to the heavens, and all of it is given as lawful knowledge. The Astronomical Book, chapters seventy two to eighty two, is a long tour of the sun, the moon and the stars, and it is led by an angel: Uriel, the same Uriel we will meet in a moment among the four who hear the cry. Uriel shows Enoch the gates through which the sun rises and sets. He shows him the courses of the moon, the order of the winds, the seasons. And he gives him a calendar, a year of three hundred sixty four days, in four seasons, so that the feasts may fall at their right times and the days of the holy year keep their order.

Chapter eighty adds a warning. In the days of the sinners, it says, the order of the heavens will be disturbed. The sinners will go astray concerning the stars, and the whole order of them will be hidden from the thoughtless. They will mistake the stars for gods.

So the book draws a line through the sky. On one side the knowledge that is given: the true calendar, the steady order of the lights, revealed to a righteous man by a faithful angel, so that people may keep the times of worship. On the other side the knowledge that is taken: the omens and the fortunes, taught by traitors, so that people may read their fate in the stars and kneel to them. The same heavens, the same sun and moon. The difference is who teaches, and what the teaching is for. One reveals an order to be obeyed. The other pretends to reveal a destiny to be feared.

That is the whole first list. Nine teachers. Azazel, who taught the sword and the paint. Semjaza and Armaros, who taught the charm and its undoing. Baraqijal, Kokabel, Ezeqeel, Araqiel, Shamsiel and Sariel, who taught the sky. And the sentence the list ends on is not a lesson at all. It is a sound.

Somewhere above the clouds, at the gate of heaven, four angels were standing at the edge of the sanctuary and looking down. And the sound reached them.

Four of them stood at the edge of heaven. Michael, Uriel, Raphael and Gabriel.

Chapter nine opens on them looking down from the sanctuary of heaven at the earth. What they saw was blood. Much blood being poured out upon the ground, and all manner of lawlessness being done on it. And they heard the cry. Not one voice but the whole sound of it, the dead and the dying, the souls of men making their suit at the gates of heaven, asking the holy ones to carry their case before the Most High.

So they went in before the throne. They addressed God by the old titles, Lord of lords, God of gods, King of kings, and they said nothing in the world is hidden from him. And then they made their report. They did not summarize. They named names.

"Thou seest what Azazel hath done, who hath taught all unrighteousness on earth and revealed the eternal secrets which were preserved in heaven, which men were striving to learn." And Semjaza, the one to whom authority had been given over his fellows. They told how the Watchers had gone in to the daughters of men, lain with them, defiled themselves, and revealed to them all kinds of sins. They told of the giants and the blood. And they ended on the cry itself, that the lamentations of the dead had gone up and could not cease, because of the lawless deeds done upon the earth.

The answer comes in chapter ten, and it comes in the form of orders, issued one after another like a general dividing a campaign among his captains.

First, a warning to a man. The Most High sent word to the son of Lamech: Noah. He was to be told to hide himself, and shown the end that was approaching, that the whole earth would be destroyed and a deluge was about to come upon it. He was told how to escape, that his seed might be preserved for all the generations. That is the whole of Noah's part in the Watchers' story. He is the one man who is warned.

Second, Raphael. The book gives his commission in words I will quote, because they are the most terrible in the chapter. "Bind Azazel hand and foot, and cast him into the darkness." Make an opening in the desert, in a place called Dudael, and cast him in. Place upon him rough and jagged rocks. Cover him with darkness. Let him abide there, and cover his face, so that he may not see light. And on the day of the great judgement, he shall be cast into the fire.

Notice the logic of it. Azazel taught the world to cut and to adorn. He is sentenced to a place with no light, where he can see nothing, not a gem and not a blade, with the rocks pressing in on him, rough and jagged, where every surface is an edge. The punishment is a parody of his own gifts.

Raphael has a second task as well: to heal the earth. To proclaim the healing of the earth, so that the plague the angels have wrought may be healed and the children of men may not perish through all the secret things the Watchers disclosed. The earth that laid the accusation is to be mended.

Third, Gabriel, and the order is brutal. Proceed against the children of fornication, the offspring of the Watchers, and destroy them. Send them one against the other, so that they destroy each other in battle. For length of days shall they not have. The giants who devoured mankind are to be turned on one another until none is left.

Fourth, Michael. Go, bind Semjaza and his associates who have united themselves with women. When their sons have slain one another, and when they have seen the destruction of those they loved, bind them fast for seventy generations in the valleys of the earth, till the day of their judgement, till the judgement that is for ever and ever is consummated. Then they shall be led off to the abyss of fire, to the torment and the prison in which they shall be confined for ever.

Seventy generations. The book makes them watch. These are the Watchers, and their punishment is to see everything they loved destroyed by its own hand, and then to wait.

The story could end there. It does not. Chapters twelve to sixteen turn back to Enoch himself, and the book does something extraordinary. It sends a man to speak to the fallen.

Enoch, the book says, had been hidden, and his dwelling was with the Watchers and with the holy ones. And the Watchers, the faithful ones, called to him and told him to go and speak to the others, who had left the high heaven. They called him a name. "Enoch, thou scribe of righteousness." Remember that title. Go, they said, and tell the Watchers they have wrought great destruction on the earth, and they shall have no peace nor forgiveness of sin.

He went, and the fallen Watchers heard him, and they were afraid. And they asked him for something. They asked him to write a petition on their behalf, that they might have forgiveness, and to read it before the Lord of heaven.

So Enoch wrote it. He sat down by the waters of Dan, in the land of Dan, south of Hermon, on the west of it, and he read their petition until he fell asleep. Then came the visions. A house of fire, walls of crystal, a throne like ice. In chapter fifteen comes the answer.

God did not accept the petition. The reply is in the form of an inversion. You sent a man to intercede for you, the Lord says to the Watchers. It should be you interceding for men, not men for you. You were spiritual, holy, living the eternal life, and you went to defile yourselves with the daughters of men and beget flesh and blood. Therefore I gave them wives, so that men might bear children. But you were of heaven.

And then the sentence that explains where the demons of the world come from. The giants, who are born of spirit and flesh, shall be called evil spirits upon the earth. When their bodies die, the spirits go out from them and wander, and they do not die, and they afflict men and women and children. This is the origin of the unclean spirits, in a book that never uses the word for demon in the way later centuries did. They are the leftover ghosts of a violence the Watchers began.

In chapter sixteen the Lord names the real fault. You were in heaven, he says, but its mysteries had not yet been revealed to you, and you knew a worthless one. This you made known to the women in the hardness of your hearts, and through that mystery women and men work much evil on the earth. Say to them, therefore: you have no peace.

A worthless mystery. A half-knowledge, snatched from the sky before its time. The list in chapter eight, all those arts, is reduced here to a single phrase. It was not wisdom. It was a fragment passed off as the whole.

So that is the judgement. Azazel in the dark. Semjaza bound. The giants turned on one another. A man in a boat, or about to be. Everything is settled. Except that the book is not done counting. Sixty one chapters further on, the roll of the fallen is read out again.

The scene has changed. The voice is not Enoch's now, and the place is not Hermon. We are deep in the middle of the book, in a part that calls itself the Parables, and a long vision of judgement has just come to its end. And then, in chapter sixty nine, another roll is read out.

"And behold the names of those angels." So it begins.

The Parables are chapters thirty seven to seventy one, a book within the book, and they are very different in tone from the Watchers' story. They are visions of a figure called the Son of Man, the Chosen One, who sits on a throne of glory and judges the kings and the mighty of the earth. The angels of punishment appear in it, and a long vision of Noah, and the coming of the Flood. And in the midst of that, chapter sixty nine returns to the fallen angels, as if the author wanted to set the record straight about who they were and what each had done.

The roll begins with names, and some are familiar. Semjaza is first. Kokabel is among them. Azazel is there, and Armaros. But others are strange, names that do not appear anywhere in chapter six, and the book tells us they are the chiefs of the angels, and the names of their leaders over hundreds, over fifties and over tens. It is the same army, counted by a different clerk.

And then, out of the roll, the book picks out five and tells what each did. The first is named Jeqon, and he is the one who led the sons of God astray and brought them down to the earth, through the daughters of men. The second is named Asbeel, who gave the holy sons of God evil counsel, so that they defiled their bodies with the daughters of men. These two are tempters, not teachers. They are the ones behind the oath.

The third is named Gadreel, and with him the list becomes a list of arts again. "It is he who showed the children of men all the blows of death." And he led astray Eve.

Pause on that. Eve. The Watchers, in the first list, come down in the days of Jared, six generations after Adam. But here is a fallen angel who is credited with the deceiving of the first woman. The book is stretching the story back into the garden, placing the corrupter of Eden among the same company as the corrupters of Hermon. Genesis, chapter three, verse thirteen, has the woman say: "The serpent beguiled me, and I did eat." The second list gives that serpent a name among the fallen, and makes him the first of the teachers of death.

And Gadreel's gifts are weapons. He showed the children of men the shield, and the coat of mail, and the sword for battle, and all the weapons of death. Read that against Azazel's verse. Swords, shields, breastplates. The same gifts, credited to another angel. And the book adds a sentence that makes the line run forward to the listener's own day. From his hand they have proceeded against those who dwell on the earth from that day and for evermore.

Now the fourth name on the roll is Penemue. I am passing over him. I said I would hold something for the end, and he is the one. We go on to the fifth.

The fifth is named Kasdeja, and what he taught is a darker list than anything in the first. He showed the children of men all the wicked smitings of spirits and demons. He showed the smiting of the embryo in the womb, so that it may pass away. He showed the smitings of the soul, and the bites of the serpent, and the smitings that befall through the noonday heat, which the text ties to a son of the serpent whose name is Taba'et.

Hear what kind of list it is. These are not crafts. They are harms. The first list taught men to make a thing. Kasdeja's list teaches men to make a victim. The unborn child, the traveller in the heat, the man bitten on the path. A catalogue of the ways a life can be ended without a weapon and without a witness, and, in the book's mind, every one of them a gift.

And then the text does something I did not expect. It does not leave Kasdeja there. It calls him the chief of the oath. This is the oath he showed the holy ones when he dwelt high above in glory, and the book gives its name, which is Biqa. Kasdeja asked Michael to show him the hidden name, so that he might say it in the oath, so that those who had revealed all that was in secret to the children of men might tremble before the name and the oath.

And the book gives us the power of that oath. It is the oath by which the heaven was suspended before the world was, by which the earth was founded on the waters, by which the sea was fenced with sand against the time of its anger, by which the sun and moon keep their courses and never depart from their order. A third oath, bigger than the first two, the oath that holds creation together, placed in the hand of Michael.

Think of the pattern. On Hermon, two hundred angels swore an oath of mutual curses to do a thing that would unravel the world. Here, the book says, there is an oath of another kind, and by it the world was held together before there was a world. The oath binds. The question the book keeps asking is: to what.

Why does this list differ from the first? The answer is in the history of the book. The Parables are a later layer. Most scholars place them around the first century before or after the common era, perhaps two centuries after the Book of the Watchers. And of all the five books of Enoch, it is the Parables alone that were not found among the fragments at Qumran. J. T. Milik, who edited the Aramaic fragments from Cave Four, found pieces of the Watchers, the Astronomical Book, the Dream Visions and the Epistle, but nothing of the Parables. They come to us from the Ethiopian copies alone.

So the second list is the work of a later hand, drawing on the old story, but with different names and a different emphasis. What the later writer cared about was not metal or paint. What he cared about was death, and the knowledge that brings it: the blow, the bite, the smiting of the womb. And hidden in the middle of that roll, between the weapons and the poisons, was a fourth name that he treated differently from all the rest. Penemue. He taught no craft with a body, no blade, no root, no star. He taught something that does not look like a weapon at all. And it is the one thing in the entire book the author says men were never made for.

Somewhere in a room lit by one flame, a man bends over a sheet of prepared skin with a reed pen in his hand. The ink is black, ground from soot and gum, and it beads at the tip of the reed. He is copying a book. It is the Book of Enoch. He has reached chapter sixty nine, and his hand does not shake, and the line he is writing is a line about the sin of what he is doing.

The fourth name on the roll is Penemue.

"And the fourth was named Penemue: he taught the children of men the bitter and the sweet, and he taught them all the secrets of their wisdom."

The bitter and the sweet. It is a phrase that can mean taste and can mean more: the good and the harm of things, the knowledge of what each substance does, the sorting of the world into what helps and what hurts. All the secrets of their wisdom. Not one art but all the secrets, the whole stock of what the wise know, in one hand.

And then the verse that is the reason this episode exists.

"And he instructed mankind in writing with ink and paper, and thereby many sinned from eternity to eternity and until this day."

Writing. Ink and paper. Not the sword and not the poison. The art by which a thing can be set down and kept. And the book does not hesitate to say what it thinks. Many sinned, and they sin to this day.

Stay on it a moment. All the other arts have victims you can see. The sword leaves a body. The root leaves a corpse in a bed. The smiting of the womb leaves an empty cradle. But writing leaves nothing. A line of ink on a sheet of skin. And the book says that this, of all the Watchers' gifts, is the one that brought the most sin across the longest time.

Then it explains itself, in lines that deserve to be heard in full.

"For men were not created for such a purpose, to give confirmation to their good faith with pen and ink."

Hear the word confirmation. Good faith. This is the language of the oath. Before writing, a man's word was a thing he stood behind with his own body and his own name, with his face in front of the witness and the curse on his own head if he lied. The Watchers swore on Hermon with their mouths. They called the curses down on themselves aloud. But after Penemue, a man could put his good faith on a sheet and let the sheet speak for him. A contract. A seal. A treaty. A debt. A promise could be signed by someone who was not there, and enforced on someone who was not there, long after the faces had changed. The book says that men were not made to hold each other to account that way.

And then comes the line that gives the reason.

"For men were created exactly like the angels, to the intent that they should continue pure and righteous, and death, which destroys everything, would not have taken hold of them, but through this their knowledge they are perishing."

Created exactly like the angels. Not below them. Not lesser. Human beings, in the book's picture, were made to stand in the same purity as the heavenly host, and death was no part of the design. It took hold of them by a door, and the door was knowledge, and the last knowledge on the roll, the one the author comes to as the heaviest, is the knowledge that fixes all the others.

Think why that might be. Take any of the Watchers' arts. The smith's craft dies with the smith unless someone watches and learns, and a hundred things can go wrong in the handing on. The charm is only as good as the memory of the one who says it. The fortunes of the stars are lost when the astrologer dies. But once there is writing, nothing dies. The art of the sword can be set down and sent across the sea. The recipe of the poison can lie in a jar for a thousand years and be read again by a stranger. The gifts of the Watchers, which would have been lost with their first pupils, outlive every teacher and every pupil. Writing is the art that makes all the others permanent. The Watchers were bound and sent into the dark, and their lessons were left lying on the page.

There is one more line in that passage, and it is the strangest in the chapter. After saying that men are perishing through this knowledge, the text adds, in the same breath, "and through this power it is consuming me." Who is the me? The voice is not explained. It is a voice that stands inside the judgement and feels the knowledge burning it. Whoever speaks, the speaker is not safe from what is being described.

Now turn the book over and look at the other side.

The Book of Enoch is a book. It was written. It was copied on skin by scribes in Aramaic, passed to the Greek, rendered into Ge'ez, copied again onto parchment by hands in Ethiopian monasteries, carried by James Bruce to Paris and Oxford, translated into English, and read aloud to you now from a page. Everything we have heard tonight survives because someone dipped a pen.

And the book knows it. Remember the title the faithful Watchers gave to Enoch in chapter twelve: scribe of righteousness. When the fallen Watchers wanted forgiveness, they did not ask Enoch to speak for them. They asked him to write. He wrote their petition and read it by the waters of Dan until he fell asleep. In chapter fourteen the book names itself: the book of the words of righteousness. In chapter eighty one, an angel shows Enoch the heavenly tablets and tells him to read what is written there, and he reads the book of all the deeds of mankind. In chapter eighty two he says to his son Methuselah that he has given him books, and tells him to preserve them from his father's hand and deliver them to the generations of the world.

And in chapter one hundred four, near the very end, the book worries about writing again. Sinners, it says, will alter and pervert the words of righteousness, and speak wicked words, and write books concerning their words. But when they write down truthfully all of Enoch's words, in their own languages, and neither change nor lessen anything, then the writing will be a gift to the righteous.

So the book does not condemn the pen as if it did not hold one. It writes about the danger of writing, in writing, and puts the anxiety into the middle of its own pages. The scribe who copied chapter sixty nine in his lamplit room was passing on a verdict against his own craft.

I will say what I think that is. It is a confession. The author of the Parables could have placed the guilty art anywhere in his roll; he put it fourth, between the weapons and the poisons, and gave it the most careful explanation of any entry. He knew that his whole book depended on the thing he was condemning, and he did not look away. A man who writes that men were not created to confirm their good faith in ink has just confirmed his own good faith in ink, and signed the page against himself. The only way to accuse the pen is with the pen. The book is a witness who testifies against the very hand that raised it, and goes on testifying anyway, because the testimony is the only thing that can be saved.

That is the last art, and the worst by the book's own measure. Not the sword that kills one man, but the art that lets the sword outlive the swordsman. Not the poison, but the page on which the poison is explained. The Watchers are chained in the dark. The lessons are on the shelf. And you are sitting, at this moment, inside the oldest answer to the question of what happens when a thing is written down.

Who wrote the Book of the Watchers, and when?

The answer, as far as anyone can say it, is that the Book of the Watchers was composed in Aramaic in the third century before the common era. The copies from Qumran, the fragments from Cave Four, show it already circulating within a few generations after it was composed. Its author is not named. The name on the book is Enoch's, the seventh from Adam, the man who walked with God, but the hand that shaped it was a scribe whose own world was under pressure, and whose answer was to tell the oldest story he could find in a new way.

George Nickelsburg, in his great commentary, reads the Watchers as a mirror. The third century before the common era was the age after Alexander, when his generals divided his empire and fought over it for decades, and the land of the Jews lay in the path of their armies. The giants who devour the harvest and then devour the people, the earth crying out under the blood, the oppressors who are men and not men: Nickelsburg sees there the violent kings of that age, their wars and their weapons, painted large as giants and made the offspring of heaven's rebels. On that reading the Watchers' arts are the arts of empire. Metal, display, magic, the reading of fortune: the toolkit of a king.

Annette Yoshiko Reed has pointed out that the story is not one story but two. There is a tradition of Semjaza, the leader, and a sin of the flesh: the angels, the women, the giants. And there is a tradition of Azazel, and a sin of teaching: the forbidden knowledge, the arts handed over. The Book of the Watchers weaves them together, and the seams show. That is why the first list has Azazel teaching the sword and the paint in one verse and Semjaza teaching enchantments in the next, and why the second list has an entirely separate roll with different names. Loren Stuckenbruck has traced how this one myth, the rebellious angels and their children, became the way a whole stretch of Jewish and Christian thought explained where evil spirits come from.

There are open questions, and they should be said once. Scholars still argue about the date of the Parables, and about whether the two traditions were once separate, and about how much of the Watchers' story is a political allegory and how much a genuine memory of older myth. What no one disputes is the shape of the book on the page. The names and the arts are there, in order, in Aramaic and in Ge'ez.

The seed of it all is four verses in Genesis, chapter six. "And it came to pass, when men began to multiply on the face of the earth, and daughters were born unto them, That the sons of God saw the daughters of men that they were fair; and they took them wives of all which they chose." And in verse four: "There were giants in the earth in those days." That is all. Four verses, and then the Flood. The Book of Enoch is what happens when someone refuses to let those four verses stay short.

The New Testament did not forget. Jude, verse fourteen, we heard, quotes Enoch and calls him a prophet. Verse six of the same letter says of the angels "which kept not their first estate, but left their own habitation," that God "hath reserved in everlasting chains under darkness unto the judgment of the great day." Chains, darkness, the day of judgement: those are the words of Raphael's order against Azazel, carried into a letter that went into the Bible.

Why, then, did most churches set the book aside? The rabbis who fixed the list of Hebrew scripture did not include it. Later Christian teachers in the Greek and Latin world grew uneasy with a story of angels and women and with the claim that a man who lived before the Flood had written a book that survived it, and by the time the lists of the Western churches were settled, Enoch was outside them. In Europe it was lost altogether. But in Ethiopia, the church kept it. The monks who copied it in Ge'ez counted it among the scriptures they carried, and for centuries it was safe in the highlands while the rest of the world forgot it had ever existed.

So come back, one last time, to the summit of Hermon.

The wind still had snow in it. Two hundred angels stood in the dark and swore their oath, a spoken thing, a thing carried on breath. Every curse they called down on one another was said aloud, and when the sound was gone, only the mountain remembered. They went down carrying gifts: the sword, the paint, the root, the star. And the book says that every one of those gifts brought grief. The oath on Hermon was the beginning, but the lessons were the cause.

And of all the lessons, the book picked the quietest. A reed, some soot, a sheet of skin. The Watchers are bound in the dark under rough and jagged rocks, Azazel with his face covered, Semjaza waiting out his seventy generations. But the book that tells of their binding exists because their last gift was never taken back. It is the irony the book itself seems to feel. The art it blames is the art it lives by, and without the art there would be no accusation, no list, no names, and no one now to remember that two hundred angels once stood together on a mountain and swore.

And somebody wrote it down.
