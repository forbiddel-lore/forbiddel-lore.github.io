Enki: The Anunnaki God Whose Advice Cost Humanity Eternal Life

Seven things the Sumerian and Babylonian myths say Enki did: his house in the Abzu at Eridu, Enki and the World Order, the paradise of Dilmun, the night Inanna carried the me to Uruk, the spell of Nudimmud that confused the tongues, the warning to Atrahasis before the Flood, and the advice that made Adapa refuse the bread and water of life.

Full script

The South Wind struck the little boat without warning, and the boat went over.

Adapa had been out on the water since the early light. He was a priest and a sage of Eridu, the old city at the edge of the marshes, and every day of his life he sailed out from its quay to catch fish for the table of the god Ea, whose house stood in the city. He knew the reed beds. He knew the shallows and the channels. He knew what the water looked like when it was safe, and on that morning it lay flat and bright as a polished mirror, and there was nothing in the sky to fear.

Then the wind came up out of the south, and it did not push the boat. It sank it.

The tablet says that Adapa went down into the house of the fish. The water closed over the wise man of Eridu, the one whom a god had taught, and for a moment he was only a body in the dark, among weed and silt and the small cold shapes of the creatures he had come to catch. Then he fought his way up, choking, and dragged himself back into the light. What rose in him was not fear. It was rage.

He turned his face toward the south and he cursed the wind. He said that its wing should be broken. Adapa was not an ordinary fisherman. He was a man whose words carried weight, a man the god of wisdom had trained, and the words were enough. Somewhere above the marsh, the wing of the South Wind cracked.

And the South Wind stopped.

For seven days it did not blow over the land. Seven days of stillness. The reeds stood straight as spears in the channels. Sails hung from their masts like wet cloth. Heat sat on the roofs of Eridu and on the fields beyond, and the air did not move through the streets, and the people of the marsh looked up at a sky that gave no answer and could not say why the world had stopped breathing.

In heaven, someone noticed. Anu, the father of the gods, the king of the sky, asked his vizier why the South Wind had not blown across the land for seven days. And the vizier told him. Adapa, the son of Ea, had broken the wing of the South Wind.

Anu cried out. The tablet says he rose from his throne, the king of heaven on his feet and in a hurry. And he gave an order. Let that man be brought to me.

Stop there.

Hold the picture. A man has broken the wing of a god's own wind, and the father of the gods wants to look him in the face. The man is wise, but he is not immortal. The text is careful to say so. And in a house in Eridu, the god who made him wise has just heard what he did, and is about to give him advice.

That advice is the reason for this episode.

This is the story of Enki, the god the Babylonians called Ea. In the oldest written myths we have, poems pressed into clay more than four thousand years ago in the Sumerian language, Enki is the god of the sweet waters under the earth, the god of craft, of cunning, of the clever way out. And across story after story the same pattern repeats. He is the god who keeps helping humanity. He helps us when the other gods would rather he did not. He tricks the great gods to do it. He tricks his own family. And now and then, he tricks us too.

I am going to tell you seven things that Enki does in the myths. We begin on the day he builds his house in the deep waters, and we end on the day one of his servants is offered eternal life, and refuses it. Each of the seven comes from a real text, told in the order that text gives it. The seventh I am holding back for the end, because it is the most disturbing of them all. It is what Enki told Adapa, in that house in Eridu, just before the man went up to heaven. It may have cost humanity eternal life.

Keep the broken wing in your mind. We will come back to it.

Before there was a city, the Sumerians said, there was a house, and the house stood on water.

The poem that tells of it is called Enki's Journey to Nippur, and it begins with a god building. His name was Enki, which is usually read as lord of the earth, and the poem sometimes calls him Nudimmud, a name that speaks of him as a fashioner of things. His realm was the Abzu. The Abzu was the sweet water that lay beneath the land and rose out of it in springs and marshes and in the great rivers, the fresh water without which no barley would ripen and no city could drink. Salt water was the sea. The Abzu was the other water, the good water, the water that gave life, and the Sumerians believed it had a master who lived inside it.

Enki built his house there, at Eridu, on the edge of the deep. They called it the E-abzu, the house of the Abzu. The poem tells of its building the way the ancients told of any holy house: foundations laid in the water, walls raised and ornamented with silver and lapis lazuli, a shrine so bright that its splendour reached up to the sky. A house like that did not stand beside the water. It stood in it, rooted in the cool dark, and the god who dwelt there ruled from the bottom of the world.

When the house was finished, Enki did what a new householder does. He went to visit his neighbour.

In the Sumerian world the great neighbour was Enlil, the god of wind and storm, who sat at Nippur, in the centre of the land, in a temple called the Ekur, the mountain house. Enlil's word was law among the gods. Decrees were handed down from his city. Enki was the clever one and Enlil was the king, and through the later myths there will be friction between them, sharp friction, over the fate of human beings. But in this poem there is none of that. There is only a god with a new house and a boat.

Enki made the boat ready. He pushed out from Eridu and turned north, and the poem follows him up the river, from the marsh country of the far south, past the reed villages and the field boundaries, into the heart of the plain. The water carried him. Wherever he passed, the poem says, the land took note that the lord of the Abzu was on the move.

At Nippur he steered in to the quay, and Enlil came down to meet him.

Then came the feast. Enlil set a banquet for his guest and called the gods of the land to the table, the gods the Sumerians named the Anunna. They sat, and they ate, and the beer went round, and the poem lingers on the pleasure of it, the hall full of gods with their hearts lifted. Enki sat among them, not as a visitor but as an equal. When the evening was over and the cups were empty, he went back down the river toward his own house in the deep, and the poem closes in praise of the lord of the Abzu.

It is a happy poem. It is the last happy poem we will read about him for some time.

But first, something strange. The Sumerians did not doubt that Enki's house existed. It stood at Eridu. People had been going to it for longer than anyone could count. And in the nineteen forties, the diggers found out how long that was.

Eridu today is a heap of eroded mud brick on the flat southern desert of Iraq, near what used to be the edge of the marshes. Nothing about it looks like the beginning of anything. In nineteen forty six an Iraqi archaeologist, Fuad Safar, began to dig there, and the British archaeologist Seton Lloyd worked beside him. At the centre of the mound stood the ruin of a ziggurat, a stepped tower, raised by a king of Ur some four thousand years ago. The diggers cut down through its platform, expecting to find its core. They found a temple.

Beneath that temple was another. Beneath that, another.

They kept going down, and every level was a shrine. Each had been built on the ruins of the one before, on the very same spot, as if the place itself could not be moved. A temple had stood, fallen, been levelled, and been rebuilt, again and again, so that the mound was a ladder of centuries with the same house at every rung. The lower the diggers went, the smaller the buildings became. At the bottom, resting on clean sand, they reached the first of them: a single small room, with a niche in the wall and an offering table before it. It belonged to the people of the age we call the Ubaid, six thousand years ago and more. Among the earliest temples known in Mesopotamia, and it stood on the exact ground where the Sumerians would later say that Enki had built his house.

As the layers rose, the shrine swelled. The room became a hall. The hall acquired a platform, and the platform acquired buttressed walls with niches, a style that would mark Mesopotamian temples for thousands of years. The offerings changed. And in the early levels, among what had been left for the god of the sweet waters, lay fish bones.

The god of the Abzu was being fed fish, at the edge of the marsh, before there was writing to say so.

There is one more witness, and it is a list. The Sumerian King List begins its record of rulers with a sentence about the moment kingship came down from heaven. When it came down, the list says, it came to Eridu. The first king it names there is Alulim, who is given a reign of twenty-eight thousand eight hundred years. The list is a stern ledger, and nothing in it is soft or poetic; it just states, flatly, that the order of kings starts in the city of Enki.

So the god had a house, and the house was the oldest place anyone knew. He had a boat, and he had a banquet, and the friendship of the king of the gods. A god with all of that does not sit still. The next poem finds him standing on the deck of his boat, turning to look at a world that has not yet been switched on, and starting to give it its orders.

Enki turned the prow of his boat toward Sumer, and the world began to switch on.

That is how the poem called Enki and the World Order reads when its pieces are laid end to end. A god in a boat moves from place to place, and at each stop he speaks a word, and something starts to work. Nothing in it is a battle. No sea monster is cut in two, no sky is torn from its mother. The order of the world here is not won. It is handed out, appointment by appointment, by a god with a loud voice and a list.

He came first to Sumer. He stood before the land and blessed it, and the poem gives his blessing with the weight of a king speaking over a newborn. Sumer, the great land, the land of the me, the powers that make civilisation work. Mark that word. We will meet it again before long. May its house rise high and touch the sky, he said. May its lords wear bright crowns. May its cattle pens be thick, its sheepfolds full, its storehouses never empty. The land was his to bless, and he blessed it with everything he could think of.

Then Ur. He blessed the city on its mound, a city that would one day be the capital of kings, and the poem pictures its quays crowded with ships, its walls hung with the produce of the world. Ur was to be a city that took in the world's wealth and looked after its own.

Then he sailed on, past the edge of the known land, to the places beyond, and the poem becomes a tour of the far horizon. Meluhha, the black land, the distant country where the great trees grew and the reeds stood tall and the birds were strange and bright, a country so far away that Sumer knew it by its goods: carnelian, gold, rare timber. Enki blessed it. Dilmun he blessed too, the land of the sunrise to which we are about to go. He set the lands of the east in motion, so that their ships would come up the rivers to Sumer with their cargoes riding low.

And now the god stood in the middle of a country with no water in it, and he turned to fix that.

He filled the Tigris. The poem is frank about how, and I will leave it frank: an act of creation described in the earthy language the Sumerians were never shy about, an outpouring that made the river run bright and full. He filled the Euphrates too, and appointed a god to watch over both, to be the master of the rivers. He stirred the marshes and filled them with fish and reeds and set a goddess over the sea. He gave the storm god the rain, and sent him over the land, so that thunder and cloudburst belonged to somebody.

Now there was water, and Enki went to work on the soil. He called the plough and the yoke into being. He set a god over the ditches and dikes, a farmer of the canal-works, and a goddess over the grain, so that barley had a patron and a seed had a mother. He summoned the field, the furrow, the threshing floor. In the poem the plain of Sumer is not a thing that has always been there. It is a thing someone made, with tools, one tool at a time, and each tool has an owner.

Then he turned to the making of cities, and his voice grew practical. He appointed a god of the brick mould, because there are no houses without bricks. He appointed a master builder, a god of foundations and walls, so that a roof might stand over a hearth and stay up. And he gave the care of weaving to the goddess Uttu, so that thread could become cloth and cloth could become a garment. Remember her name. She is not a minor figure in the myths of Enki, and the next story will not let her go unharmed.

The poem is a kind of civil service of the cosmos. Every force in the Sumerian world has a position, a title, a job. The rain falls because someone is in charge of the rain. The river floods because someone is in charge of the river. The land is not wild. It is administered, and the administrator is a god who lives in a house in the deep.

You can feel what the poem is doing. It is saying that civilisation is not an accident, and not a punishment, and not a curse laid on men by gods who wanted servants. It is a gift. It has a giver. And the giver is not Enlil, the king of the gods, but his clever neighbour in the south, the one who builds and plans and arranges, who looks at a land with no water and says: here, let me fix that.

But a tour of appointments has a weak point, and the poem finds it. Someone was left out.

Inanna, the bright goddess, the young queen of heaven, stood before Enki and spoke in her own voice, and what she said was a complaint. He had given offices to the gods, one after another. He had set a god over the river and a god over the plough and a goddess over the loom. And herself? She had been given nothing. Everyone had a task, a title, a share. She alone had no office in his order.

The poem lets her words stand. It does not hurry to patch them over.

She would not be left without, and the night she took what she wanted is one of the best-known stories in the whole of Sumerian literature. But that story is further on. First we have to go east, to the place Enki had blessed with a promise, a land where nothing yet hurt, and where he would eat something he should not have eaten.

In the beginning, the poem says, the land of Dilmun was pure. It was clean. It was bright.

In Dilmun the raven did not croak. The lion did not kill, and the wolf did not seize the lamb. The dog did not learn to chase the kid. The man with the sore eyes did not say, my eyes are sore, and the man with the sore head did not say, my head is sore. The old woman did not say, I am an old woman, and the old man did not say, I am an old man. Nothing in that land knew the words for pain, or for age, or for fear. It is a picture of a world before anything had gone wrong, and the Sumerians painted it more than a thousand years before anyone wrote down the Book of Genesis.

The poem is called Enki and Ninhursag, and it is the story of how paradise got its first wound.

Dilmun, for all its purity, had a problem. It had no fresh water. The goddess of the land, Ninsikila, said as much to her father Enki, with some sharpness: he had given her a city, and a city needs a drink. She had no canals, no springs, no rain-fed fields. What kind of a pure land was it, if it could not grow anything?

Enki heard her. He spoke to the sun god, and told him to go down into the earth and bring up sweet water out of the ground. The god obeyed. Water gushed up out of the earth of Dilmun, the wells filled, the fields turned green, and the land became a place of dates and gardens, of harbours and trade. All of it was the gift of Enki, the lord of the Abzu, who had only to send his water up from the deep.

Then Enki looked around at what he had made, and his eye fell on the goddess Ninhursag, the lady of the mountain, mother of the land. He lay with her in the marshes of Dilmun. Nine days passed, the poem says, nine days that were nine months, and she gave birth to a daughter, a goddess of the green things that grow.

Enki saw the daughter, walking by the riverbank, and he lay with her too. And she bore a daughter. And Enki saw that daughter, and he did it again. A third daughter was born, the third in the line, and her name was Uttu.

Here the story takes a darker turn. Ninhursag, who knew what her husband was, spoke to Uttu in warning. She told her to be careful of the man who comes to the door, because a man like that will say anything. But Enki was patient, and he was cunning, and he had help. He sent his vizier, Isimud, a god with two faces, who looked both ways at once. Between them they set a trap made of gifts. Enki would give Uttu cucumbers, he said, and apples, and grapes. She opened her door.

The poem does not dwell on what happened inside. It does tell us what Ninhursag did next. She found the traces of it on Uttu, wiped them away, and planted them in the ground. And eight plants sprang up.

Enki, wandering through the marsh, saw them. They were beautiful. He had never seen their like. He asked his vizier what they were, and Isimud, who knew everything the god needed to know, named each one for him. And Enki, one after another, cut them and ate them.

He ate all eight.

It was the oldest mistake in the world: the thing in the garden, so lovely, so unfamiliar, the pleasure of putting it into the mouth. Ninhursag came out to the marsh and found the plants gone, and she knew what had happened. She cursed him. The words are plain. Until he was dead, she said, she would not look on him with the eye of life. And she vanished, and the world lost the mother of the land.

Enki began to sicken.

The poem names it part by part. Eight places in his body, the very places the plants had fed, began to fail. A god who had ruled water and soil and bricks and rivers was lying in the marsh with his strength going out of him. The Anunna sat down in the dust. Enlil, the king of the gods, was at a loss. No one could find Ninhursag. No one knew how to call her back.

It was a fox who came up with the plan. Not a god, not a vizier, a fox. He went to Enlil and asked what his reward would be if he found Ninhursag and brought her. Enlil promised him honour in Nippur. The fox set off across the wilderness, found the goddess where she had gone, and spoke to her. He spoke well, the poem implies. And Ninhursag, her anger slowly easing, came back.

She looked at Enki, dying in front of her, and she asked, in a gentler voice than she had used for the curse: my brother, what hurts you? He said, my head. And she took him into her own body, the poem is blunt, and she set him in the place of birth. From it, for each failing part of his body, she brought forth a god to heal it. A god for the head. A god for the mouth, who is the goddess of beer. A god for the side. And a goddess for the rib, whose name is Ninti.

The poem closes with eight healed organs, eight new deities, and the order of the gods restored. The story is not about punishment. It is about repair. Enki ate what he should not have eaten. The mother of the land cursed him. The mother of the land healed him. And the paradise of Dilmun, scarred once, went on.

In nineteen forty four the Assyriologist Samuel Noah Kramer, whose book Sumerian Mythology brought these poems to a modern audience, pointed to a pun inside the story. Ninti means lady of the rib. But in Sumerian the word ti also means to make live. So the goddess born to heal Enki's rib was also the lady who gives life. Now turn to the book of Genesis. The Lord God put the first man to sleep, and took one of his ribs, and made it into the first woman, and the man named her Eve. Kramer saw a trace of the Sumerian pun in the biblical story. The King James Version reads, in the second chapter of Genesis, verses twenty one and twenty two: "And the LORD God caused a deep sleep to fall upon Adam, and he slept: and he took one of his ribs, and closed up the flesh instead thereof; And the rib, which the LORD God had taken from man, made he a woman, and brought her unto the man." And in the third chapter, verse twenty: "And Adam called his wife's name Eve; because she was the mother of all living." The rib. The life. The woman. It was Kramer's argument, and he made it with care. The Sumerian poem comes with a garden, a forbidden eating, a curse, and a rib that means life.

But Enki, when he got up from that marsh, was not changed. A god who has been cursed, nearly killed, and healed by the woman he wronged, goes back to being a god. His appetites were still his appetites. In the next story, it is not plants that bring him down. It is beer.

Inanna came to Eridu on a night when Enki was in the mood to give.

She had come from Uruk, where her temple stood, and she had come with a purpose. She remembered the complaint she had made when he handed out the offices of the world and left her with nothing. She had been thinking about it ever since. She had decided that if Enki would not give her a share of what he guarded, she would go and look at what he guarded, and see whether he might be talked out of some of it.

What he guarded were the me.

The word is Sumerian and it has no real English equivalent, which is why translators leave it alone. The me were the divine powers of civilisation: the principles, the arts, the offices, the abilities by which the world of gods and men worked. Kingship was a me. The priesthood was a me. The craft of the smith, the craft of the carpenter, music, the art of the scribe. Truth was a me. So was the descent into the underworld, and the ascent from it. They were not objects, though in the story they could be carried on a boat. They were not ideas, though they could be named. They were something between a law and a tool. Whoever held a me held the power to do the thing, and the right to do it.

And Enki, in the poem called Inanna and Enki, kept them in the Abzu, in Eridu, in his own house.

Inanna presented herself at the door. Enki's vizier Isimud received her, and Enki, who had been told she was coming, gave orders to treat the young goddess well. Let there be barley cake, he said, and butter, and cool water. Let the beer be poured. Let us sit with her and drink.

So the god of wisdom and the queen of heaven sat down together, in the house at the bottom of the world, and began to drink. The poem lets the evening go long. They drank beer, and they drank again, and the cups went round, and the toasts came up out of the jars of the Abzu, one after another. Enki's heart was light. His tongue was loose. And in the warmth of it he began to speak, and what he said, in the name of his own power and in the name of his own deep waters, was a gift.

To my daughter Inanna, he said, I give the lordship. I give the godship. I give the high and enduring crown, and the throne of kingship.

She took it. She did not speak. She took it, the poem says, and she sat there with the cup in her hand, and Enki went on.

He gave her the offices of the priests and priestesses, one by one. He gave her truth. He gave her the descent into the underworld, and the ascent out of it. He gave her the art of song and the craft of the carpenter, the metalworker, the scribe, the leatherworker, the mason, the basket weaver. He gave her the ability to kindle strife, and to quiet it, and to give judgements, and to make decisions. He gave her the sounds of joy and of lament. The tablet's list goes on and on, a hundred gifts or near it, and with every one the pile in front of her grew, and with every one the god of the Abzu emptied his storehouse a little more.

If you want a modern word for what was passing across the table, call the me the operating system of civilisation. Call them the source code of the Sumerian world, the instructions from which everything else runs. Enki, with a few cups in him, handed over the master keys, one after another, to a goddess who had come to his house to get them. It is a bold way to put it, and the tablet does not use those words, but it is exactly what the tablet is describing: the night the keys to civilisation were passed across a drinking table.

Inanna did not argue. She accepted each gift and said that she would take it. And when the last of them was given, she stood, and she gathered them, and she carried them out of the house of Enki and loaded them onto the Boat of Heaven.

The Boat of Heaven slid away from the quay at Eridu. The goddess and her vizier stood on its deck and watched the house in the water recede. They had everything. They had the crowns, the crafts, the arts, the very power by which the world made itself.

In the morning, Enki woke.

The poem puts his waking in a few short lines. The god looked around. The place at his side was empty. He called for his vizier. Isimud came. And Enki, with the heaviness of a man waking up to find something wrong, asked about the me. Where are the me? Where is the lordship, the godship, the crown, the throne?

My lord, said Isimud, you gave them to your daughter.

The god of the Abzu stood still. Then he asked where the boat was now.

It had already gone, Isimud told him. It had been sailing for hours.

Enki did not have to think long. He summoned the vizier, and he summoned a band of sea monsters, and he sent them after the Boat of Heaven, with an order: bring it back. Bring back everything on it.

What followed is one of the great chase scenes of Sumerian literature. Seven times Enki sent his pursuers after Inanna. Seven stops on the river, one after another, and at each of them the monsters came upon the boat and tried to seize it. And at each of them Inanna's vizier, standing at her side, gave the same answer. Enki himself had given the me, she said. Enki himself had spoken the words. The god of the Abzu had given them in his own house, in his own name, and a word of a god, once spoken, cannot be called back.

There is something almost comic in the way Enki's creatures keep arriving at each stop, and keep being turned away. The cleverest god in the world is chasing his own words down a river and cannot catch them.

At last the Boat of Heaven reached Uruk. Inanna stepped ashore at the quay, and there was a celebration. The me were carried into her city. The city that would one day boast the first great kings and the first great walls, and that would give its name to the whole land of Sumer's southern plain, took possession of the powers of civilisation on the night they were delivered by a goddess who had outdrunk a god.

The tablet ends with Enki's word standing. The god of wisdom, who tricked other gods for a living, had been tricked, and the trick had cost him everything he held. And yet the poem does not condemn him. In the Sumerian mind, to give is not a mistake. It is the nature of Enki to give, and the poem seems to say that when the god of the Abzu gives, the gift cannot be taken back.

That is the fourth thing. Now we turn to the fifth, and it is the strangest of all. For once he does not give. He takes something away, from all of us: our common speech.

The messenger ran. He ran from Uruk across the plain, up through the foothills, over the passes of the eastern mountains, a long way, with a king's message in his mouth.

The king was Enmerkar, and Uruk was his city, the city of Inanna. And Enmerkar had an ambition. He wanted a temple, and he wanted it from the best of everything: gold and silver and lapis lazuli, shaped stone and skilled hands. All of that, the poem says, lay in a distant country called Aratta, high in the mountains, whose ruler held the stones and the craftsmen. Enmerkar was determined that Aratta should send them down to Uruk, to build a shrine for Inanna in her own city, and a shrine in Eridu, to adorn the house of Enki.

So he sent a messenger, with a demand.

The lord of Aratta heard it and laughed. He did not intend to bend his knee to Uruk. He sent back an answer, with conditions. Enmerkar sent a second message. The lord of Aratta sent a second answer. The poem is a long contest of words carried by a runner over a mountain road, a duel of kings who never meet, each trying to wear the other down, each refusing to yield. It is called Enmerkar and the Lord of Aratta, and it is the story of a man whose argument grew so large that he could not fit it into a single mouth.

At last Enmerkar made a decision. He would send the messenger once more, with a message so long and so grand that it could not be carried by memory. And so he did something no king in the poem had done before. He took a lump of soft clay, shaped it into a tablet, and pressed his words into it. Until that day, the poem says, there had been no such thing. It tells the beginning of writing as the invention of a desperate king who could not make himself understood across a mountain.

The messenger carried the tablet up to Aratta. He put it in the lord's hand. And the lord of Aratta looked at the wedge-shaped marks, and they were words.

And inside that long message, among the demands and the threats, Enmerkar gave a spell. It is called the incantation of Nudimmud, the name the poem uses for Enki, and it is one of the strangest passages in all of Sumerian literature.

It speaks of a time before the world was divided. There was no snake in those days, the spell says, and no scorpion. There was no hyena, no lion, no wild dog, no wolf. There was no fear and no terror. A man had no rival. In those days the lands of the world, Shubur-Hamazi and Sumer, the great land of the me of lordship, and Uri, and the land of the Martu resting in safety, the whole universe, all the people, in one speech, gave praise to Enlil.

One language. One voice. One song going up to the god of Nippur.

And then Enki changed it.

Enki, the lord of abundance, whose commands are trustworthy, the lord of wisdom who understands the land, the leader of the gods, the lord of Eridu, endowed with wisdom, changed the speech in their mouths. He brought contention into it. He took the speech of mankind that had been one and made it many. The spell does not say why. It does not explain what Enki wanted, or what the people had done, or whether it was a punishment or a whim. It says only that the god of Eridu reached into the mouths of the lands and turned one tongue into a crowd.

Then Enmerkar made his request. The spell calls on Enki, by that same name, to turn the speech of the lands back, to let the whole world speak once more in one tongue to Enlil, and, the political thrust of it is clear, to have Aratta stand with Uruk, to bring the mountain country down to bow before the city of the plain.

That is the passage, and it has been argued over since its translation. In nineteen sixty eight Samuel Noah Kramer published it under the title The Babel of Tongues, and read it as the Sumerian forerunner of the tower of Babel, an old tale of a time when all humanity spoke one language and a god broke it apart. Others read the same lines the other way round. In their view the spell looks forward more than back. The one tongue is not a lost golden age, but a wish: Enmerkar's hope, spoken in the shape of an incantation, that the lands would be united under Enlil, with Uruk at their centre.

Both readings are in the lines, and the lines do not choose.

Now the biblical text. Genesis, the eleventh chapter, the first verse: "And the whole earth was of one language, and of one speech." The people journey to a plain and settle, and they say, in verse four: "Go to, let us build us a city and a tower, whose top may reach unto heaven; and let us make us a name, lest we be scattered abroad upon the face of the whole earth." And the Lord comes down, and in verse seven, says: "Go to, let us go down, and there confound their language, that they may not understand one another's speech." And verse nine: "Therefore is the name of it called Babel; because the LORD did there confound the language of all the earth: and from thence did the LORD scatter them abroad upon the face of all the earth."

Look at the shape of both. A world with one speech. A god who breaks it. In the Sumerian, the speaker is Enki. In Genesis, the Lord. In the Sumerian there is no tower. In Genesis, there is no Enmerkar. The two stories are not the same story. But they rhyme, and they rhyme at the most unlikely place, on the matter of why we cannot understand one another.

And the Sumerian version has this twist. The god who breaks the speech of humanity is the same god who is, throughout the myths, the friend of humanity. Enki does not hate us. He built the house that gave us water, set the rain on the fields, gave Inanna the arts of the scribe and the smith. And then, in this one poem, he reaches into our mouths and cuts our voices into pieces. If the spell is about the past, it is the story of how a helper became a divider. If it is about the future, it is a king's prayer that the god who divided will, for once, reunite.

What happened next in Aratta is a tale of riddles and rivals and sorcerers, and a wise woman who beat a magician at his own game, and it ends, as these things do, with the mountain country giving way. But it is the spell that stays. A king pressed it into clay because he could not carry it in his mouth. It has survived four thousand years because it asks the question that every language-divided people ask: who did this?

The answer, in Sumer, was a god with a house in the water. And in the next story the same god turns the other way. Not a god who breaks the world, but a god who goes against the will of heaven to save one man from drowning.

A man stood beside a wall in the dark, and the wall began to speak.

The story comes down to us in three great versions, in two languages and across more than a thousand years, and in every one of them the same thing happens. The gods have decided to destroy humanity with a flood, and they have taken an oath not to tell. And one of them, the one with a house in the deep waters, finds a way to break the oath without breaking it.

The oldest of the three is in Sumerian, in a text the Assyriologist Thorkild Jacobsen named the Eridu Genesis. Its tablet is damaged, and the beginning is lost, but what we have of it runs like this. The gods had made humanity and set kings over them, and the cities were founded, Eridu first of all. Then the gods assembled, and An and Enlil and Enki and the goddess who had shaped the people sat in council, and they decided to bring a flood and wipe the human race out. The goddess who had made mankind wept at the decision. But the decision was taken, and the gods swore.

Enki, the poem says, took counsel with himself.

There was a king in those days, a man named Ziusudra, who served the gods with humility. His city was Shuruppak. He was a priest as well as a king, and he went daily to the god's shrine, devout, quiet, listening for dreams. And one day Ziusudra was standing by a wall.

A voice came to him from the wall. The poem breaks off around the words, but its sense is clear. Stand at my left side, the voice said, by the wall, and I will speak a word to you. Listen to it. Pay attention to what I say. A flood will sweep over the cities, and it will destroy the seed of mankind.

The god did not appear. The god did not break his oath. He spoke to a wall, and the man who happened to be leaning against it heard.

The tablet is torn after that. When the text resumes, there is a storm. It is a flood. The winds rage, the waters rise, and a huge boat is riding on them, and in the boat, the poem implies, are Ziusudra and a cargo of living things. For seven days and seven nights the flood swept over the land. Then Utu, the sun god, came out, and light fell on heaven and earth. Ziusudra opened a window in the boat and let the light in. He bowed to the sun. He sacrificed an ox and a sheep.

And An and Enlil, the poem says, bestowed on him life like that of a god. They gave him breath eternal. And they settled him in a distant land, in Dilmun, in the place where the sun rises.

The same story, the same trick of the wall, runs through the Babylonian tales. In the poem called Atrahasis, a long Akkadian epic that begins with the creation of humanity, Enlil grows tired of the noise of the human race. People multiply, and their uproar keeps the god from sleep. He sends a plague to thin them. And Ea, as the Babylonians called Enki, tells the wise man Atrahasis to do something clever: have the people stop making offerings to all the gods and bring them only to the god of plague, and shame him into relenting. It works. The plague stops. Enlil sends a drought. Ea helps again. Then famine, and Ea helps again. Each time the trickster in the deep finds a loophole. And each time Enlil, who is not a fool, realises that his plans are being undone.

At last Enlil calls the gods together and orders them to swear an oath not to warn mankind of the flood.

So Ea speaks to a wall. In Atrahasis, and again in tablet eleven of the Epic of Gilgamesh, he addresses a reed hut, and he says what the gods cannot say to a man. He tells the reed wall to listen. Man of Shuruppak, tear down your house. Build a boat. Give up your possessions, and save your life. Put aboard the seed of every living thing. In Gilgamesh, Ea tells him how to build it: like the Abzu, it should be roofed over. He gives the dimensions of a vessel as wide as it is long, a cube of timber, with seven decks and nine compartments to each deck, sealed with pitch within and without.

And Ea gives him a lie to tell. The people of the city will ask what he is doing. Tell them, says Ea, that Enlil hates you, that you can no longer live in the city, that you are going down to the Abzu to live with Ea, and that the god will send you abundance, a rain of bread in the morning and wheat in the evening. The neighbours help build the boat, believing they will be rewarded. The very people who are about to drown carry the timber.

It is the most uncomfortable detail in the whole story, and the Babylonians told it without flinching. The god who saves one man does it by deceiving a city.

The flood comes. In Gilgamesh the storm is so terrible that the gods themselves are afraid of it and flee up to the highest heaven, cowering like dogs. For six days and six nights the wind blows. On the seventh the sea grows calm. The survivor opens the hatch. All is silence, and all of humanity has turned to clay. The boat comes to rest on a mountain. He sends out a dove, and a swallow, and a raven. The raven does not return. He steps out and makes an offering, and the gods, who have been starving without their sacrifices, crowd around it like flies.

Then Enlil comes down, and sees that someone has lived.

He is furious. Who has escaped? Who has broken the oath? And Ea, with the calm of a god who has done what he meant to do, rebukes him. How could you, the wisest of the gods, bring such a flood without thought? Let the sinner bear his own sin. Let a lion come, or a wolf, or a famine, rather than a flood that kills everyone. And then, because it will not be the last word, Ea admits it: I did not reveal the secret of the gods. I let a wise man have a dream, and he understood.

Enlil, the king of the gods, stops. He takes the survivor and his wife by the hand and leads them onto the shore. He makes them kneel. He stands between them, and he blesses them. Until now, he says, this man has been a man. Now he and his wife shall be like gods. They will live far away, at the mouth of the rivers.

Eternal life. For one man, because Enki broke the council's plan to give it to him. In the Sumerian poem Ziusudra is carried to Dilmun, the land where nothing yet grew old. In the Babylonian poems he lives at the far edge of the world, at the mouth of the rivers, where a hero named Gilgamesh will one day travel to ask him for the secret of immortality, and fail.

It is a good story for Enki's reputation. The god of the deep waters, who gave men their rivers and their ploughs, stood against the king of the gods for the sake of humanity. And the man he saved received what no one else in the stories receives. He was given life like a god's.

So hold both ideas together, because they will not sit still for long. One man was given eternal life through Enki's help. Another was denied it through Enki's advice. The second man is the one who lost his boat to the South Wind, and who is, at this very moment, being sent to heaven with a set of instructions in his head.

Ea did not panic. That is the first thing to notice about him, in the house at Eridu, on the day the king of heaven sent for his servant.

The tablet says that Ea knew what heaven knew. Nothing that happened in the sky or the sea escaped him, and when Anu called for Adapa, the god of the deep waters understood at once what the call meant. A man had broken the wing of the South Wind. A god who ruled the sky had been stopped for seven days. A human being had done what no human being was meant to be able to do, and now a god was calling him to account.

He could have hidden Adapa. He could have argued. Instead he gave him advice, and the advice was exact.

He began by changing the man's appearance. He had Adapa put on a mourning garment, a garment of dishevelled grief. He told him to leave his hair unkempt, matted and untended, the way a man wears it when he is in the middle of a loss. A sage of Eridu, a priest who set the table for a god, was made to look like someone who had just buried a father.

Then Ea told him what to do.

You will go up to heaven, he said. You will take the road that goes there. And when you reach the gate of Anu, you will find two gods standing guard: Dumuzi and Ningishzida.

These are not minor figures. Dumuzi is the shepherd god, the young husband of Inanna, who in other stories goes down into the land of the dead and does not come back. Ningishzida is a god the Sumerians linked with the world below. Both of them, in one way or another, are gods of vanishing. One is the shepherd who disappears into the earth. The other is a lord of the underworld's door. They stand at the gate of heaven as its guards, and it is they, not Anu himself, whom Adapa must win.

They will ask you why you are dressed this way, Ea told him. For whom are you in mourning? And you will answer them: two gods have vanished from the land. That is why I am in mourning. And they will ask which two. Tell them: Dumuzi and Ningishzida.

It is a gentle trick, and a clever one. Ea is sending Adapa to the gate in a state of flattery. Two gods who know what it is to disappear from the earth will hear that someone has mourned them. They will look at each other, Ea said. They will be struck by it. And they will speak a good word for you to Anu, and when you stand before him, he will show you a kind face.

And then Ea gave him the last of his instructions, the one he repeated, the one he made sure the man would not forget.

When you stand before Anu, he said, they will offer you food. It will be bread of death. Do not eat it. They will offer you water. It will be water of death. Do not drink it. They will offer you a garment. Put it on. They will offer you oil. Anoint yourself. Do not neglect the instruction I have given you. Hold fast to the words I have spoken.

Bread of death. Water of death. Do not eat. Do not drink.

The words are plain. The god is not hinting. He is giving his servant a short, clear order, the kind that you give a man you are sending into danger, the kind a father gives a son who is going to the court of a king. Adapa heard it. He bowed. And Ea made him take the road to heaven.

He went up. The tablet does not describe the road. We can only say that a man in a mourning garment, with unkempt hair and a broken wind behind him, climbed to the gate of the sky and came to its guards.

Dumuzi and Ningishzida were standing there. They saw him coming, and they spoke to him. Adapa, they asked, what is this? Who is the man who comes before us in mourning? For whom is the garment? For whom the unkempt hair?

And Adapa answered what Ea had told him to answer. Two gods have disappeared from the land, he said. That is why I wear the mourning garment.

Who are they, the gates asked. Who are the two gods who have disappeared from the land?

Dumuzi and Ningishzida, said Adapa.

And the two guards looked at one another. The effect was exactly what Ea had foretold. They were moved. They were struck. A man had come to the gate of heaven to mourn them, to grieve for gods who were missing from the earth, and their hearts turned toward him. They brought him in. They stood beside him before the throne of Anu, and they spoke for him.

Anu was waiting. The king of the sky looked at the man who had broken the wing of the wind.

Why did you do it, he asked. Why did you break the wing of the South Wind?

Adapa told him. He said that he had been fishing for the house of his lord Ea, out in the middle of the sea. The sea was like a mirror. And the South Wind had blown and had sunk him, and had cast him into the house of the fish. And in the anger of his heart, he said, he had cursed it.

It was a plain account. It was an honest account. And it was backed, now, by two voices that Anu could not easily ignore, the two gods at the gate who spoke for Adapa and put in a good word.

The tablet says that Anu's heart grew calm. The anger that had thrown him from his throne ebbed. A man had done a terrible thing and had told the truth about why, and the king of heaven found himself, to his own surprise, listening.

And then Anu asked a question that has echoed through the study of this tablet ever since. Why, he asked, had Ea shown the heart of heaven and earth to a worthless human? Why had he given him wisdom? Why had he made him a man of distinction, with a name?

It is a startling thing to say. It is Anu, the king of heaven, scolding Ea in absentia, for giving a mortal too much. And the question is not answered. Because Anu, having asked it, immediately asks another. What shall we do for him?

He had a thought. He gave the order.

Bring him the bread of life.

The servants came, and in their hands was bread.

It was not the bread of death. Ea had warned about that, and Adapa had his guard up, and he was waiting for it. This bread was different. It was the bread of life. Anu, who had been angry a few moments before, who had risen from his throne in a rage and sent for the man who broke the wing of his wind, now ordered that the same man be offered the thing no human being had ever been given. Let him eat, said the king of heaven, and live for ever.

The bread was set before him. Adapa did not eat.

They brought the water of life. A cup of it was placed beside him. It was cool, and bright, and there was no one in the room who did not understand what it was. Adapa looked at it. He did not drink.

They brought the garment. He took it and put it on. They brought the oil. He anointed himself.

He had done exactly what Ea had told him to do.

The hall must have gone very quiet. The tablet does not say so, but the next lines make it plain. Anu looked at the man standing in front of him, the man in a new garment, with oil on his skin, with the bread of life and the water of life untouched on the table. And then the king of heaven did something no one expected.

He laughed.

He laughed at Adapa. He said: Come, Adapa. Why did you not eat? Why did you not drink? Now you will not live. The tablet's words are short, and they are devastating. You will not live. Alas for the human race.

Adapa gave his answer. Ea, my lord, he said, commanded me. Do not eat. Do not drink.

There is no more argument. There is no appeal. There is no second chance. Anu said: take him, and return him to his earth.

The sentence is over almost before it begins. The tablet is damaged after this point, and the last lines are broken, and what can be read of them turns to sickness, the diseases that came to afflict human bodies, and to the goddess of healing, who could soothe them. The story seems to say that something that day entered the human world, and that it did not leave. Adapa went down from heaven the way he had come up, with a new garment, a well-oiled body, and the knowledge that he had refused the gift of the gods.

Now the question. It is the question the whole episode has been building toward. Did Ea lie to Adapa?

There are three possible answers, and every reader of the tablet has to pick one.

The first is that Ea lied. He told Adapa that Anu would offer bread of death, knowing that Anu would offer bread of life, and he did it to keep humanity mortal. He was a god of cunning. He had tricked Enlil, tricked the gods, tricked a whole city. Why not trick one man into a refusal?

The second is that Ea did not know. He gave advice in good faith, in the middle of a crisis, with the information he had. Anu was angry. Ea expected a trap. He told Adapa to refuse the food because he feared that Anu, in his rage, would poison him. He did not foresee that the king of heaven would change his mind. No god sees everything.

The third is that Ea meant well, and was simply wrong. His instructions were meant to protect Adapa, and they ended up costing him everything.

The Israeli scholar Shlomo Izre'el, in his book Adapa and the South Wind, treats the tale as a myth about why human beings die. It does not matter, on this reading, whether Ea lied or erred. The point is the shape of the story: a wise man, a god's favour, an offer of life, and a refusal that fixes the human condition for ever. The tablet is an answer to a child's question. Why do we die? Because once, in heaven, someone was offered life, and said no.

I will give you my own answer, and I will give it straight. Ea knew.

Think about who he is. This is the god who spoke to a wall to break the council's oath. This is the god who sent a man into a city with a lie to tell, so that the drowning would build the boat. Every time we meet him in these tales, he is the one who knows the secret, who sees the loophole, who is a step ahead of the king of heaven. And he is the god who made Adapa wise, and gave him all things but eternal life, and the tablet says so in its first lines: wisdom, but not eternal life. It is a pattern. Ea gives humanity the world. He does not give it the one thing the gods keep for themselves. He is the great friend of humanity, and he draws a line, and the line is death. Eternal life for the man in the boat at the flood, a gift given by the council and not by him. Eternal life for no one else. He told Adapa what to say at the gate, and he told him what not to eat, and the two instructions together added up to a mortal who would walk home down the road of heaven and tell his people that the gods had offered him life and that he had sent it back.

That is the seventh thing. That is what he told Adapa before he went up to heaven.

Now consider the tablet itself, because its history is almost as strange as its story.

The best-preserved copy of the Adapa story was not found in Mesopotamia at all. It was found in Egypt, at Tell el-Amarna, the ruins of the city the pharaoh Akhenaten built in the fourteenth century before the common era. When the tablets of Tell el-Amarna came out of the ground in the eighteen eighties, most of them were what you would expect from a royal archive: letters. Diplomatic letters, from the kings of Babylon and Assyria and Mitanni, from the princes of Canaan, written in Akkadian, the language of international correspondence in the ancient Near East. And among those letters, in the archive of the pharaohs, lay a copy of a Babylonian myth about a fisherman who broke the wing of the wind.

Why was it there? Because the Egyptian scribes who had to read and write Akkadian had to learn it, and they learned it the way scribes everywhere learn, by copying literature. The tablet of Adapa at Amarna was a school text. And you can still see the schoolroom on it. Someone has added red dots to the clay, small marks in red ink above and between the signs, the way a student marks up a difficult page. An Egyptian scribe was working his way through the story of Adapa, one line at a time, learning a foreign language by reading about the day mankind was offered eternal life and turned it down.

The other fragments come from a different archive, a thousand years later and several hundred miles to the north. In the seventh century before the common era, the Assyrian king Ashurbanipal gathered tablets from across his empire into a library at Nineveh. He had the scribes copy every text they could find: omens, hymns, epics, myths. In his own inscriptions, he claims that he could read writing from before the flood. Fragments of Adapa were among the thousands of pieces of clay that were dug out of the ruins of that library in the nineteenth century.

So the story of Adapa was read by an Egyptian schoolboy in the fourteenth century and by an Assyrian scribe in the seventh. For centuries after it was first told, people in different countries and in different languages kept copying out this one tale. A myth about a man who obeyed his god, and lost the one gift that mattered. They never stopped telling it. It seems they never got tired of asking whether Ea told the truth.

Seven things, and the god behind them is not one god but many.

He is the builder of the house in the deep, who sails up the river to feast with the king of the gods. He is the great administrator, who fills the rivers and sets the rain and hands out the plough. He is the lover who cannot stop himself, who eats the eight plants and lies sick in the marsh. He is the drunkard, who gives away the keys of civilisation across a table and spends the next morning chasing his own words down a river. He is the divider of speech. He is the saviour of the one man in the flood. And he is the god who advised Adapa at the gate.

The Danish-American scholar Thorkild Jacobsen, in The Treasures of Darkness, argued that the Sumerian gods were first of all the powers behind what happened in the world: the rain, the grain, the river, the storm. A Sumerian looked at the sweet water that rose in the marshes and felt it as a presence, a person with a will. Enki is that water, personified. He is cunning because water finds its way round every obstacle. He is generous because water gives life. He is slippery because water cannot be held. And in the long run, said Jacobsen, the Sumerians came to think of the world as a kind of household, a great estate, with Enlil as its lord and the other gods as its stewards, among whom Enki was the clever one who knew how to make it all work.

Where do these stories come from, and when? The poems we have were written in Sumerian on clay tablets, many of them copied in the scribal schools of Nippur, around eighteen hundred before the common era, though the stories behind them are older, reaching back into the third millennium. The Akkadian tales of Atrahasis and Adapa come from the Babylonian and Assyrian world, down through the second millennium and into the first. By the time Ashurbanipal built his library at Nineveh, in the seventh century before the common era, the Sumerian language had been dead for more than a thousand years, and his scribes were copying it the way a modern scholar copies Latin. The tablets were then lost. They lay under the sand of Iraq and Egypt until the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, when diggers brought them up, and a handful of readers, Samuel Noah Kramer among them, learned to read them again. Stephanie Dalley's Myths from Mesopotamia and the collection edited by Jeremy Black, The Literature of Ancient Sumer, have put most of them within reach of anyone who can read English.

And when you read them, you find that they sound familiar.

There is a garden where nothing hurts. There is a forbidden eating, a curse, a rib, a woman called the mother of the living. There is a time when the whole earth spoke one language, and a god who broke it. There is a flood that destroys the world, and one man in a boat who is saved because a god warned him. And there is, in the Bible as in the tablet, a way to eternal life that is lost. In Genesis, the third chapter, verse twenty two: "And the LORD God said, Behold, the man is become as one of us, to know good and evil: and now, lest he put forth his hand, and take also of the tree of life, and eat, and live for ever." And in verse twenty four: "So he drove out the man; and he placed at the east of the garden of Eden Cherubims, and a flaming sword which turned every way, to keep the way of the tree of life."

The comparisons are real. Eden and Dilmun. The rib and Ninti. Babel and the spell of Nudimmud. The flood and the man of Shuruppak. The tree of life and the bread of life. But they are comparisons, parallels, and no more. Scholars do not agree on whether one tradition borrowed from another, or whether both drew on older ways of telling the same stories, and the tablets do not settle it. The Sumerian tales stand on their own: a man who wins life from the gods, a man who loses it, and a god in the middle with his own reasons.

And there is something else the tablets do that Genesis does not. In Genesis the loss of eternal life is the result of a human act: an apple, an act of disobedience. In the Sumerian and Babylonian tales it is the opposite. Adapa loses eternal life by obeying. He does exactly as he is told. He is faithful to the god who made him, and the faithfulness costs him everything.

That is the thing the story leaves us with. It does not tell us that Ea was cruel. It does not tell us he was kind. It tells us that the god of wisdom, who gave us so much, drew a line. And that the line was drawn through the middle of a single meal.

So go back to the end. Adapa has been sent down. The garment is on his shoulders, the oil on his skin, and the bread of life untouched on a table in heaven. He walks back along the road he came up, through the gate where Dumuzi and Ningishzida stand. He comes down through the sky toward a country where the South Wind will blow again. Somewhere ahead of him, in a house at the edge of the marsh, the god who gave him the advice is waiting.

What did he say to him, when he came home? The tablet does not tell us.

It only says that Adapa lived, and then he died.
