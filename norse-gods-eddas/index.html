The Norse Gods: The Eddas, the Whole Story

The Poetic Edda and Snorri's Prose Edda: the world made from Ymir, Odin and the runes, Thor and Loki, the death of Baldr, Ragnarok, and what the Norse actually worshipped.

Full script

In the winter of about twelve seventy, an imagined farmstead stood somewhere in Iceland, beyond the settlements and fields where sheep were kept through the cold months. Let us call the scribe Olaf. He is an imagined farmer and copyist, not a person in the historical record.

Olaf had a calfskin before him. The skin had been prepared, cut, and folded into leaves. A lamp gave enough light for letters, though not enough to make the room bright. Outside, the winter narrowed the day. Inside, a hand moved across the page.

The land had been Christian for about two hundred and seventy years. Churches stood in Iceland. Priests read Latin books. Baptism, burial, and the calendar belonged to a Christian order. Yet older poems still circulated in memory, at gatherings, in performances, and among people who knew their inherited verse.

Olaf did not write a scripture. No institution had given him a Norse sacred canon to copy. He wrote poems that had survived because people had remembered them, performed them, taught them, and eventually set them down.

One poem began with a seeress addressing Odin. She spoke of the first age, before the world had shape. She described a time when there was no sand, no sea, no cooling wave, no earth below and no sky above. Then the gods raised the land, fixed the sun and moon in their courses, and gave names to places and times.

The poems did not offer one continuous book with one author. They preserved voices, scenes, speeches, warnings, riddles, and accounts of divine deeds. Some passages spoke in the voice of Odin. Others presented a seeress, a giant, a god, or a human hero. Their order in a manuscript was not necessarily the order in which they had first been composed.

On the calfskin, the scribe could copy an account of the world made from the body of a giant. Ymir was killed by Odin and his brothers. His flesh became the earth. His blood became the sea. His bones became the mountains. His skull became the sky. Sparks from the realm of fire became lights in the heavens.

The same body of tradition placed a great tree at the centre of the worlds. Yggdrasil rose among gods, humans, giants, the dead, and other beings. Its roots reached toward wells. A serpent troubled one root. An eagle occupied its upper branches. The tree was not simply a decoration. It gave the myths a structure in which different peoples and destinies were connected.

Odin appeared as a god of knowledge, poetry, war, death, and kingship. He was not presented as a simple ruler seated safely above the other gods. He gave an eye for wisdom. He hung for nine nights on a tree, wounded by a spear, and learned the runes. He travelled in disguise. He sought information from the dead and from giants. His knowledge had a price.

Thor carried Mjollnir, the hammer that protected gods and humans. In one poem, a giant stole it and demanded Freyja as his bride. Loki travelled to Freyja, Thor dressed as the bride, and the hammer was placed on the bride's lap during the wedding feast. Thor seized it and killed the giant.

In another tale, Thor visited a hall ruled by Utgard-Loki. There he entered contests that seemed humiliating. He tried to empty a drinking horn. He wrestled an old woman. He attempted to lift a cat. Later, the ruler explained that the contests had concealed larger forces: the sea, old age, and the world serpent Jormungand.

Loki moved through these stories as helper, trickster, parent, enemy, and destroyer. The gods relied on his cunning and suffered from it. He helped obtain treasures. He caused the death of Baldr. He was bound beneath the earth, and his punishment belonged to the chain of events leading toward the final battle.

That battle had a name: Ragnarok. The sun would be swallowed. The dead would rise. Fenrir would break free. Jormungand would emerge from the sea. Odin would face Fenrir. Thor would kill Jormungand and then fall from its poison. The gods would fight, and the world would burn.

The poems and Snorri's later book preserve this movement from creation to destruction. But the preserved texts are not direct records of a pagan priesthood speaking in its own temples. They were written in Christian-era Iceland, long after the conversion, and they describe traditions that had travelled through memory and poetry.

Their survival is therefore part of the subject. So is their distance from the people who once raised stones, wore pendants, named farms, made offerings, and told stories in languages that have left only scattered traces.

The manuscript associated with the poems is called the Codex Regius. It dates to about twelve seventy. It is real. The imagined farmstead and the imagined scribe are only a way to place the act of copying within the world that produced the surviving book.

The poems tell of a world made from a giant's body, a tree of nine worlds, one-eyed Odin, Thor and Loki, and a last battle. What do these poems and Snorri's later book really tell us about gods once worshipped in the north?

To answer, we must go back to the manuscripts, the poems, and the surviving objects that preserve the distance between myth and worship.

There was no Norse scripture in the sense of a single revealed book preserved by a religious institution. The evidence comes from several kinds of source, created at different times and for different purposes.

The most important poetic collection is now known as the Poetic Edda. Its poems are preserved chiefly in the Codex Regius, an Icelandic manuscript written around twelve seventy. The manuscript contains poems about gods and heroes, including Volospa, Havamal, Lokasenna, and Thrymskvida.

The poems had existed before the manuscript. Their dates of composition are difficult to establish. Some may preserve older material. Others may have been shaped in the Christian period. A poem could carry inherited language while also reflecting the concerns and poetic habits of the person who performed or composed it.

The Codex Regius entered modern scholarly knowledge in the seventeenth century. Bishop Brynjolfur Sveinsson discovered it in Iceland in sixteen forty three and sent it to the Danish king. It was held in Denmark for centuries. In nineteen seventy one, the manuscript was returned to Iceland. It is now associated with the Arnamagnæan manuscript collection in Reykjavik.

The manuscript does not identify its original scribe. It does not state when each poem was first made. It does not explain which verses were learned from performance and which were copied from an earlier written exemplar. Its value is immense, but it is not a window without a frame.

The other major source is the Prose Edda, written by Snorri Sturluson around twelve twenty. Snorri was an Icelandic chieftain, poet, historian, and political figure. He lived in a Christian society. He knew the language and conventions of skaldic poetry and wrote a handbook to explain them.

The Prose Edda is not a pagan liturgical book. It is a work about poetry. Snorri wanted poets to understand kennings, the compressed metaphors that formed a central part of skaldic verse. To explain why a poet might call gold the hair of Sif, or a ship the horse of the sea, he retold myths about gods and their deeds.

Its first major section is Gylfaginning. The work frames the mythological account as a dialogue. A figure called King Gylfi travels to meet the gods, who appear under names connected with the hall of High, Just-as-High, and Third. Questions and answers then present the creation of the world, the nature of the gods, their adventures, and the destruction at Ragnarok.

This frame matters. Snorri does not write as a worshipper explaining the correct rites of Odin or Thor. He writes as a Christian author preserving the mythic knowledge needed for poetry. He presents the gods as figures of an earlier tradition, and at points his narrative uses euhemerizing explanations, treating gods as ancient people whose stories later became divine.

The Prose Edda has its own structure and aims. Snorri rearranges material. He combines poetic passages with prose explanations. He sometimes expands a brief reference into a narrative. He sometimes gives a version not found in the surviving Poetic Edda.

The skaldic poems add another body of evidence. These court poems were composed by named poets, often in praise of rulers, and many contain mythological references. Their language can be difficult because a single kenning may depend on a whole myth. A poet could refer to Thor's fishing trip, Odin's theft of the mead, or a divine object without narrating the complete story.

Such poems provide early witnesses to mythic names and relationships. They do not provide a complete catalogue of popular belief. A court poet's use of a myth for praise does not show how every household imagined the gods.

There are also outside notices. Tacitus, writing in the Roman world near the end of the first century, described Germanic peoples in Germania. He mentioned gods and ritual practices, but he did not describe the later Norse pantheon in the form preserved by Icelandic manuscripts. The distance in time and place is large.

Around ten seventy five, Adam of Bremen described a temple and cult centre at Uppsala in Sweden. His account names gods and reports ritual activity. It is valuable evidence for the eleventh century, but it was written by a Christian cleric who had his own purposes and categories. Scholars use it carefully rather than treating it as a complete description of all Scandinavian religion.

Runestones preserve names and formulas, but most are not mythological narratives. Place names can preserve associations with gods such as Thor, Freyr, or Odin. Their distribution suggests that divine names entered the landscape and social memory. A place name, however, does not state what ceremony occurred there or how often it was used.

Objects provide another line of evidence. Small hammer pendants, often identified with Mjollnir, have been found in Viking-Age contexts. Their forms vary. Some are made of silver, iron, bronze, or other materials. They show that a hammer symbol had meaning for some people, especially during the period when Christian symbols were also present.

An object can establish use, display, or burial. It cannot by itself reveal the owner's private understanding. A hammer pendant may have marked affiliation, protection, identity, or a combination of meanings.

The sources are therefore late in relation to the period when many of the myths were probably circulating. They are also Christian-era sources. Most historians do not solve this problem by discarding them. They compare poems with prose, compare mythic names with earlier inscriptions, compare literary accounts with archaeology, and mark where a conclusion rests on a later witness.

Carolyne Larrington's translation and study of the Poetic Edda presents the poems as varied works rather than as chapters of one unified theology. Jesse Byock's translation of the Prose Edda emphasizes Snorri's role as a Christian Icelandic writer preserving the mythology of poetry. Anthony Faulkes's edition and translation makes the structure and language of Snorri's Edda accessible while keeping its literary purpose in view.

John Lindow has stressed that Norse mythology is not a single systematic doctrine. Its gods and stories appear in different genres, with different emphases and sometimes unresolved tensions. Hilda Ellis Davidson used literary, archaeological, and comparative evidence to reconstruct patterns in the religious world of northern Europe, while recognizing the limits of that reconstruction.

The record can show that names, stories, objects, and places were connected. It cannot turn a thirteenth-century narrative into a direct transcript of a Viking-Age ceremony. The work of history lies in keeping those layers separate.

The first scene belongs to no human year. Before the gods, before humans, before the earth had a shore, there was Ginnungagap, a great emptiness.

In Volospa, the seeress looks back toward the first age. She describes a time when there was no sand, no sea, no cooling wave, no earth and no sky. The world had not yet taken the forms by which it could be named.

Gylfaginning gives the emptiness a geography. To the north lay Niflheim, a realm of cold, mist, and ice. To the south lay Muspell, a realm of heat and flame. Between them stretched Ginnungagap.

From Niflheim came rivers carrying cold and poison. They flowed into the emptiness. Ice gathered there. From Muspell came heat and sparks. When the frost met the warmth, the ice melted, and life appeared in the space between cold and fire.

The first living being was Ymir. Snorri describes him as the ancestor of the frost giants. He was not created by a god. He arose from the meeting of opposing forces.

A cow also appeared. Her name was Audhumla. Four rivers of milk flowed from her udders, and Ymir fed on them. Audhumla fed in another way. She licked salty blocks of ice.

On the first day, hair emerged from the ice. On the second day, a head appeared. On the third day, the whole figure came forth. This was Buri, an ancestor of the gods.

Ymir's body generated further beings. While he slept, sweat from his armpits produced a male and a female. His feet joined and produced a son. The story presents the giant race as arising through strange, bodily means rather than through a marriage or a divine decree.

Buri had a son named Bor. Bor married Bestla, the daughter of a giant. They had three sons: Odin, Vili, and Ve.

The new gods belonged to the generation that confronted Ymir. They killed him. Snorri describes the flood of blood that came from the giant's wounds. It drowned almost all of the frost giants. Only Bergelmir and his wife escaped in a lúðr, a vessel or container, and from them the giant race continued.

The gods carried Ymir's body into the middle of Ginnungagap. They made the earth from his flesh. They made the sea from his blood and sweat. They made the mountains from his bones. Stones came from his teeth and broken bones.

They made the sky from his skull and placed it above the earth. Four dwarfs held up the sky at its corners. His brain became clouds. Sparks and glowing embers from Muspell were set in the sky. Some became the sun and moon, and others became the stars.

The gods set the sun and moon in motion. In Volospa, the sun did not yet know where its hall was, and the stars did not yet know their places. The gods gave names to morning and evening, to noon and the middle of night, and to the years. Time became ordered along with the world.

The gods then established a place for themselves. It was called Asgard. They built halls and arranged the divine settlement. A bridge, Bifrost, connected their world with the human world. It appeared as a rainbow and was guarded by Heimdall.

The first humans came later. Gylfaginning says that Odin, Vili, and Ve found two trees or pieces of wood on the shore. They shaped them into human beings. Odin gave breath and life. Vili gave understanding and movement. Ve gave appearance, speech, hearing, and sight.

The man was called Ask. The woman was called Embla. From them came the human race. The gods gave them Midgard as a dwelling place. They made a boundary around it to protect it from the giants, using the giant's eyebrows.

Volospa tells the creation of humans in a more compressed form. Three powerful beings came from the company of the gods and found Ask and Embla on the shore, lacking strength and destiny. They gave them breath, understanding, movement, and form. The names of the three are not identical in every account.

The world was not complete when humans appeared. Its inhabitants included gods, giants, dwarfs, elves, the dead, and other beings. Their realms were connected and opposed, but the texts do not always arrange them in one consistent map.

Yggdrasil was the great tree that held the worlds together. Its name is often translated as Odin's horse, a reference to the tree on which Odin later hung. The tree rose through the mythic cosmos.

One root reached toward the gods' well, the well of Urd. There the Norns shaped or determined the lives of beings. The Norns were named Urd, Verdandi, and Skuld in Gylfaginning. They watered the tree and laid laws for lives.

Another root reached toward the land of the frost giants, where Mimir's well was found. Odin later sought wisdom there and gave an eye in exchange for a drink.

A third root reached toward Niflheim. Beneath it lay Hvergelmir, a spring from which rivers flowed. The dragon Nidhogg gnawed at the root. Other creatures lived in the tree. An eagle sat at the top, and a hawk sat between its eyes. A squirrel carried messages between the eagle and Nidhogg.

The tree suffered while sustaining the worlds. Deer ate its leaves. Serpents moved among its roots. The Norns tended it with water and white clay. Its condition belongs to the larger pattern of a cosmos that is alive, vulnerable, and moving toward a final crisis.

The nine worlds are named in later explanations, but the poems do not provide a single complete list in a single passage. Asgard is the home of the Aesir. Midgard is the human world. Jotunheim is associated with the giants. Niflheim belongs to cold, mist, and the dead. Muspell is the realm of fire and its forces.

Other names appear in different contexts, including the world of the Vanir, the realm of the elves, and the realm of the dwarfs. The idea of nine worlds is clear in the tradition, but the exact arrangement is not presented consistently across the surviving sources.

The creation story therefore has two forms. Volospa gives an elevated poem of beginnings, naming the emptiness, the shaping of the earth, the coming of humans, and the tree. Gylfaginning supplies a more continuous narrative, with Audhumla, Buri, Bor, Bestla, Ymir's death, and the detailed division of his body.

Snorri's account is known from the Prose Edda manuscripts, especially the tradition represented by the early thirteenth-century text. Volospa is chiefly known through the Codex Regius, copied around twelve seventy, though parts of it also survive elsewhere. Their wording and order are not identical.

Scholars disagree about how much Snorri inherited from lost poems and how much he arranged himself. Margaret Clunies Ross emphasizes the literary and cultural processes by which mythic traditions were shaped in Old Norse poetry. The evidence supports comparison, not a single recoverable original version.

Odin's pursuit of knowledge began with a sacrifice made to himself.

The well of Mimir lay beneath one of the roots of Yggdrasil. Its water carried wisdom. In the tradition preserved by Snorri, Odin wished to drink from it. Mimir demanded a pledge. Odin gave one of his eyes.

The text does not describe the wound as a scene of public ritual. It states the price and its result. Odin gained knowledge, while the eye remained associated with the well. Volospa later refers to the eye that Odin had left as a pledge. The image joins wisdom with loss.

Odin's knowledge was never complete. He sought answers from gods, giants, seers, and the dead. In Volospa, he questions the seeress about the origins of the world, the death of Baldr, and the events that will lead to Ragnarok. She knows more than the god asks her to reveal.

Havamal gives Odin another ordeal. The speaker says that he knew he hung on a windy tree for nine whole nights, wounded by a spear and given to Odin, himself to himself. He looked down and took up the runes, then fell from the tree.

The passage is a speech in the first person, but its mythic voice is not the same as an ordinary historical report. It presents the discovery of runic knowledge as a sacrifice. Odin does not receive the runes freely. He undergoes pain, isolation, and deathlike suspension.

Havamal continues with advice about the powers of the runes. Some passages describe how runes can assist in healing, release, protection, travel, speech, and conflict. The poem does not provide a simple manual of one uniform practice. It presents knowledge as dangerous, useful, and dependent on the person who possesses it.

The same poem gives Odin a second kind of knowledge: verbal skill. He speaks of winning mead, gaining wisdom, and learning how to interpret speech. The poem combines sayings about conduct with divine myth. Odin is both the speaker of practical counsel and the figure who gained supernatural wisdom.

Odin travelled with two ravens, Huginn and Muninn. Gylfaginning says that they flew over the world and returned to tell him what they had seen. Their names are commonly connected with thought and memory. Odin sat in his hall while the birds gathered information beyond it.

The god also had wolves, Geri and Freki. He gave them all the meat set before him because wine was enough for him as food. His appearance varied by occasion. He could be an armed ruler, a traveller, a one-eyed old man, or a figure in a wide hat and cloak.

The myths associate him with war and the dead. Warriors killed in battle could be selected for Odin's hall. The valkyries carried some of the fallen to Valhalla, where they prepared for a future battle. Other dead went elsewhere. The sources do not make all dead people belong to one destination.

Odin's authority was also connected with poetry. The story of the mead of poetry, told by Snorri, begins with a peace settlement between the Aesir and the Vanir.

The two divine groups had fought a war. The sources do not offer one complete cause in every version. The conflict involved the assault or mistreatment of Gullveig, a female figure associated in Volospa with gold and repeated burning, and it developed into a war between the two groups.

The Aesir and the Vanir exchanged blows. Neither side won a final victory. They made peace by creating a shared token. Each side spat into a vessel, and from the mixture they made Kvasir.

Kvasir was the wisest of beings. He travelled widely and answered questions. Two dwarfs, Fjalar and Galar, killed him. They mixed his blood with honey and made the mead of poetry. Whoever drank it became a poet or a scholar.

The dwarfs told the gods that Kvasir had drowned in his own wisdom. They also killed the giant Gilling and his wife. The giant's son, Suttung, demanded compensation. The dwarfs gave him the mead.

Suttung placed the mead with his daughter Gunnlod inside a mountain. Odin wanted it. He worked for Suttung's brother Baugi, drilling through the mountain with an auger. When the hole was made, Odin changed into a serpent and entered.

Inside, Odin spoke with Gunnlod. The prose account says that he stayed with her for three nights and received three drinks. With each drink he emptied one of the vessels holding the mead. He then changed into an eagle and flew away.

Suttung pursued him in another eagle form. Odin reached Asgard and released the mead into vessels. Some of it spilled behind him. Snorri says that the portion that spilled became available to bad poets, while the portion he carried became the gift of poetry.

This story presents poetry as stolen, guarded, and unevenly distributed. It also links the skill of skalds with the character of Odin. A poet's art was not merely technical decoration. In the myth, it came from a substance born of violence, intelligence, and deception.

The war between the Aesir and the Vanir ended with an exchange of hostages. Njord, Freyr, and Freyja came to the Aesir. In return, the Aesir sent Hoenir and Mimir.

Njord was associated with wealth, seafaring, and prosperity. Freyr was connected with fertility, peace, good seasons, and kingship. Freyja was associated with love, beauty, wealth, magic, and the reception of some of the battle dead. These associations come from different texts and are not organized into one official theology.

Hoenir appeared impressive and strong, but he relied on Mimir's counsel. When Mimir was absent, Hoenir gave uncertain answers. The Vanir killed Mimir, cut off his head, and sent it back to Odin. Odin preserved it with herbs and spoke charms over it. The head then gave him counsel.

The story makes political exchange part of divine history. The gods are not a single unchanging family. They belong to groups with different powers and origins. Peace does not erase the distinction. It brings the groups into one order.

Odin's identity is therefore assembled from several roles. He is the god who sacrifices an eye, hangs on the tree, gathers knowledge through ravens, commands the dead, wins poetry through deception, and helps govern a world threatened by forces beyond his control.

Havamal is preserved in the Codex Regius, though its sections may have different histories and voices. Volospa is also preserved there. Snorri's account of Kvasir and the mead appears in Skaldskaparmal, the second major section of the Prose Edda.

Jesse Byock describes Snorri's Edda as a handbook in which myths explain poetic language. Anthony Faulkes's work on the text shows how carefully its prose must be read as a medieval Icelandic composition. The surviving sources support Odin's association with wisdom, poetry, sacrifice, and war, but they do not establish how every historical community ranked those powers.

The hammer was gone before the wedding began.

Thrymskvida says that Thor woke and found Mjollnir missing. No one knew where it had gone. Loki went to Freyja and asked to borrow her feather garment, which allowed its wearer to fly. Freyja agreed.

Loki travelled to Jotunheim. There he found Thrym, a giant, sitting on a burial mound. Thrym had stolen the hammer and hidden it deep in the earth. He would return it only if Freyja were given to him as a bride.

Loki returned to Asgard and told the gods. They went to Freyja and asked her to prepare for the journey. Freyja became angry. Her anger shook the hall, and her necklace broke. She refused to be sent to a giant.

The gods then sought another plan. Heimdall proposed that Thor should dress as the bride. He would wear a bridal veil and carry the appearance of Freyja. Thor objected, but Loki argued that the gods would lose their weapon if the plan failed.

Thor put on the bridal clothing. Loki accompanied him as a maidservant. The two travelled to Jotunheim and arrived at Thrym's hall.

The giant prepared a feast. The bride ate an ox, eight salmon, and delicacies intended for the women. Thor also drank three casks of mead. Thrym was astonished by the appetite.

Loki explained that the bride had not eaten for eight nights because she had been so eager to reach Jotunheim. The explanation satisfied the giant.

Thrym lifted the veil to kiss the bride. He recoiled from the eyes. They were bright and fierce. Loki explained that the bride had not slept for eight nights because of eagerness.

The giant's sister then asked for a bridal gift. Thrym ordered Mjollnir to be brought and placed on the bride's lap. He intended the hammer to consecrate the marriage.

Thor seized Mjollnir. He struck Thrym and killed him. He struck the giant's sister and the other giants in the hall. The marriage became a recovery of the weapon and a reversal of the giant's demand.

The poem is comic in its use of appetite, clothing, and deception, but the object at its centre is serious. Mjollnir is the instrument by which Thor protects the gods and defeats their enemies. The story makes its return depend on Thor entering a role that the poem marks as humiliating but effective.

Another tale begins with Thor setting out to test his strength among the giants. He travelled with Loki and came to a hall ruled by Utgard-Loki. The hall was enormous, and its ruler challenged the visitors to contests.

Thor wanted to show his power. Utgard-Loki asked what skills the travellers possessed. Loki competed in eating against a being named Logi. Loki ate quickly, but Logi consumed both the food and the wooden trough. The contest revealed that Logi was fire itself.

A companion of Thor named Thjalfi raced Hugi. Thjalfi was swift, but Hugi was thought itself. The human or divine competitor could not win.

Thor then faced three trials. He was asked to empty a drinking horn. He drank deeply, but the level fell only a little. Utgard-Loki said that the task had been modest and that Thor had failed.

Next, Thor was asked to lift a cat from the floor. He strained and raised one paw, but the cat's back remained on the ground.

Finally, an old woman named Elli challenged Thor to wrestle. Thor fought her with all his strength. Slowly, she forced him down until one knee touched the floor.

The visitors were allowed to leave. After they had gone beyond the hall, Utgard-Loki revealed the truth. The drinking horn was connected to the sea. Thor had drunk so much that the level of the ocean had fallen, producing the tides.

The cat was Jormungand, the world serpent, which lay encircling the earth. Thor had lifted part of the serpent from the ground. The old woman was old age. No one could defeat her completely.

The ruler himself and the hall then disappeared. Utgard-Loki explained the contests as illusions or transformations that had tested Thor's strength without allowing him to understand the true scale of what he faced.

Thor's strength had not been disproved. The story had instead placed it against forces that could not be overcome by force alone: the sea, the serpent surrounding the world, thought, fire, and age.

Thor later set out to fish for Jormungand. The serpent was one of the children of Loki and Angrboda. Odin had cast it into the sea because he feared what Loki's children would do. Jormungand grew until it encircled Midgard and grasped its own tail.

In the fishing story preserved by Gylfaginning, Thor travelled with the giant Hymir. Thor wanted bait and used the head of Hymir's largest ox. The two went out to sea.

Hymir rowed until he reached the place where he usually fished for whales. Thor demanded that they go farther. They continued until Hymir became afraid of the waters.

Thor fixed the ox head to his hook and cast the line. Jormungand took the bait. The serpent sank beneath the sea. Thor pulled against it. His feet broke through the boat, and he stood on the seabed while holding the line.

The serpent emerged. Its poison was directed at Thor. The story describes the struggle as a meeting of the god's strength and the creature's enormous body.

Thor raised Mjollnir to strike the serpent. In one version, Hymir cut the line before the blow could land. In another, Thor struck Jormungand and drove it back into the sea. The difference reflects the unstable transmission of the episode.

Thor returned from the sea. The encounter was not a final defeat of Jormungand. The serpent remained in the world and would emerge again at Ragnarok.

The three stories establish a pattern. Thor restores the hammer through disguise, survives the humiliation of the giants' contests, and reaches the world serpent without destroying it. His strength protects the ordered world, but it does not make that world invulnerable.

Thrymskvida is preserved in the Codex Regius, while the accounts of Utgard-Loki and the fishing trip are found in Gylfaginning. Snorri's prose gives the giant contests a sequence and explains their hidden meanings. The poem and the prose do not form one single original narrative.

Carolyne Larrington treats Thrymskvida as a poem with its own comic and heroic design, not simply as a fragment of Snorri's account. John Lindow places Thor's encounters within a wider pattern in which the god defends the human and divine order while repeatedly meeting powers that exceed ordinary measure.

The texts establish that Mjollnir, Jormungand, and Thor's struggles were central elements of the surviving mythic tradition, while the exact form of each story changed between poetic and prose transmission.

The trouble begins with hair.

In Skaldskaparmal, Loki cuts the golden hair from Sif, Thor's wife. Thor discovers what has happened and threatens Loki with violence. Loki promises to obtain new hair from the dwarfs.

He goes first to the sons of Ivaldi. They make hair of gold that grows like ordinary hair when it is placed on Sif's head. They also make Skidbladnir, a ship that can carry all the gods and fold small enough to fit in a pocket, and Gungnir, the spear of Odin, which never misses its mark.

Loki then wagers his head with the dwarf Brok that Brok's brother Sindri cannot make treasures better than these. Sindri begins the work. He places a pigskin in the forge and tells Brok to keep pumping the bellows without stopping.

Loki changes himself into a fly and bites Brok. Brok continues.

Sindri places gold in the forge. Loki bites him harder. Brok continues.

Sindri puts iron into the fire and warns Brok not to stop pumping. Loki bites the dwarf on the eyelid. Blood runs into Brok's eye. He wipes it away for a moment, and the work is damaged.

The objects are judged by the gods. Sindri's gold becomes Draupnir, a ring from which eight rings of equal weight drop every ninth night. The iron becomes Mjollnir, the hammer of Thor. Its handle is shorter than intended because Loki's bite forced Brok to pause. The hammer still strikes whatever it is thrown at and returns to Thor's hand.

The gods decide that the treasures of the sons of Ivaldi and Sindri are all valuable. Loki has lost the wager. Brok claims his head.

Loki argues that the wager concerned his head, not his neck. Brok cannot cut through the neck without taking more than the head. Since the agreement gives no permission to damage the neck, Loki escapes the immediate penalty. He does allow Brok to sew his lips shut with a leather thong, but later removes it by tearing the stitches.

The story then turns to the wall of Asgard.

A builder arrives and offers to construct a strong wall around the gods' home. He asks for the sun, the moon and Freyja as payment. The gods consider the demand too high. They agree to it only if the builder can finish the wall in a single winter, and only if he receives no help from any human being. Loki adds the condition.

The builder has a horse named Svadilfari. The horse is extraordinarily strong. It drags enormous stones while the builder works. As winter comes to an end, the wall is almost complete. The gods see that the builder will succeed.

They blame Loki, because his proposal created the agreement. Loki is ordered to prevent completion. He changes himself into a mare. Svadilfari breaks away from the builder and follows Loki. The builder cannot finish the wall before the deadline.

The gods refuse to pay. The builder reveals his giant nature and attacks them. Thor kills him with Mjollnir.

Loki returns after some time with an eight-legged foal. The foal is called Sleipnir. Odin takes the horse, and Sleipnir becomes the swiftest of horses.

The prose narrative next names Loki's children with Angrboda, a giantess in Jotunheim. They are Fenrir, Jormungand and Hel.

The gods learn that these children are being raised in Jotunheim. They also receive prophecies concerning them. Odin orders that Jormungand be thrown into the sea surrounding Midgard. The serpent grows until it encircles the world and bites its own tail.

Hel is sent to Niflheim. Odin gives her authority over those who die from sickness and old age. Her realm is described as vast. One side of her body appears living, while the other appears corpse-like. The dead enter her domain through gates.

Fenrir remains among the gods. He grows quickly and becomes so powerful that only Tyr has the courage to approach him. The gods decide to bind him.

They first use chains called Laeding and then Dromi. Fenrir breaks both. The gods ask the dwarfs to make a stronger bond. The dwarfs produce Gleipnir from six things that cannot be gathered: the sound of a cat's footfall, a woman's beard, the roots of mountains, the sinews of bears, the breath of fish and the spittle of birds.

Gleipnir looks like a soft ribbon, but it is stronger than any chain.

Fenrir suspects a trick. The gods take him to an island and ask him to let them test the bond. Fenrir says that a demonstration would be meaningless unless one of the gods places a hand in his mouth as a pledge that he will be released.

The gods hesitate. Tyr places his right hand between Fenrir's jaws.

Fenrir is bound with Gleipnir. The gods fasten the bond to a great stone and drive the stone deep into the ground. They attach another stone beneath it. Fenrir strains, but the bond does not break.

The gods laugh. Fenrir closes his jaws and bites off Tyr's hand. Tyr loses the hand, but the gods have secured the wolf.

Fenrir continues to struggle. The gods place a sword between his jaws, with the point against the upper jaw and the hilt against the lower. His howling becomes the source of a river called Ván. He remains bound until the events of Ragnarok.

This passage combines several kinds of material. Skaldskaparmal explains the origins of objects that poets use as names or images. Mjollnir, Gungnir, Draupnir and Sleipnir are not merely possessions in the narrative; they also become part of the vocabulary of later verse. Anthony Faulkes's edition of Snorri's Edda presents these stories within a work composed in Iceland in the thirteenth century.

The story of the builder belongs to Gylfaginning, while the account of Fenrir appears there as well. Snorri arranges separate myths into a continuous account of the gods. The Poetic Edda contains related material, but not every episode appears there in the same form. Scholars therefore treat Snorri as both a transmitter of older traditions and an author who shaped them into a learned narrative.

The physical evidence confirms the importance of several divine names without confirming every tale. Thor's hammer amulets have been found across Scandinavia, and horse equipment appears in graves and settlements. Those objects show that the hammer and the horse had religious and social significance. They do not establish that Norse communities told the story of the dwarf craftsmen, the wall builder or Tyr's hand in precisely Snorri's form.

Baldr's death begins with dreams.

In Gylfaginning, Baldr dreams of danger. He tells the gods, and they understand that his life may be threatened. Odin rides to Hel to learn more. There he finds a prophetess and questions her about the dead. She says that a couch has been prepared for Baldr and that the gods will mourn.

Odin returns with this knowledge.

Frigg, Baldr's mother, travels through the world. She asks every living thing and every object to swear that it will not harm Baldr. Fire, water, metals, stones, earth, trees, sickness, animals, birds and snakes make the oath. Frigg does not demand an oath from mistletoe. It is young, small and appears too harmless to matter.

The gods then make a game of Baldr's protection. They throw weapons and stones at him. They strike him with other objects. Nothing injures him. The gods regard the game as proof of his invulnerability.

Loki learns that mistletoe was excluded. He finds the plant, cuts it and makes a dart from it.

At the gathering stands Hodr, Baldr's blind brother. Loki asks why Hodr is not taking part. Hodr explains that he cannot see where Baldr stands and has no weapon. Loki gives him the mistletoe dart and directs his hand.

Hodr throws. The dart strikes Baldr, and Baldr falls dead.

The gods are silent. The game has become a killing.

Odin's son Hermod agrees to ride to Hel. Odin gives him Sleipnir. Hermod reaches the realm of the dead and finds Baldr seated in a place of honour beside Hel.

Hermod asks for Baldr's release. Hel says that Baldr may return if all things in the world, living and dead, weep for him. If even one thing refuses, Baldr must remain.

The gods send messengers throughout the world. Every creature and object weeps. Then the messengers find a giantess named Thokk in a cave. She refuses to shed tears. She says that Hel should keep what she has.

The gods believe that Thokk is Loki in disguise. Because of her refusal, Baldr does not return.

Gylfaginning then turns to Loki's punishment. The gods find him near a waterfall. He has changed himself into a salmon and tries to escape. They catch him in a net. Loki struggles, but Thor seizes him.

The gods take Loki to a cavern. They place him beneath three stones. A serpent is fixed above him, and venom drips onto his face. Sigyn, his wife, holds a bowl over him and catches the venom. When the bowl becomes full, she leaves to empty it. Venom then falls on Loki. His convulsions are presented as the cause of earthquakes.

Loki remains bound until Ragnarok.

The poem Lokasenna presents an earlier and more confrontational scene. Aegir holds a feast for the gods. Loki arrives after a servant has refused him entry. When Odin grants him a place, Loki demands his share of the drink and begins to accuse the guests.

He insults Bragi's courage and questions the loyalty of other gods. He attacks Njord for his marriage and refers to Freyr's relationship with Gerd. He mocks Tyr for the loss of his hand. He accuses Frigg of sexual misconduct, and Frigg answers that she will not reveal the secrets of her own family. Loki then attacks Frigg again.

The exchanges continue. Freyja condemns Loki's behaviour and says that he is speaking falsely. Loki answers with another accusation. Thor arrives late. Loki insults him as well, but Thor threatens him with Mjollnir. Loki retreats, saying that the hammer will not strike him while the gods are inside the hall.

The poem ends with Loki fleeing. Its subsequent prose tradition tells of his capture and binding.

Lokasenna does not narrate Baldr's death in the same sequence as Gylfaginning. It refers to Baldr and to Hodr, and it places Loki among the gods as an abusive speaker whose accusations expose rivalries, sexual honour and obligations between kin. Gylfaginning makes the death of Baldr the central event that leads toward Ragnarok. Volospa gives a different emphasis. Its seeress presents Baldr's death as part of a larger vision of cosmic collapse, mourning and renewal, and it does not supply Snorri's full chain of explanation.

The contrast matters because the poems and Snorri's prose are not identical witnesses. Carolyne Larrington's translation of The Poetic Edda follows the principal medieval manuscript, Codex Regius, while also noting places where the text is damaged or difficult. Codex Regius was written in the thirteenth century, but the poems it preserves were composed at different times and may have circulated orally before being written down.

The mistletoe story is known in full from Snorri's account. Volospa's treatment is shorter and more allusive. Some scholars see Baldr's story as an old myth reshaped by Christian ideas of death and return. Others argue that the evidence is too limited to identify a single source of the story's form. The manuscripts establish the wording that survived, not the exact beliefs of every earlier community.

The final movement begins with winter.

Volospa describes a great winter, with snow coming from every direction. The seasons lose their ordinary order. Kinship bonds fail. Violence spreads among human beings. The poem presents this period as the approach of Ragnarok.

Gylfaginning calls it the Fimbul winter. It lasts through three successive winters, with no summer between them. Before it begins, a period of conflict divides families and destroys social order.

The sun and moon are pursued by wolves. In Gylfaginning, one wolf swallows the sun and another takes the moon. Volospa describes the sun becoming dark and the earth sinking into the sea. The stars disappear from the sky.

The bonds of the monsters break.

Fenrir escapes from Gleipnir. Jormungand rises from the sea and spreads poison through the air. The sea surges over the land. Naglfar, the ship made from the nails of the dead, moves across the water. The ship carries the forces of the giants, while Loki takes command.

The sky splits. Muspell's people advance, led by Surtr. His sword shines like fire. The bridge Bifrost breaks beneath the weight of the advancing host.

Heimdall blows his horn, Gjallarhorn. The sound reaches every part of the world. Odin consults Mimir's head, and the gods gather for their final battle.

The order of the deaths is given with differences between the sources.

Odin fights Fenrir. The wolf swallows him. Odin's son Vidar kills Fenrir by placing one foot on the wolf's lower jaw and pulling apart its mouth. Gylfaginning describes a special shoe made from scraps of leather that shoemakers cut from the toes and heels of ordinary shoes.

Thor faces Jormungand. He kills the serpent with Mjollnir. He walks nine steps away and then falls dead from the serpent's poison.

Tyr fights Garm, the hound guarding Hel's realm. They kill one another.

Freyr confronts Surtr. Freyr has given his sword to Skirnir in the story of Gerd, and he now lacks the weapon that would have served him. He fights with a stag's antler and is killed.

Heimdall and Loki fight each other. Each dies.

The fire of Surtr spreads across the world. Earth and sky burn. The mountains collapse. The sea covers the land.

Then the poem's imagery changes.

A green earth rises from the sea. The fields produce crops without being sown. The surviving gods meet again. Odin's sons Vidar and Vali remain alive, and Thor's sons Modi and Magni possess Mjollnir. Baldr and Hodr return from the realm of the dead. Hoenir survives in Volospa, while the details differ between the poem and Gylfaginning.

Two human beings, Lif and Lifthrasir, survive in a wood. They live on morning dew and become the ancestors of a renewed human population. The sun has a daughter who follows the path of the dead sun.

The final figure is not described in the same way by every source. Volospa ends with the seeress seeing a powerful figure descend from above and rule. The wording is difficult and has been read in relation to Christian imagery by many scholars. Gylfaginning also ends with a renewed world but frames the account through its conversation between King Gylfi and the disguised Odin.

This is one of the main points of debate. Some scholars argue that the vision of a final ruler, the moral collapse before the destruction and the renewed world reflect Christian influence in the Icelandic versions. Iceland had been formally converted around the year one thousand, more than two centuries before the surviving manuscripts were written. Christian writers knew biblical accounts of judgment, fire and renewal.

Other scholars emphasize that destruction and regeneration are also compatible with older Germanic mythic patterns. The poems do not identify a single author or date, and no pre-Christian manuscript preserves the whole sequence. The evidence supports influence as a possibility, not a complete explanation.

John Lindow treats Ragnarok as a complex narrative assembled from several traditions rather than a simple statement of one uniform ancient doctrine. Carolyne Larrington's notes likewise distinguish the language of Volospa from Snorri's more systematic prose. Snorri may preserve older material, but he also chooses its order, supplies explanations and connects separate stories.

Volospa's version is compressed and prophetic. It moves through images: the wolf, the darkened sun, the broken bonds, the burning earth and the returning green land. Gylfaginning expands these images into actions and conversations. It names the participants, explains the weapons and places the events into a larger account of the gods.

The material record offers no direct archaeological testimony for a single universally accepted Ragnarok narrative. Fire damage at settlements can have ordinary causes. Depictions of armed gods, serpents, ships and mounted figures may relate to myths, but an image alone rarely identifies a complete story. The end of the world is therefore known chiefly through medieval poetry and prose, not through a surviving pre-Christian description of a ritual or a creed.

The myths describe gods. The evidence for worship comes from other kinds of sources.

In Skirnismal, Freyr sees Gerd, a giant's daughter, and falls into grief because he desires her. He sends Skirnir to Jotunheim with gifts and threats. Skirnir offers eleven golden apples and the ring Draupnir. Gerd refuses. He then threatens her with a magical staff and with exclusion from human society. At last she agrees to meet Freyr at a particular grove after nine nights.

The poem is not a record of a ceremony. It is a mythic dialogue about desire, exchange, power and marriage. Freyr's connection with fertility and prosperity appears in later descriptions as well, but the poem itself does not provide a general list of what every worshipper believed.

Adam of Bremen, writing in the eleventh century, describes a temple at Uppsala and names Odin, Thor and Freyr as the principal gods. He assigns them different functions in his account. Odin is connected with war, Thor with power and weather, and Freyr with peace and fertility.

Adam was a Christian writer based in northern Germany. His description was written from a distance and may reflect information gathered through intermediaries. It is valuable because it gives a location, divine names and an account of public cult, but it is not a neutral transcript of an Uppsala ceremony. Archaeology at Gamla Uppsala shows monumental halls, mounds and a powerful central settlement. It has not produced a surviving temple inscription that verifies every detail in Adam's account.

The word blot appears in Old Norse sources for a sacrifice or religious offering. Written accounts connect such rites with feasting and with gifts to gods or other supernatural powers. The sources mention animals, blood and communal meals, but they vary in detail and date. Descriptions by Christian authors are shaped by their own purposes. They establish that sacrifice and feasting were remembered as parts of pre-Christian religion; they do not provide a universal procedure followed throughout Scandinavia.

Place names add another kind of evidence. Names incorporating Thor, Freyr, Freyja, Njord and Odin occur in parts of Scandinavia. A concentration of names associated with a god can indicate local importance, especially when the names refer to farms, fields or other settled places. Place names are difficult to date precisely, and their meaning is not always certain. They show patterns of devotion over landscapes rather than a single religious system.

Small hammer pendants are among the clearest objects associated with Thor. They have been found in graves and settlements from the Viking Age, including examples of metal and other materials. Their shape connects them with Mjollnir, although not every pendant carries an inscription or an unmistakable image. Some may have marked identity, protection or participation in a religious tradition. The objects do not reveal the words spoken over them or the beliefs held by each person who wore one.

Graves show differences in burial treatment. Some contain weapons, jewellery, animals, vehicles or tools. Others contain very little. Ship burials and richly furnished graves indicate status and social memory, but grave goods cannot be translated directly into a complete theology. They may represent possessions, offerings, symbols of rank or objects placed for reasons now unknown.

The Vanir also appear in evidence outside the poems. Freyr, Freyja and Njord are named in literature, place names and later genealogical traditions. Tacitus, writing in the first century, describes a goddess called Nerthus among Germanic peoples, but the identification of Nerthus with Njord remains a scholarly proposal rather than a certainty. The sources do not show that all communities divided the gods into two clearly organized groups called the Aesir and Vanir.

Iceland accepted Christianity in the year one thousand, according to the account in Ari the Wise's Íslendingabók and the later narrative of the conversion. The decision was made at the Althing, where public acceptance of Christianity was followed by a limited permission for some older private practices. The change was political as well as religious. Iceland remained connected to a wider Christian world through law, trade and ecclesiastical organization.

The Scandinavian kingdoms converted at different times and through long processes. Denmark had Christian rulers and missions by the tenth century, while Christian institutions expanded under later kings. Norway's conversion is associated in the written sources with rulers such as Olaf Tryggvason and Olaf Haraldsson, though the process involved local variation and conflict. Sweden converted over a longer period, and pre-Christian practices continued after royal and institutional Christianity had taken root.

Hilda Ellis Davidson argued that myths, objects and place names should be read together, while keeping their different evidential limits. John Lindow emphasizes the distance between literary myth and the reconstruction of actual ritual. Both approaches avoid treating a medieval poem as a direct report from every earlier shrine.

The evidence points to a varied religious world. Odin, Thor, Freyr and Freyja were important in different regions and contexts. Offerings, feasts, graves, place names and amulets show forms of devotion and social identity. No surviving source gives a complete account of what all Norse people worshipped.

The Eddas survive in thirteenth-century Icelandic manuscripts.

Codex Regius preserves the main collection of poems now called the Poetic Edda. Its scribe wrote it in the thirteenth century, probably around the second half of that century. The poems themselves are older than the manuscript, but their exact dates differ. Some may reach back to the Viking Age. Others may have been revised, combined or transmitted through oral performance before being written.

The prose Edda was composed by Snorri Sturluson in the thirteenth century. Gylfaginning gives a narrative of the gods from the making of the world to Ragnarok. Skaldskaparmal explains poetic language through stories of divine objects and conflicts. Snorri's work is a guide to poetry as well as a mythology.

Snorri often presents the gods as ancient human rulers. In his opening frame, Odin and his followers come from Troy and establish kingdoms in the north. Later generations mistake these powerful ancestors for gods. This method is called euhemerism. It was common in Christian and classical writing, where pagan gods could be explained as deified human beings.

Some scholars regard Snorri's euhemerism as a literary frame placed around older mythic material. Others stress that it affects the way he presents the gods and that his account cannot be separated from his Christian education, his knowledge of European learning and his aims as a poet and historian. Snorri preserves traditions, but he also arranges and interprets them.

The poems raise a related question about Christian influence. Iceland had been Christian for more than two centuries when the surviving manuscripts were made. Images of death, judgment, fire and renewal can be compared with Christian writing. The similarities are real, but the poems do not identify the sources of their imagery. Older Germanic traditions may also account for parts of the material. Scholars therefore disagree about the extent and direction of influence.

The Eddas became known more widely in Europe after their study and publication in the early modern period. In the nineteenth century, Jacob and Wilhelm Grimm treated Germanic mythology and language as evidence for the history of Germanic culture. Later writers, composers and artists drew on Odin, Thor, Baldr and Ragnarok, often transforming the medieval material for new purposes. Those modern adaptations are part of the reception history, not evidence for what Viking Age communities believed.

The historical record can establish that Norse myths were preserved in medieval Icelandic poetry and prose. It can identify manuscripts, objects, place names, graves and foreign reports. It can compare the wording of Volospa, Lokasenna, Gylfaginning and Skaldskaparmal. It cannot establish the private belief of an individual worshipper, the exact words spoken at a lost rite or one single date for the composition of every poem.

The imagined farmer from the opening has no recoverable name and no documented biography. Nothing establishes which god he honoured, whether he attended a feast, or how he understood the stories of Baldr and Ragnarok. The world around him is clearer: farms carried names associated with gods, graves received objects, and communities lived through a gradual transition from older cults to Christianity.

The Eddas preserve stories, not a complete religion.
