The End of Roman Paganism: How Rome's Ancient Gods Lost Their Empire

From Numa's rites and the pax deorum to Decius, Constantine, Julian, the Altar of Victory and Theodosius: how public sacrifice ended and why the old gods did not vanish overnight.

Full script

Italy, sometime in the early first century before the common era.

The rain had not come for forty days.

Beyond a small village, wheat had yellowed before it was ready for harvest. The earth had hardened beneath the sun. A farmer stood in his field, rubbing a clod of dry soil between his fingers, as if he might discover a reason for the sky's silence.

Let us call him Marcus.

He is an imagined Roman farmer, not a person preserved in the historical record. But the world around him is grounded in the evidence: a world where harvests mattered, household worship was common, and people made offerings to divine powers they hoped would protect their families.

That evening, Marcus returned home with what remained of the grain. His wife had placed a small offering before the household shrine. Figures of the Lares, protective powers associated with the home and family, stood in the lamplight.

Their child had been ill for days. Their stores were running low. Marcus had already prayed, offered wine, and promised a portion of the next harvest if the gods allowed it to exist.

Now he made another promise. The small silver ornament left to him by his father would be given up if his child recovered and the rain returned.

His wife watched him place it beside the offering.

Neither of them knew whether the gods had heard.

They could only perform the rite, honor the powers they believed governed their world, and wait.

For a modern audience, it might seem that Marcus was trying to purchase divine favor. A Roman might have understood the exchange differently. A vow created an obligation. If a god granted the requested outcome, the person who made the vow was expected to fulfill it. The relationship was not always gentle, and it was not always comforting. It was practical, public, inherited, and deeply woven into daily life.

A family honored its household gods. A farmer prayed for the land. A soldier made a vow before battle. A city performed ceremonies when disaster threatened the community. Rome itself sought to maintain a favorable relationship with the gods, a condition Roman writers called pax deorum: the peace, or good standing, between the divine powers and the Roman people.

And that relationship had consequences far beyond a household shrine.

If the gods protected Rome, then a military defeat might mean more than the failure of an army. A plague might require more than medicine. A strange event in the sky could be interpreted as a sign that public rites had to be performed before the state could safely proceed.

Religion was part of the machinery of Roman power.

But the Romans did not worship just one god. Their world was crowded with divine powers: gods of war, boundaries, fertility, healing, the hearth, the city, and the fields. Some belonged to old local traditions. Others came from Greek cities, Egypt, Anatolia, and the wider Mediterranean. Rome could welcome a foreign god during a crisis, regulate a cult it considered dangerous, or turn an ancient rite into a ceremony of imperial loyalty.

For centuries, the old gods stood at the heart of Roman public life.

Then a new religious movement spread through the empire. Some of its followers refused to sacrifice to the traditional gods, even when refusal could cost them their freedom or their lives. Emperors demanded public conformity. Later, an emperor would favor the faith that earlier rulers had tried to suppress.

And eventually, the government that had upheld the old ceremonies began passing laws against public sacrifice.

The change did not happen in one day. Temples did not all close at once. Beliefs did not vanish with a single decree. In some places, traditional worship continued after imperial power had turned against it.

So how did a civilization that sought divine protection for nearly every aspect of public life come to restrict the worship of its ancestral gods? And did those gods really disappear?

To answer, we must go back to the stories Romans told about their own beginnings, and to the institutions through which they believed the divine world could be kept in balance.

Roman tradition placed Numa Pompilius after Romulus, the founder and first king of Rome. In the story preserved by the historian Livy, Romulus had established the city through war, while Numa brought order through religion.

Numa was said to have come from the Sabine town of Cures. The Romans chose him because he was known for justice and religious knowledge. Where Romulus embodied military energy, Numa represented restraint. He turned attention away from conquest and toward ceremonies, laws, priesthoods, and the calendar.

It is a compelling story about the kind of city Rome wanted to believe it had become. But there is an important problem: Livy wrote during the age of Augustus, centuries after the period in which Numa was supposed to have lived. His account preserves Roman tradition. It is not a contemporary record of the king's actions.

According to Livy, Numa established priestly offices and placed the supervision of public rites under the pontifex maximus. He appointed flamines, priests devoted to particular gods. The flamen Dialis served Jupiter, and the office carried strict rules governing clothing, movement, and ritual conduct.

Numa was also credited with establishing the Vestal Virgins, who maintained the sacred fire of Vesta. The fire represented the continuity of the Roman community. Its care belonged to the city, not simply to one household. The Vestals held an unusual legal and social position, with privileges unavailable to most Roman women, but their office also imposed severe obligations.

The Greek writer Plutarch told a related story in his Life of Numa. He connected the king with the nymph Egeria, who supposedly advised him in sacred matters. Plutarch's version emphasized philosophy, moderation, and the political value of reverence. The story of Egeria is part of the tradition surrounding Numa; it cannot independently establish that such a meeting took place.

Numa was also associated with the calendar, boundary worship, and the Salii, priests who carried sacred shields and performed dances and songs. In these stories, religion restrained violence and protected the ordered relationships between households and communities. Boundaries mattered because property mattered. The calendar mattered because ceremonies had to occur at the right time. Priests mattered because a rite performed incorrectly could fail to achieve its purpose.

How much of this system truly began with one king? We cannot know. Archaeology confirms that major cult centers and monumental religious buildings existed in early Rome, but it cannot prove that Numa personally founded each priesthood or designed the first calendar.

That distinction matters. The stories of Numa tell us not only about Rome's uncertain beginnings, but about how later Romans explained their institutions. By attributing important rites to an ancient king, they gave those rites the authority of tradition.

The religious inheritance of early Rome was also more complicated than a single foundation story suggests. Latin communities maintained household customs, agricultural observances, boundary rites, and offerings to ancestral powers. Rome developed in central Italy, in contact with neighboring peoples. Etruscan religious specialists influenced Roman practices of divination and the interpretation of lightning and other signs. Greek communities in southern Italy contributed myths, images, and forms of worship.

Roman religion was never sealed off from the outside world.

The Romans often identified their gods with Greek counterparts. Jupiter was associated with Zeus, Juno with Hera, and Minerva with Athena. This process made communication between religious traditions possible, but it did not erase every difference. A Roman god could share a name or image with a Greek deity while retaining distinctive local rites and political meanings.

At the center of Roman public religion stood Jupiter, Juno, and Minerva, known together as the Capitoline Triad. Their great temple on the Capitoline Hill was among the most important religious landmarks in Rome. Triumphs, vows, and state ceremonies connected the hill to the power of the city.

The earliest phases of the temple are difficult to reconstruct because later building destroyed or covered much of the evidence. Archaeology establishes the existence of a major early sanctuary and its repeated rebuilding. It does not give us a complete account of the earliest arrangement of the cult or prove every detail later writers attached to it.

The Romans did not have one sacred book that defined a universal creed. Their religious authority rested on inherited custom, public offices, ritual performance, and the memory of institutions. That made their religion flexible, but it also made correct procedure extremely important.

The gods were not simply characters in stories. They were powers to be honored through action.

Imagine a Roman magistrate preparing to lead an army out of the city. His task is military, but before the campaign proceeds, religious procedure matters too. He may make a vow to a god, seek auspices, and ensure that the proper rites have been observed.

If signs are taken incorrectly, a political opponent may challenge the legitimacy of the action. If an accepted prodigy suggests that the gods have been offended, the Senate may order ceremonies intended to restore the relationship between Rome and the divine.

The boundary between religion and government is difficult to locate because, for the Roman state, the two were deeply intertwined.

The pontifices formed one of the most important priestly colleges. They advised on sacred law, helped regulate the calendar, and supervised aspects of public ritual. The pontifex maximus held the senior position. But no single priest performed every ceremony. Flamines served particular gods. Vestal Virgins maintained Vesta's fire and prepared materials used in public rites. Augurs dealt with auspices, signs observed under formal procedures.

The augurs were not simply fortune-tellers. Their work concerned whether public actions had been undertaken with recognized divine approval. A magistrate might seek auspices before an assembly, election, or military undertaking. Religious procedure could become a tool in political conflict, but that did not make it meaningless. It shows that sacred rules operated inside the institutions of the Republic.

The Vestal Virgins reveal the same mixture of sacred duty and public power. Their temple stood in the Roman Forum, near the house where the Vestals lived. The sacred fire was not merely a decorative flame. Its maintenance represented the continuity of Rome itself. Ancient accounts describe severe penalties for violations of the Vestals' vows, although those accounts must be read as literary testimony rather than as a complete record of every case.

Roman time was organized through a public calendar. The Fasti marked festival days and indicated when public business could take place or was restricted. They are valuable evidence, but a festival listed on a calendar does not tell us exactly what every participant did. Public ceremonies varied across time and place, and household practices left a less complete record.

Some festivals were lively and strange by modern standards. The Parilia, held on April 21, was associated with the purification of livestock and rural communities and later with Rome's foundation traditions. The Lupercalia took place in February and was connected in ancient accounts with purification, fertility, and the stories of Romulus and Remus. Saturnalia, celebrated in December, included public festivity, meals, and a temporary relaxation of some social conventions. It did not abolish Roman hierarchy; its license had a defined place within the ordinary order.

Sacrifice was one of the central forms of public worship. A victim was selected according to the god and occasion. Priests or magistrates conducted the rite with attendants. After the animal was killed, its internal organs could be examined, and portions of the victim were dedicated or burned while meat could be distributed among participants. The exact procedure varied. Roman sacrifice was not one uniform ceremony repeated identically everywhere.

A vow worked differently. It was a promise to perform a religious act if a desired outcome occurred. A magistrate might vow a temple or games during a crisis. If the condition was fulfilled, the promised offering became due. Inscriptions often record vows fulfilled after victories or recoveries from danger.

This was a religion of obligations, not a system built around one universal statement of belief. Philosophers could disagree about the nature of the gods while public ritual continued. Cicero's dialogue On the Nature of the Gods presents competing philosophical positions, but it is not a neutral description of what every Roman believed.

The Senate also had authority over extraordinary religious decisions. When reports of prodigies arrived, officials could decide whether the event should be recognized and what rites should follow. A prodigy might be an unusual natural event, a strange birth, or another occurrence interpreted as abnormal. The question was not always how nature worked. The immediate public question was what action should be taken.

In some circumstances, the Senate consulted the Sibylline Books. Roman tradition said that these prophetic texts had been offered to an early king by a Sibyl and were preserved under official supervision. The Senate controlled their consultation. They were not a public scripture available for anyone to read. The original collection was destroyed when the Capitoline temple burned in 83 BCE. A new collection of prophetic materials was later assembled, and the books were eventually destroyed in late antiquity.

Their importance was political as well as religious. The books gave the state a way to present extraordinary rites as responses to an ancient authority, while keeping control over when that authority was invoked.

Calendars, priesthoods, vows, sacrifices, and consultations of prophetic texts all served a shared purpose: maintaining a favorable relationship between Rome and its gods.

But as Rome expanded, the number of gods and religious practices within its world grew too.

In 293 BCE, Rome faced an epidemic. According to Roman tradition, a delegation was sent to the Greek sanctuary at Epidaurus to seek help from the healing god Asclepius, known to the Romans as Aesculapius. The story said that the god came to Rome in the form of a serpent and was received on an island in the Tiber, where a sanctuary was established.

Whether every detail of the story happened exactly as later writers described it, the tradition reveals something important about Roman religion. When the state faced danger, it could respond by seeking help from a god whose power had been recognized elsewhere.

A foreign god was not automatically an enemy. Sometimes the state itself invited a new cult into Rome.

In 204 BCE, during the Second Punic War against Carthage, Rome brought the Great Mother Cybele from Pessinus in Phrygia. The decision followed consultation of the Sibylline Books. A sacred stone representing the goddess was carried to Rome. The state accepted her power, but the cult's foreign ritual specialists remained subject to regulation. Later Roman writers often described the Galli, priests associated with Cybele, as strange or foreign.

This reveals the tension at the heart of Roman religious expansion. Rome could welcome a powerful deity while controlling how that deity was worshipped. Acceptance did not mean unlimited freedom.

The same tension became much more dramatic in the affair of the Bacchanalia.

In 186 BCE, the Senate intervened against rites associated with Bacchus, the Roman name commonly associated with the Greek Dionysus. The god was linked with wine, performance, and forms of group worship. The historian Livy, writing more than a century after the events, described the affair as a crisis involving secret meetings, initiation, misconduct, and political danger.

In Livy's account, an informer named Hispala Faecenia revealed the existence of the rites to Publius Aebutius. The consul Postumius investigated and brought the matter before the Senate. The government then imposed severe restrictions on Bacchic associations. Meetings required authorization, and the number of participants and officials was limited. Livy reports arrests and executions.

Here, unlike in many ancient stories, a surviving inscription provides an important check on the narrative. The Senate's decree, discovered at Tiriolo in southern Italy, confirms that the government imposed strict controls on Bacchic associations. It does not confirm every dramatic detail in Livy's account.

Livy framed the affair as a confrontation between Roman discipline and foreign excess. His presentation reflects the moral and political concerns of his own time as well as his account of earlier events. The decree itself demonstrates that the Senate feared unauthorized associations and gatherings that operated outside its supervision.

But the worship of Bacchus did not disappear. Bacchic imagery and dedications continued across the Roman world. The state had not simply banned a god forever. It had tried to regulate a form of worship it considered dangerous.

The cult of Isis offers another example. Isis was an Egyptian goddess whose worship spread widely through the Hellenistic and Roman worlds. Inscriptions and sanctuaries show that her cult attracted people from different social backgrounds. Roman officials sometimes restricted Egyptian shrines or gatherings, especially when they believed they fell outside accepted civic control. Yet Isis continued to be worshipped in Rome and in provincial cities.

The cult of Mithras, known chiefly from inscriptions, reliefs, and underground sanctuaries called mithraea, became visible especially from the second to fourth centuries CE. Its central image commonly shows Mithras killing a bull, surrounded by symbols whose meanings remain debated. Soldiers, merchants, and officials appear among its dedicators. The surviving evidence is incomplete, and it does not justify treating Mithraism as a single uniform religion practiced identically throughout the empire.

Rome's religious map was cumulative. Older Latin and Roman rites continued alongside Greek myths, imported gods, and local traditions. Merchants, migrants, soldiers, and officials carried practices across the empire. The Senate could invite a deity during a crisis; local communities could maintain their own sanctuaries; emperors could sponsor or restrict particular forms of worship.

Rome did not have one rule that made every foreign cult acceptable or every foreign cult forbidden. The question was how a cult fitted into civic order and who controlled its public expression.

This flexibility helped Roman religion survive as the empire grew. But it also made religion an instrument of political authority. And as emperors became central to the state, their own relationship with the gods became increasingly important.

In 44 BCE, Julius Caesar was assassinated. He had served as pontifex maximus, the chief priest of Rome, and after his death the Senate authorized divine honors for him. In 42 BCE, he was formally recognized as Divus Julius, the deified Julius Caesar. A temple to him was dedicated in the Forum in 29 BCE, near the place associated with his funeral.

The event changed the political meaning of Roman religion. The divine world was no longer only a matter of ancestral gods, local sanctuaries, and traditional rites. The memory of a ruler could itself become an object of public cult.

Augustus, Caesar's adopted heir, presented his rule as the restoration of order after civil war. He repaired temples, restored neglected shrines, and revived priesthoods. In the Res Gestae, his account of his achievements, Augustus claimed to have restored eighty-two temples in Rome during his sixth consulship. The number is part of his own public record, but archaeology confirms the scale of religious construction and restoration associated with his reign.

In 12 BCE, after the death of Lepidus, Augustus became pontifex maximus. The office placed him at the head of the pontifices and gave him a central role in public religious administration. The old priesthoods did not vanish. The Vestals continued their service, augurs retained their responsibilities, and the Sibylline Books remained under official control.

Augustus connected his household with Rome's religious inheritance. The deified Julius gave him a sacred link to his adoptive father. In Rome itself, Augustus was not normally worshipped as a god while alive in the same way as a deified ruler after death. In the provinces, however, communities could establish cults of the living emperor, often alongside Roma, the personification of the Roman state.

The imperial cult did not follow one identical model everywhere. In Greek cities of Asia Minor, ruler worship had precedents in the Hellenistic kingdoms. Local communities petitioned for temples, festivals, and priesthoods. Roman authorities recognized some arrangements and regulated others. Priests of the imperial cult were often prominent citizens whose public service also strengthened the standing of their cities.

The emperor's religious role was therefore not simply a personal claim to divinity. It was part of the way the empire organized public loyalty. Temples, festivals, inscriptions, and ceremonies made Roman authority visible across great distances.

At the same time, ordinary household religion continued. Roman homes might contain lararia, small shrines displaying images of the Lares and the genius of the head of the household. Families made offerings of wine, incense, or food. They might honor protective powers associated with the home, the neighborhood, ancestors, or particular occupations.

A Roman could therefore participate in several religious worlds at once. The household had its protective powers. The city maintained public temples. The province had local gods and traditions. The imperial cult expressed loyalty to Rome and its ruler. These layers did not require every person to hold one unified theology.

The empire's religious strength lay partly in this capacity to accommodate many practices while linking public ceremony to Roman authority.

But in the third century CE, that relationship came under new pressure. The empire faced civil wars, frontier invasions, rapid changes of ruler, and financial strain. Emperors needed signs that the gods and the state remained aligned. Public sacrifice could become a test of whether people were willing to participate in the religious order that officials believed protected the empire.

Soon, that test would place Christians in danger.

In 250 CE, Emperor Decius issued an order requiring inhabitants of the empire to perform sacrifice before officials. The surviving evidence includes papyri, certificates, literary accounts, and later Christian writings. Some of the certificates, known as libelli, survive with names and statements confirming that individuals had performed the prescribed acts.

Imagine standing before a local commissioner. You are asked to make an offering to the traditional gods, taste the sacrificial food, and receive a document confirming that you have complied. For many inhabitants, this was an ordinary civic act. For Christians who believed that sacrifice to other gods was forbidden, it could become a crisis of conscience.

The surviving certificates show the administrative side of the policy. This was not simply a demand that people announce what they believed in private. It required a visible act, performed before an official, and recorded in writing.

The exact purpose of Decius's order remains debated. It may have been intended to strengthen public unity and demonstrate that the population participated in ceremonies for the safety of the state. The wording was broad rather than directed only at Christians, but Christians were especially likely to refuse because the required sacrifice conflicted with their religious commitments.

Some Christians complied. Some obtained certificates without performing the sacrifice. Others refused and faced punishment. Their communities later struggled with how to receive those who had sacrificed or surrendered sacred texts. The letters of Cyprian of Carthage preserve some of the disputes over repentance and reconciliation.

The episode reveals a recurring Roman assumption: the authorities were often more concerned with public conformity than with private belief. A person could have complex philosophical ideas about the gods, but the state expected recognized civic obligations to be performed. The conflict became dangerous when Christians rejected the act itself.

This was not the first time Christians had faced Roman prosecution, nor was Christianity treated as a criminal identity under one permanent empire-wide law throughout the period. The exchange between Pliny the Younger and Emperor Trajan in the early second century shows a different approach. Pliny asked how Christians should be treated in Bithynia. Trajan replied that they should not be actively sought out, but those accused and proven guilty could be punished if they refused to sacrifice to the gods and the emperor's image.

Local accusations, particular imperial policies, and conflicts with civic authorities all shaped the experience of Christians. The history was uneven. It was not a single, continuous campaign.

A more severe persecution began under Diocletian. In 303 CE, imperial decrees ordered the destruction of Christian meeting places, the seizure of scriptures, the loss of certain legal privileges, and the punishment of clergy who refused to comply. Later measures extended the penalties and required sacrifice in some regions.

The persecution did not unfold identically throughout the empire. Enforcement varied between provinces and rulers. Diocletian and Galerius supported the policy strongly in parts of the empire, while Constantius in the West applied it less severely. The broad sequence of decrees is better documented than every reported execution. Some martyr stories preserve community memory rather than verbatim legal records, so individual details require caution.

The government presented these measures as a defense of the traditional religious order. The emperors believed they were responsible for the welfare of the empire, and traditional sacrifice was understood by many officials as part of that responsibility. Christian communities had their own leaders, texts, meeting places, and networks. To some officials, their refusal to sacrifice appeared to challenge the unity of the state.

But the empire was not divided neatly into two uniform camps. Some Christians complied. Some officials delayed enforcement or avoided the harshest measures. Some non-Christian inhabitants had little enthusiasm for persecution. Local administration mattered, and local practice varied.

In 311 CE, Galerius issued an edict of toleration. The attempt to compel Christians to return to traditional worship had failed. Christians were permitted to exist again, provided that they behaved in an orderly way and prayed for the welfare of the emperor and the state.

The language still assumed that imperial authority could regulate religious life. But the demand for uniform sacrifice had been abandoned.

The edict did not make Christianity dominant overnight. Traditional cults continued, temples remained important, and Roman public life still used inherited religious language. Yet the persecutions had revealed the limits of the old system. The state could demand an action. It could not guarantee that every person would give that action the same meaning.

A new phase began when an emperor decided not merely to tolerate Christianity, but to favor it.

In 312 CE, Constantine defeated Maxentius at the Milvian Bridge outside Rome. Christian writers later connected the victory with a sign from the Christian God. Eusebius described a heavenly sign and divine instruction; Lactantius gave a different account involving a sign placed on soldiers' shields. These are later literary accounts, and they differ in detail. They are not contemporary battlefield reports.

The political result is clear. Constantine emerged as master of the western empire. In 313, Constantine and Licinius agreed on a policy of religious toleration commonly associated with the Edict of Milan. The surviving text is preserved in a letter of Licinius to provincial officials. It ordered the restoration of property taken from Christians and affirmed freedom for inhabitants to follow the religion they preferred.

Constantine's policies increasingly favored Christianity. Christian communities received imperial money, buildings, legal support, and privileges for clergy. Property taken during the persecutions was returned. The emperor also intervened in disputes within Christianity, showing that his concern was administrative as well as personal.

But Constantine did not instantly abolish traditional religion. He retained the title pontifex maximus, an office held by Roman emperors for centuries. Coins, inscriptions, temples, and public offices continued to use traditional religious forms. Constantine restricted some practices, especially certain kinds of divination and private sacrifice associated with magic or political conspiracy, but he did not close every temple or prohibit every sacrifice throughout the empire.

This distinction is essential. A law against a specific practice did not necessarily end every related ceremony. Imperial legislation often addressed particular activities, and enforcement varied by region. The Christian emperor's rise changed the balance of power without instantly producing a religiously uniform empire.

Constantine sponsored Christian basilicas, including major churches at Rome. These projects altered the urban landscape, but temples remained important monuments and often continued to function. When Constantine died in 337, the empire had not become entirely Christian.

His sons continued the movement toward Christian privilege. Laws increasingly restricted sacrifice, and some temples were closed or damaged. Yet the surviving evidence shows an uneven pattern. Some sanctuaries were converted into churches, some were damaged, some continued in altered form, and others declined as local finances and civic priorities changed.

The empire was changing, but it had not reached a final settlement.

Then, in 361, a new emperor came to power with a very different religious ambition.

Julian had been raised within the Constantinian dynasty and received a Christian education. As an adult, however, he turned toward the traditional gods. He became emperor in 361 and ruled until his death in 363.

Julian did not simply want to preserve a few old ceremonies. He attempted to restore traditional worship as a central part of imperial life. He returned some temple property, restored privileges to traditional priests, and urged them to practice discipline, charity, and generosity. His letters show that he believed the old religious system needed stronger organization.

In one letter concerning a high priest in Galatia, Julian criticized priests who neglected the poor. He urged them to match the charitable work associated with Christian communities. His criticism is revealing. Julian understood that Christianity's strength did not rest only on doctrine or imperial favor. It also rested on institutions, social networks, and the ways communities cared for their members.

Julian also restricted Christian influence in education. A law of 362 required teachers to possess official qualifications and to share the moral outlook of the classical texts they taught. Julian argued that teachers who rejected the gods should not publicly interpret authors whose works praised those gods. The measure did not abolish Christian schools, but it limited Christian access to the traditional literary curriculum.

His restoration was an imperial project. The emperor remained the center of military and administrative power, and Julian used laws, appointments, correspondence, and public ceremonies to strengthen traditional cults. He was not restoring the Republic. He was attempting to give the old gods a renewed place within a Christianizing empire.

The project depended heavily on his personal rule. Julian died during a campaign against Persia in 363. The circumstances of his death are disputed. A Christian writer, Gregory of Nazianzus, claimed that Julian was killed by a Christian soldier, while other accounts give different explanations. No version can be established with certainty.

His successor, Jovian, restored Christian privileges and reached a settlement with Persia. Julian's death ended the last sustained attempt by a Roman emperor to make traditional cults the central ideological program of the empire.

But it did not end all traditional worship. Temples remained, people continued to honor old gods, and some senators and local communities maintained inherited customs. The struggle was no longer simply about whether the old gods existed. It was about who would control public institutions, imperial patronage, and the meaning of Rome's past.

That struggle came into view in a place that symbolized the city's political memory: the Senate house.

Inside the Roman Senate house stood the Altar of Victory, associated with the goddess Victoria. It represented more than a religious object. For many senators, it belonged to the inherited ceremonies through which Rome had understood its success and identity.

The altar had been removed during the reign of Constantius II, restored under Julian, and removed again by Gratian in 382. Gratian also withdrew certain public privileges and support associated with traditional priesthoods.

In 384, Quintus Aurelius Symmachus, a senator and defender of ancestral customs, sent a petition to the emperor Valentinian II asking that the altar be restored. His appeal, known as the Third Relatio of Symmachus, argued for the authority of tradition, senatorial dignity, and religious plurality. Different peoples, he suggested, approached the divine through different rites. Rome's prosperity had long been associated with its ancestral ceremonies.

Symmachus was not simply asking for a statue to be put back in a room. He was arguing that the rituals of the Senate formed part of the identity of the state. Remove them, and Rome risked severing itself from the customs that had sustained it.

Ambrose, the Christian bishop of Milan, answered the emperor in a letter. He argued that public funds should not support pagan sacrifice and that a Christian emperor should not restore an altar associated with a religion he considered false. He challenged Symmachus's appeal to Rome's past success and pressed the claim that imperial policy should reflect Christian conviction.

The altar was not restored.

The dispute was settled through correspondence and court politics, not by a public vote of the entire population. Yet the exchange reveals how much remained at stake. Traditional religion still had influential defenders. Christian leaders were now prepared to intervene directly in imperial policy. The meaning of Rome's past had become a contested political question.

What should a Christian emperor owe to the gods of his ancestors? Could an empire preserve its traditions while refusing the sacrifices through which those traditions had been maintained? And if Rome had prospered under the old gods, what did their loss mean?

These questions were not answered by one argument. They were answered gradually through laws, patronage, public decisions, and changes in local life.

Theodosius became emperor in the East in 379 and later ruled the whole empire. During his reign, imperial legislation sharply restricted traditional public sacrifice. Laws issued in 391 prohibited public sacrifice and restricted access to temples. Measures in 392 went further by penalizing sacrifice, consultation of entrails, and the offering of incense to images.

The legal texts survive in the Theodosian Code, compiled later. They reveal the intentions of the imperial court, but they do not tell us exactly how every city enforced every law. An imperial prohibition was not the same thing as instant obedience across a vast empire.

Some sanctuaries had already declined as municipal funding weakened. Others were converted, abandoned, stripped for building materials, or maintained as civic monuments. Christian attacks on particular temples occurred in some places, sometimes with the support of bishops or officials. The pattern differed from one region to another.

In 394, Theodosius defeated the western usurper Eugenius at the Battle of the Frigidus. Later Christian accounts gave the victory religious meaning, presenting it as confirmation of divine favor. The political result strengthened Theodosius's authority, but the battle did not itself close every temple or end every traditional practice.

The Vestal Virgins lost their public position during this period. The exact sequence is difficult to reconstruct, but the institution ended in the late fourth century as traditional public religion lost state support. For centuries, the Vestals and the fire of Vesta had symbolized the continuity of Rome. Their disappearance from public life marked a major institutional change.

By the end of Theodosius's reign, traditional sacrifice had lost its legal protection and imperial patronage. Christian bishops had access to the court, and Christian symbols increasingly shaped public authority.

But it would be misleading to imagine every Roman waking one morning to discover that the old gods had vanished. The laws changed what could be done openly and what the state would support. They did not reveal the private beliefs of every family. They did not instantly erase local memories, inherited habits, or the presence of ancient images in the landscape.

The end of public sacrifice was not the end of every old belief.

The phrase "the end of paganism" can make a long transformation sound like a single final battle. The historical record is more complicated.

Some temples were closed by law or local action. Some were damaged or stripped of valuable materials. Some were converted into churches. Others were abandoned as public funding and patterns of urban life changed. A building could survive long after its original rites had ceased. The Pantheon in Rome, for example, was consecrated as a Christian church in the early seventh century.

The fate of a building does not reveal the fate of every belief. A temple converted into a church shows that sacred architecture received a new use. It does not tell us whether every local resident had abandoned older ideas before the conversion. Laws, inscriptions, sermons, archaeology, and literary sources must be read together.

Christian writers rejected sacrifice to the old gods, but they lived and worked within cities, calendars, legal institutions, and civic traditions shaped by Rome. Christian public life developed in that environment. Some sacred places and festivals acquired new meanings, though direct lines of descent are difficult to establish in individual cases. Christianity was not simply Roman religion under another name, but neither did it emerge outside the institutions and culture of the empire.

Historians disagree about the scale and timing of the transition. Alan Cameron challenges the picture of a large, coherent pagan party defeated in one final struggle. He argues that the evidence for an organized pagan aristocracy in late fourth-century Rome has often been overstated. Other scholars place greater emphasis on the cumulative effects of imperial favor, law, patronage, and Christian institutions. Ramsay MacMullen emphasizes conversion as a social process, while Robin Lane Fox highlights the continuing strength and variety of traditional worship during the early fourth century.

These interpretations are not entirely incompatible. They differ over scale, timing, and the meaning of the surviving evidence. The record shows both the growing power of Christian institutions and the persistence of older practices in different settings.

Mary Beard, John North, and Simon Price have emphasized that Roman religion was not a fixed list of gods or a single creed. It was a changing set of practices embedded in civic life, families, local communities, priestly offices, and imperial authority. Jörg Rüpke's work likewise emphasizes ritual, office, communication, and the changing organization of religious action.

This approach helps explain why the transition lasted so long. Jupiter, Juno, Minerva, Vesta, Isis, Cybele, Bacchus, Mithras, and countless local gods did not disappear at one moment. Their images, sanctuaries, and rituals occupied different social settings. Imperial support strengthened the old system under Augustus, shifted toward Christianity under Constantine and his successors, and then turned against public sacrifice under Theodosius.

The historical sequence is clear enough to establish the major turning points. Julius Caesar received official deification. Augustus restored temples and became pontifex maximus. Decius ordered public sacrifice in 250. Diocletian began a major persecution in 303. Galerius issued toleration in 311. Constantine favored Christianity after 312. Julian attempted a restoration of traditional cults from 361 to 363. Gratian removed the Altar of Victory in 382. Symmachus and Ambrose argued over its return in 384. Theodosius prohibited public sacrifice in 391 and 392.

What the record cannot establish is the private belief of every Roman, the exact level of compliance with every law, or a single date when all traditional worship ended.

The old gods lost the support of the imperial state. Public sacrifice was prohibited. Priesthoods lost privileges and funding. Temples changed their function or fell out of use. Christian institutions gained authority. But the process unfolded over generations, and local life changed at different speeds.

Return, for a moment, to the imagined farmer from the beginning.

We do not know whether his child recovered, whether the rain came, or whether he ever surrendered the silver ornament he inherited from his father. He was created to help us enter a world that the evidence can only partly reveal.

But we do know that Romans made offerings at household shrines. We know they prayed for family protection, made vows in moments of danger, and understood ritual as part of their relationship with divine powers. We know that cities and emperors performed public ceremonies to protect the state. And we know that, over time, the legal and political foundations of those ceremonies changed.

A law could forbid a sacrifice. An emperor could withdraw support from a priesthood. A temple could become a church. An old image could lose its public authority.

But no decree could tell us exactly what every person believed in the privacy of a home.

The end of Roman paganism was not a single moment when a civilization stopped believing in its gods. It was a long transfer of public legitimacy and imperial power, accompanied by changes in law, architecture, institutions, and daily life. The old religious world lost its official place at the center of the empire, while traces of it survived in buildings, literature, art, memory, and local practice.

Rome's gods had once stood at the heart of the state's understanding of itself. They were honored in the Senate, invoked by magistrates, carried into military vows, and remembered at household shrines. For centuries, their worship helped organize the relationship between families, communities, and the empire.

Then the empire changed.

The temples remained as stones. The names remained in poems. The ceremonies faded at different times in different places. And the question of what Rome had lost, or what it had become, continued to shape how later generations remembered the ancient world.

The gods did not vanish from history.

But the empire that had once sacrificed to them no longer belonged to them.
