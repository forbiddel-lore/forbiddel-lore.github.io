The Afterlife in the Bible: From Sheol to Heaven and Hell

How the Bible's picture of death changed, from silent Sheol to resurrection, Gehenna and the hell of later Christianity.

Full script

Psalm one hundred fifteen, verse seventeen says, "The dead praise not the LORD, neither any that go down into silence." Ecclesiastes chapter nine, verse five says, "For the living know that they shall die: but the dead know not any thing." Ecclesiastes chapter nine, verse ten adds, "for there is no work, nor device, nor knowledge, nor wisdom, in the grave, whither thou goest."

The earliest Hebrew view of death is very different from what most readers expect. In the oldest layers of the Hebrew Bible, the dead do not immediately enter a heaven of reward or a hell of punishment. They go to Sheol, a shadowy realm beneath the earth, where the dead are gathered together and separated from ordinary human life. The later biblical ideas of resurrection, judgement, heaven and hell emerged gradually, in different periods and in different Jewish and Christian writings.

The evidence comes from several kinds of sources. The Hebrew Bible contains poems, stories, laws and visions composed over many centuries. Archaeology reveals graves, tombs, inscriptions and burial customs, but it cannot excavate a soul or verify a realm beyond death. Later Jewish and Christian texts preserve interpretations that developed after the earliest biblical material. The history is therefore a history of changing language about death, justice and restoration.

In the oldest Hebrew texts, death is not usually a passage to a sharply divided moral afterlife. Sheol receives kings and slaves, the righteous and the wicked. It is associated with darkness, silence, dust and the loss of activity. The dead are remembered by the living, but they no longer take part in the worship and work of the community.

That picture did not remain unchanged. During the Second Temple period, especially from the second century before the common era onward, some Jewish texts began to speak of the resurrection of the dead, the separation of the righteous from the wicked, and a final judgement. Christianity inherited these ideas and joined them to claims about Jesus's resurrection. Later Christian writers and artists expanded them into elaborate accounts of heaven, hell and purgatory.

The result is not one biblical doctrine of the afterlife. It is a sequence of overlapping conceptions.

Sheol appears in the Hebrew Bible as the destination of the dead generally. The word is difficult to translate because it does not correspond exactly to a single modern term. English versions have rendered it as "the grave," "the pit," or "hell," depending on context. Those translations can obscure the fact that the Hebrew word names a realm of the dead rather than a place reserved only for the wicked.

The story of Jacob and Joseph gives one of the clearest examples. Joseph's brothers have sold him into slavery, but they tell Jacob that a wild animal has killed him. Jacob tears his clothes, puts on sackcloth, and refuses consolation. He says, "For I will go down into the grave unto my son mourning." The Hebrew word translated "grave" is Sheol. Jacob does not say that Joseph has been punished, or that Jacob expects to meet him in a place of reward. He expects to descend to the realm of the dead in grief.

The story later turns when Joseph is revealed to be alive in Egypt. Jacob learns that the son is not dead and is restored to him. The narrative uses Sheol as the natural destination that Jacob expects after his own death. The story does not describe the condition of Joseph's supposed spirit. Its concern is the grief of the living and the unexpected recovery of a lost son.

Several psalms describe Sheol in similar terms. Psalm six asks, "For in death there is no remembrance of thee: in the grave who shall give thee thanks?" The psalmist is not making a systematic map of the universe. The words belong to a prayer for rescue from illness or danger. Yet the assumption is clear. In death, the worshipper can no longer praise God in the public life of Israel.

Psalm eighty eight goes further into darkness. The speaker is treated as one counted among those who go down to the pit. The dead are described as forgotten, cut off from the hand of God, and unable to hear praise. The psalm ends without rescue. Its language is poetic, but it records a powerful early view: death is an existence outside the ordinary relationship between God, worship and the living community.

Job fourteen presents death as a common human destiny. A human being is born like a flower and cut down. The person lies down and does not rise until the heavens are no more. Job asks whether a human being can live again after death. In the immediate context, the question expresses longing rather than confidence. Job hopes for renewal, but the poem does not establish a settled doctrine of resurrection.

The book of Ecclesiastes also speaks in stark terms. The living know that they will die, but the dead know nothing. Their love, hatred and envy have perished. There is no work, planning, knowledge or wisdom in Sheol. The passage belongs to a book that repeatedly examines the apparent futility of human effort. Its dead are not assigned different post-mortem destinations according to moral merit. Death is the end of participation in the affairs of the world.

These texts use several overlapping images. Sheol is beneath the earth, though not necessarily a place reached by ordinary travel. It is a pit, a land of darkness, a house for the dead and a destination from which return is normally impossible. The dead are sometimes called shades, or rephaim. The term can also refer to a class of ancient dead beings in poetic passages. Its meaning changes with context, but it belongs to the language of the underworld.

The dead are also described as being gathered to their people. At the end of Abraham's life, Genesis says that he dies at a good old age and is gathered to his people. The same formula appears for Isaac, Moses and Aaron. Burial is mentioned separately in some of these passages. Being gathered to one's people therefore seems to express more than the physical act of being placed in a family tomb, although it does not provide a detailed geography of the afterlife.

Burial mattered because the body remained connected to family, land and ancestry. Archaeological evidence from ancient Israel includes rock-cut tombs, burial caves, benches for primary burial and later deposits of bones in ossuaries. The exact customs changed across time and region. In many Iron Age tombs, bodies were placed in family contexts. Later, after the flesh had decomposed, bones could be gathered and arranged with the remains of earlier relatives. Such practices do not prove a specific doctrine of Sheol, but they show that death was handled through continuing relations between the dead, the household and the ancestors.

The Hebrew Bible sometimes treats ancestral presence with suspicion. The dead can be remembered, but they are not to become an alternative source of authority. Leviticus says, "Regard not them that have familiar spirits, neither seek after wizards, to be defiled by them." Deuteronomy forbids a range of practices associated with divination and consultation of the dead. Those laws belong to a world in which communication with the dead was considered a real danger or a real possibility, not merely an absurdity. The prohibition itself does not tell how such consultation worked. It tells how the writers wanted Israel to relate to it.

Sheol is not always presented as a place completely beyond God's reach. Several poetic passages say that God is present even there. Psalm one hundred thirty nine asks where a person can go from the divine spirit and lists Sheol among places that cannot escape divine awareness. Yet divine awareness is not the same as ordinary life, worship or reward. The early texts can affirm God's power over Sheol while still describing the dead as silent and inactive.

The distinctions familiar from later Christianity are therefore absent or weak in this earliest layer. Sheol is not simply hell. It is not the Christian heaven. It is a shared destination of the dead, with little or no developed moral separation.

The story of Saul and the medium at Endor occupies a difficult place in this tradition. It is told in First Samuel chapter twenty-eight. Saul is king of Israel, and the Philistines gather for war at Shunem. Saul sees the Philistine army and becomes afraid. He asks the LORD for guidance, but no answer comes through dreams, the Urim or the prophets.

Earlier in the story, Saul has removed from the land those who have familiar spirits and those who are wizards. Now, in his fear, he tells his servants to find a woman with a familiar spirit so that he can consult her. His servants identify a woman at Endor. Saul disguises himself, puts on other clothing, and goes to her by night with two men.

The woman is cautious. She says that Saul has caused the people with familiar spirits and wizards to be cut off from the land. She asks why he is setting a trap that could bring about her death. Saul swears by the LORD that no punishment will come to her. The oath is one of the story's ironies. The king who enforced the prohibition now promises safety to someone whom the law condemns.

Saul asks the woman to bring up Samuel. Samuel was the prophet who had rejected Saul earlier in the narrative and had anointed David. The woman sees a figure and tells Saul that an old man is coming up and that he is covered with a mantle. Saul understands that the figure is Samuel. He bows with his face to the ground.

Samuel asks why Saul has disturbed him by bringing him up. Saul answers that he is in great distress. The Philistines are fighting against him, and God has departed from him and will not answer him. Saul asks Samuel to tell him what to do.

Samuel replies that the LORD has become Saul's enemy. He says that the LORD has done what Samuel had announced earlier: the kingdom has been taken from Saul and given to David. Samuel then states that Israel and Saul will be delivered into the hands of the Philistines on the following day, and that Saul and his sons will be with him.

The woman is no longer central to the exchange. Saul collapses full length on the ground, terrified by Samuel's words and weakened because he has eaten nothing all day. The woman approaches him and urges him to eat. At first he refuses. His servants and the woman press him, and he rises from the ground. The woman has a fatted calf in the house. She kills it, takes flour, kneads it and bakes unleavened bread. Saul and his servants eat and then leave during the night.

The next chapter reports the battle. Saul's sons Jonathan, Abinadab and Melchishua are killed. Saul is badly wounded by archers. He asks his armour-bearer to kill him, but the man refuses. Saul then falls on his own sword. The Philistines find his body and the bodies of his sons, and the people of Jabesh-gilead later recover and bury them.

The story does not offer a general theory of the dead. Samuel appears as a deceased prophet who can speak and deliver a true message, but the encounter is framed as a violation of the religious order. Leviticus nineteen commands Israel not to turn to those with familiar spirits. Deuteronomy eighteen lists those who practise divination, observe times, use enchantments, consult familiar spirits, act as wizards or seek the dead among practices rejected by Israel's law.

The narrative creates a tension between the ban and the apparent success of the consultation. Saul does receive Samuel's message, and the prediction of defeat is fulfilled. Many interpreters have therefore asked whether the story presents a genuine appearance of Samuel, a deceptive spirit, or a literary scene in which the prophet's authority is extended beyond death. The Hebrew wording calls the figure Samuel within the narrative, but it gives no independent explanation of the mechanism. The text's emphasis falls on Saul's disobedience and his isolation from God.

The episode also shows that Sheol was not imagined simply as nonexistence. Samuel is dead, yet he is portrayed as a recognizable person with memory and speech. At the same time, this is an exceptional narrative, not a description of ordinary post-mortem life. Samuel does not give a tour of Sheol. He appears, delivers judgement and returns to the realm from which he has been called.

The law and the story stand together without being harmonized into a single system. The law forbids consulting the dead. The story presents a king who does so at the moment of political collapse. The result is not comfort, instruction or reunion. It is a final announcement that the kingdom will be taken from him and that death is near.

Ideas about the underworld existed across the ancient eastern Mediterranean. Israel's Sheol belongs to a wider family of images, but the similarities do not show that one culture simply copied another. Each tradition has its own myths, rituals and moral structure.

In Mesopotamian literature, the underworld is often called the land of no return. The dead enter it and do not come back to ordinary life. It is ruled by a queen, commonly named Ereshkigal, and populated by the dead. The available texts were written and copied over long periods in Sumerian and Akkadian, so the exact form of a story can vary between manuscripts.

The descent of Ishtar, known in a Sumerian version as the descent of Inanna, is one of the major Mesopotamian accounts. Ishtar, the goddess associated with love, sexuality and war, decides to descend to the underworld. She goes to the gate and orders the gatekeeper to open it. The gatekeeper informs Ereshkigal, who allows Ishtar to enter but commands that she pass through the gates according to the law of the underworld.

At the first gate, the gatekeeper removes Ishtar's crown. At the second, another piece of her regalia is taken. At the third, a necklace; at the fourth, ornaments from the breast; at the fifth, a ring; at the sixth, a measuring rod; and at the seventh, the final garment. The text presents her stripped of the symbols of her status as she reaches the presence of Ereshkigal.

Ereshkigal looks at Ishtar with the eye of death. Ishtar is turned into a corpse and hung on a hook. In the upper world, the effects are catastrophic. Sexual activity ceases, and the world loses the goddess's presence.

The god Enki, called Ea in the Akkadian tradition, devises a plan. He creates figures who can enter the underworld and attend to Ereshkigal's distress. They offer sympathy rather than challenge. Ereshkigal rewards them, and they ask for Ishtar's body. They sprinkle it with the plant of life and the water of life. Ishtar rises, but the law of the underworld requires a substitute.

Ishtar leaves accompanied by demons. She must provide another being in her place. Eventually she gives her husband Dumuzi, though later versions complicate the arrangement by allowing him to spend part of the year in the underworld while his sister takes the other part. The story explains a cycle of absence and return associated with fertility, but its basic underworld law is harsh. Entry strips away status, death claims the visitor and release requires an exchange.

The episode of Enkidu in the Epic of Gilgamesh gives a more direct account of what happens to a human companion after death. Gilgamesh and Enkidu have shared adventures, including the killing of Humbaba and the Bull of Heaven. After the gods decide that one of them must die, Enkidu becomes ill and dies.

Before his death, Enkidu dreams of the underworld. He sees a figure whose face resembles a man but whose appearance is like a bird. The figure takes him by the hair and leads him toward the house of darkness, the dwelling from which those who enter do not return. The house is described as a place where the inhabitants sit in darkness and where dust and clay are their food.

In the dream, kings and priests are present among the dead. Those who once occupied high offices are no longer distinguished by their earthly rank. The dead wear feathers like birds, and the underworld is governed by Ereshkigal. Enkidu wakes distressed and tells Gilgamesh what he has seen.

The poem later describes Enkidu's death and Gilgamesh's grief. Gilgamesh refuses to accept the loss. He summons craftsmen to make an image of Enkidu and mourns him publicly. He eventually seeks a way to escape death, but the narrative does not grant him immortality. The underworld dream and the rest of the epic make mortality the central human condition.

The Mesopotamian materials resemble Sheol in their shared underworld and common fate. They differ in the identities of the gods, the myths of entry and return, and the place of ritual. The comparison shows that the Israelites lived in a world where underworld language was widespread. It does not by itself establish a direct line of borrowing.

Egyptian ideas about death took another form. Egyptian tombs, coffins, papyri and funerary inscriptions preserve a large body of material concerning the passage of the dead. The modern name Book of the Dead refers to collections of spells placed with the deceased. Ancient Egyptians called such collections something closer to a book of going forth by day. The spells were not one fixed book. Their order and selection varied from manuscript to manuscript.

Spell one hundred twenty-five contains the famous judgement scene. A deceased person enters a hall associated with the judgement of the dead. The heart is weighed against a feather, usually understood as the feather of Ma'at, the principle of truth, balance and right order. Anubis oversees the scales. Thoth records the result. Osiris presides as ruler of the dead.

The papyrus images show the deceased approaching the judgement hall and addressing a group of divine judges. The person makes a series of negative declarations, denying acts such as murder, theft, false speech, adultery and dishonest dealings. The exact wording varies among manuscripts. A monster called Ammit, with features of a crocodile, lion and hippopotamus, waits near the scales. If the heart fails the test, Ammit consumes it. If the heart is accepted, the deceased is admitted to a blessed existence.

The Egyptian heart was more than a physical organ. It was the seat of memory, character and moral identity. The body, name, shadow and other aspects of personhood also mattered in Egyptian thought. The judgement scene therefore does not resemble the later Christian doctrine of a disembodied soul entering heaven or hell. It is part of a complex system in which the dead person continues through the protection of the body, the spoken spells, the maintenance of the name and the verdict of the gods.

Egyptian sources also contain images of fields, waterways and divine company for those who pass the judgement. The blessed dead can work, travel and receive offerings. The underworld is not simply a silent universal grave. It is a structured realm with dangers, guardians, divine courts and possible reward. Egypt thus provides an ancient example of a developed moral afterlife, but it is not the origin of every later Jewish or Christian idea.

Greek poetry presents yet another picture. In Homer's Iliad and Odyssey, Hades is both the god of the dead and the realm under his authority. The dead are shades, often called the souls or images of the deceased. They retain identity and can sometimes speak, but their condition is diminished compared with embodied life.

In the Odyssey, Odysseus travels to the edge of the world and performs a ritual that allows the dead to approach. The text describes a pit and offerings of milk, honey, wine, water and meal, followed by the blood of animals. The narrative gives the ritual in literary form. The blood enables the dead to speak with greater clarity. Odysseus first prevents the shades from drinking until he has questioned the prophet Tiresias.

Tiresias appears and explains why Odysseus has come. After drinking, he gives a prophecy about the journey home and the dangers ahead. Odysseus then speaks with his mother, Anticleia, who died while he was away. He tries to embrace her three times, but each time she slips through his hands like a shadow. She explains that the dead have no flesh or bones in the way the living do. They are held in Hades and cannot return to ordinary life.

Other dead heroes appear. Agamemnon recounts his murder after returning from Troy. Achilles speaks about the condition of the dead. Odysseus praises him as blessed in life, but Achilles replies that he would rather be a poor labourer serving another man than rule over all the dead. The passage gives a striking expression of the diminished state of Homeric souls.

Yet Greek myth also contains differentiated regions. In the Odyssey, Menelaus is told that he will not die in Argos but will be carried to the Elysian plain at the edge of the earth, where life is easy. In later Greek and Roman traditions, the blessed may inhabit Elysium while notorious offenders suffer in Tartarus. These distinctions do not form a single uniform doctrine. Homeric poetry, mystery cults, philosophical schools and later mythographers use the underworld differently.

The Greek material is relevant because Jewish writers of the Hellenistic and Roman periods lived in societies where Hades, the soul and post-mortem judgement were familiar concepts. Greek vocabulary also influenced translations. In the Greek version of the Hebrew Bible, Sheol is often translated with the word Hades. That translation created a linguistic bridge between Jewish scripture and Greek-speaking readers, even when the underlying ideas were not identical.

The Mesopotamian, Egyptian and Greek sources show that ancient views of death ranged from a common underworld to elaborate moral judgement and differentiated destinies. The Hebrew Bible's early Sheol belongs closer to the first pattern, though it later became part of new systems.

Isaiah fourteen contains one of the Hebrew Bible's most vivid scenes of the underworld. The chapter is introduced as a taunt against the king of Babylon. It begins with the restoration of Jacob and the end of oppression. The nations are described as resting from their labour, and the trees of the forest rejoice because the oppressor has fallen.

Then the scene moves beneath the earth. Sheol is stirred to meet the king at his coming. The dead shades rise from their thrones. The kings of the nations speak to the fallen ruler and ask whether he too has become weak like them. They remind him that his pomp has been brought down to the grave, with the sound of musical instruments beneath him and worms spread under him.

The king's ambition is recalled in a series of poetic claims. He had said in his heart that he would ascend into heaven, exalt his throne above the stars of God and sit upon the mount of assembly. He had imagined making himself like the Most High. Instead, he is brought down to the sides of the pit.

The King James Version renders one line, "How art thou fallen from heaven, O Lucifer, son of the morning!" In the Hebrew text, the expression refers to a shining one, a morning star or dawn figure. It is part of the taunt against the king of Babylon, not a narrative about a cosmic rebel named Lucifer.

The Latin translation of the Bible used the word lucifer, meaning "light bringer" or "morning star," for this expression. In later Christian interpretation, the Latin word became a personal name for Satan before his fall. That development joined Isaiah fourteen to other passages, especially the New Testament saying about Satan falling like lightning from heaven. The identification became standard in much Christian tradition, but it is not the original subject of Isaiah fourteen.

The chapter's underworld scene is important because the dead shades recognize the political ruler and mock his collapse. Sheol is not only a silent pit in this poem. It is a literary assembly of dead kings. Their words dramatize the reversal of status: the man who seemed to dominate the nations is now equal to the rulers he conquered.

Scholars have long debated whether the passage preserves older mythic language about a divine or astral being. The immediate context identifies the target as the king of Babylon, and the poem's political purpose is clear. The language of ascent and descent could draw on wider ancient imagery, but the text does not require a biography of Satan. The later meaning of Lucifer belongs to interpretation after the Hebrew poem had been translated and reread.

The major turn in Jewish ideas about death appears in texts from the Second Temple period. The Hebrew Bible had contained occasional language that could be read as hope for restoration, but the clearest statements of resurrection and differentiated destiny come from a later age.

Isaiah twenty-six is a national poem of hope. It speaks of the dead and the inhabitants of the dust. The earth is said to give birth to the dead. The King James Version reads, "Thy dead men shall live, together with my dead body shall they arise. Awake and sing, ye that dwell in dust." The passage continues, "for thy dew is as the dew of herbs, and the earth shall cast out the dead."

The imagery is compressed and difficult. Some scholars read it as a promise of national restoration expressed through the language of resurrection. Others see a more direct hope that individual dead people will rise. The chapter does not describe a final court, heaven or hell. It does show a new possibility: death may not have the last word over the people of God.

Daniel twelve gives a more explicit statement. During a time of crisis, the angelic figure tells Daniel that many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake. Some will rise to everlasting life, and some to shame and everlasting contempt. Those who are wise will shine like the brightness of the firmament, and those who turn many to righteousness like the stars forever.

This is the first clear biblical passage to join resurrection with a divided outcome. The dead do not merely return. They rise to different destinies. The passage is usually dated to the second century before the common era, in the period of persecution under Antiochus the Fourth Epiphanes. The book presents its revelations as belonging to Daniel in the Babylonian period, but most historians understand the final composition to reflect the crisis of the years around one hundred sixty four before the common era.

Antiochus ruled the Seleucid kingdom, which included Judea for part of this period. The books of Maccabees describe pressure on Jewish practices, conflict over the Temple and resistance led by the Maccabean family. The details of policy, chronology and local response remain debated, but the crisis created a severe problem of justice. People who remained faithful could be tortured and killed, while rulers and collaborators could appear to prosper. Resurrection offered a way for divine justice to extend beyond death.

Second Maccabees chapter seven tells the story of seven brothers and their mother. The king arrests them and tries to force them to eat pork, an act forbidden by Jewish law. The brothers refuse. The narrative proceeds through a sequence of deaths.

The first brother speaks for all of them. He tells the king that they are ready to die rather than break the laws of their fathers. He is tortured and dies.

The second brother is brought forward. Before he dies, he tells the king that the wicked ruler may take the brothers from the present life, but the King of the universe will raise them to eternal life. The text describes his torture and death.

The third brother is next. When commanded, he extends his hands and says that he received them from heaven and hopes to receive them again because of obedience to the laws. The narrator says that the king and those with him are astonished at the young man's courage.

The fourth brother speaks of choosing death at human hands because of the hope given by God that the dead will be raised again. For the king, he says, there will be no resurrection to life.

The fifth brother is brought forward. He rebukes the king for exercising authority over mortals as if it were divine. He says that the Jewish nation is being disciplined for its sins, but that the king should not think he will escape punishment after acting against God.

The sixth brother also speaks before death. He acknowledges that the suffering has come because the nation has sinned, but he warns the king not to imagine that he will go unpunished for fighting against God.

The seventh brother is the last. The king tries persuasion and promises rewards, but the boy refuses. The mother urges him to accept death and tells him that the creator formed humanity. She expresses hope that, after the brothers have died for the law, God will restore them to life.

The youngest brother then addresses the king. He rejects the ruler's offer and says that the brothers are suffering because of their devotion to God. He warns the king that divine wrath will reach him and his descendants. He says that the brothers have died under God's covenant and will receive everlasting life, while the king will face judgement. The mother dies after her sons.

The narrative does not present resurrection as an abstract doctrine detached from history. It is a claim made by people under torture. The brothers do not expect to avoid death. They expect God to reverse the verdict imposed by the persecuting state. Their bodies, which are mutilated and killed, will be restored. Resurrection here is bodily and political as well as spiritual.

The Wisdom of Solomon presents another development. The book was written in Greek, probably in the Hellenistic period, and is often associated with Jewish intellectual life in Alexandria. It begins by urging rulers to love justice and seek God. The ungodly reason that life is short and that death ends all things. They decide to oppress the poor and test the righteous.

The author rejects that calculation. The souls of the righteous are in the hand of God, and no torment will touch them. In the sight of the foolish they seem to have died, but their departure is understood as peace. The righteous are disciplined or tested, and their hope is full of immortality. They will shine and judge nations, while the wicked will face consequences.

Wisdom of Solomon uses language that sounds closer to Greek philosophical descriptions of the soul than the older Hebrew poems do. Yet it remains a Jewish work shaped by Israel's God, covenantal justice and wisdom tradition. Its immortality is not simply an escape from the body. The book also looks toward divine judgement and restoration. The mixture is characteristic of the period.

First Enoch contains several visions of judgement, heavenly books, resurrection and differentiated destinations. It is not one book written at one time. It is a collection of works composed and edited over several centuries. The sections commonly called the Book of the Watchers and the Similitudes of Enoch use journeys through cosmic realms and scenes of divine judgement.

In the Book of the Watchers, Enoch is taken through places where the dead are separated. One area contains the spirits of the dead awaiting the day of judgement. The text describes divisions between the righteous and those who have sinned, with different conditions before the final verdict. The exact arrangement varies in translation and interpretation, but the central idea is separation before the final consummation.

The Similitudes of Enoch speaks of a chosen one or Son of Man who sits on a throne of glory. Kings and mighty rulers who have oppressed the righteous are confronted. The righteous receive a future in which they live in light and blessing, while the wicked face judgement. First Enoch also includes the resurrection or transformation of the righteous in several passages, though its terminology is not identical to Daniel or the New Testament.

These texts show that Jewish thought had become diverse. Some writers emphasized resurrection of the body. Others spoke of the righteous soul's security after death. Others described an intermediate division of the dead before a final judgement. There was no single authorized map shared by every Jewish group.

Why did this change occur? Historians usually discuss several possible causes, and the evidence does not justify reducing the development to one source.

One proposal points to Persian or Zoroastrian influence. Persian religion, especially in later texts, includes ideas of judgement, resurrection, the renovation of the world and the defeat of evil. The Persian Empire ruled Judah for more than two centuries after the conquest of Babylon by Cyrus in the sixth century before the common era. Contact between Jewish communities and Persian imperial culture was therefore extensive.

The difficulty is dating. Many Zoroastrian texts were written down much later than the periods in which their ideas may have circulated orally. The Gathas are commonly associated with Zarathustra, but their date remains disputed. The Avesta was transmitted and compiled over a long period. Similarity between Jewish and Persian ideas does not prove the direction of influence, and shared ancient themes can develop independently. Persian influence remains possible, but the evidence cannot establish a simple borrowing story.

A second proposal emphasizes Greek ideas about the soul. From the Hellenistic period onward, Jewish writers used Greek language and philosophical categories. The Wisdom of Solomon speaks of the souls of the righteous and of immortality. Philo of Alexandria, a Jewish philosopher of the first century of the common era, interpreted scripture through Platonic concepts and treated the soul's relation to the body in ways that differ from earlier Hebrew poetry.

Yet resurrection is not a natural result of Greek philosophy in general. Plato's works often treat the body as a burden and the soul's liberation as desirable. Jewish resurrection texts usually insist that the body is restored by God. The Maccabean brothers do not hope to become bodiless minds. They expect the creator to return what has been destroyed. Greek language and philosophy supplied concepts, but Jewish writers adapted them to their own account of creation and justice.

A third explanation locates the change inside Jewish history. The problem of righteous people dying unjustly is already visible in Job and the psalms. It becomes acute when martyrs are killed for refusing royal commands. If God is just, and if the righteous can be murdered without receiving justice in this life, a future resurrection provides a solution. Daniel and Second Maccabees make that connection explicit.

Jon Levenson, in Resurrection and the Restoration of Israel, argues that biblical and Jewish hopes for resurrection should be understood in relation to God's restoration of Israel, creation and the defeat of death, rather than as a simple adoption of Greek ideas about immortal souls. His approach gives weight to national and bodily restoration. Resurrection is not merely an escape for isolated individuals. It is part of a larger renewal.

Alan Segal, in Life After Death, traces the diversity of Jewish and Christian ideas across the Second Temple and early Christian periods. He emphasizes that resurrection, immortality and the fate of the soul are related but not identical concepts. Different texts can preserve different combinations of these ideas.

Philip Johnston, in Shades of Sheol, examines the Hebrew Bible's language of death and the underworld. He argues that Sheol is not a fully developed place of punishment in the early biblical material. Later ideas of judgement and differentiated destiny should therefore be distinguished from the older language rather than projected backward into it.

The historical development can be described without choosing one cause. Persian contact may have contributed vocabulary or conceptual possibilities. Greek culture changed the intellectual environment and supplied a language for discussing the soul. Internal Jewish debates about covenant, martyrdom, creation and divine justice supplied powerful reasons for reworking the older picture. The evidence supports interaction rather than a single origin.

The first century of the common era preserves several Jewish positions. Josephus, the Jewish historian writing in Greek after the destruction of the Jerusalem Temple, describes differences among Pharisees, Sadducees and Essenes.

Josephus says that the Pharisees believe souls have power to survive death. They also hold that good souls pass into another body, while the wicked suffer eternal punishment. His summary is shaped by Greek terms and by his effort to explain Jewish groups to a Roman audience. It should not be treated as a complete transcript of Pharisaic teaching, but it confirms that resurrection and post-mortem distinction were associated with the Pharisees.

Josephus says that the Sadducees deny the continuation of the soul and reject punishments and rewards after death. He also says that they accept only what is written and do not follow the traditions attributed to the Pharisees. The New Testament gives a similar contrast. Acts twenty-three reports that the Sadducees say there is no resurrection, neither angel nor spirit, while the Pharisees acknowledge both.

The Essenes, in Josephus's account, are described as believing that souls are immortal and that the body is temporary. Josephus compares their expectation of the soul's freedom to Greek ideas, though the comparison may reflect his presentation rather than Essene terminology. The Dead Sea Scrolls, associated with communities connected to the Judean desert, contain hymns, rules, biblical interpretations and apocalyptic writings. They show intense concern with purity, angels, final judgement and the division between the righteous and the wicked.

The scrolls do not provide one simple Essene doctrine of heaven and hell. Some texts speak of the resurrection or restoration of the righteous, while others emphasize the present community's participation in an eternal covenant. The evidence is substantial but fragmentary. It confirms that Jewish groups near the time of Jesus differed sharply over the fate of the dead.

The New Testament continues this diversity while giving it a new center. Its central claim is that Jesus was raised from the dead. Different books connect that claim to the future resurrection of others, to judgement and to the destiny of the righteous.

Luke sixteen contains the parable commonly called the rich man and Lazarus. A rich man is dressed in purple and fine linen and feasts every day. A poor man named Lazarus lies at the rich man's gate, covered with sores. He longs to be fed with the crumbs that fall from the rich man's table. Dogs come and lick his sores.

Lazarus dies and is carried by angels to Abraham's bosom. The rich man also dies and is buried. In Hades, being in torment, he lifts his eyes and sees Abraham far away with Lazarus at his side. He calls to Abraham and asks him to send Lazarus so that Lazarus may dip the tip of his finger in water and cool the rich man's tongue, because he is tormented in flame.

Abraham answers that the rich man received good things during his life while Lazarus received bad things. Now Lazarus is comforted and the rich man is tormented. Abraham adds that a great gulf has been fixed between them, so that those who would cross from one side to the other cannot.

The rich man then asks Abraham to send Lazarus to his father's house, where he has five brothers. He wants them warned so that they do not come to the place of torment. Abraham replies that the brothers have Moses and the prophets and should hear them. The rich man says that they will repent if someone goes to them from the dead. Abraham answers that if they do not hear Moses and the prophets, they will not be persuaded even if someone rises from the dead.

The story uses Hades, not Gehenna, and it presents a conscious post-mortem separation before the final resurrection described elsewhere in the New Testament. The rich man and Lazarus are not said to have received new bodies. The scene also reverses social position. The rich man's wealth does not protect him, and the poor man's suffering is answered by comfort.

Interpretation varies. Some scholars treat the passage as a parable about wealth and the authority of scripture rather than as a geographical description of the afterlife. Others note that it draws on wider Jewish and Greek stories about reversals after death. The text itself gives the characters speech, memory and awareness, but it does not explain how this intermediate state relates to the final resurrection.

Luke twenty-three tells of Jesus's crucifixion. One criminal being executed beside him rebukes the other criminal, who has joined in mocking Jesus. The second criminal says that the two men are receiving a just punishment, while Jesus has done nothing wrong. He then says, "Lord, remember me when thou comest into thy kingdom."

Jesus answers, "Verily I say unto thee, To day shalt thou be with me in paradise." The quotation is from the King James Version, Luke chapter twenty-three, verse forty-three.

The meaning depends partly on punctuation. The earliest Greek manuscripts did not contain the modern system of commas. The traditional reading places "today" with the promise: the man will be with Jesus in paradise that day. Some modern interpreters connect "today" with the verb of speaking, producing a different emphasis. The Greek word translated paradise can mean a garden or a place of blessedness. In the Greek version of the Hebrew Bible, it is used for a garden, and in later Jewish and Christian writing it can denote a blessed destination of the righteous.

The passage therefore contributes to the emerging language of immediate blessedness after death, but it does not describe a complete heaven. Its context is a promise made at execution, and its imagery is concise.

Paul's most sustained discussion appears in First Corinthians fifteen. Some members of the Corinthian community have asked how the dead are raised and with what kind of body they come. Paul answers by appealing to the resurrection of Christ and by using an analogy from agriculture. A seed is sown in the ground and later rises as a plant. What is sown is not the same in appearance as what grows, but there is continuity between them.

Paul contrasts the body that is sown with the body that is raised. He speaks of corruption and incorruption, dishonour and glory, weakness and power, a natural body and a spiritual body. The spiritual body does not mean a body made of an immaterial substance. It is a body transformed and animated by God's spirit.

Paul then contrasts Adam, the living soul, with Christ, the life-giving spirit. He says that the natural comes first and the spiritual afterward. He states that flesh and blood cannot inherit the kingdom of God in their present corruptible condition. At the last trumpet, the dead will be raised incorruptible, and the living will be changed. Death will be swallowed up in victory.

Paul's account is emphatically resurrection-based. He does not describe the immortal soul leaving the body as the final human destiny. The body is transformed, not discarded. At the same time, other passages attributed to Paul speak of being with Christ after death. In Philippians, Paul says that departing and being with Christ is far better. In Second Corinthians, he speaks of being away from the body and at home with the Lord. Scholars debate how these statements fit together. They may reflect an intermediate state before resurrection, or different rhetorical ways of expressing hope.

The New Testament also uses the word Gehenna. The term comes from the Valley of Hinnom, a place associated with Jerusalem. Jeremiah seven condemns the people for building a place called Topheth in the valley of the son of Hinnom, where children were burned in fire. The King James Version says that the people burned their sons and daughters in the fire, "which I commanded them not, neither came it into my heart."

Second Kings twenty-three describes King Josiah's reforms. Josiah defiles Topheth, which is in the valley of the children of Hinnom, so that no one can make a son or daughter pass through the fire to Molech. These passages connect the valley with condemned practices and with divine judgement.

In the New Testament, Gehenna becomes an image for final destruction or punishment. Jesus warns that it is better to lose a hand, foot or eye than for the whole body to be cast into Gehenna. The imagery is severe, but the texts do not present a single detailed map of its geography. Gehenna is not identical in origin to Sheol or Hades.

A popular claim says that Gehenna was a burning rubbish dump outside Jerusalem, and that later Christian hell imagery developed from this municipal fire. Ancient sources do not support that account. The association of Gehenna with a continuously burning city dump is generally traced to medieval commentary rather than to evidence from the biblical or early Jewish periods. The biblical texts instead connect the valley with Topheth, child sacrifice and judgement.

Revelation twenty presents a final judgement scene. An angel seizes the dragon, identified as the ancient serpent and the Devil, binds him and confines him for a thousand years. After the thousand years, the dragon is released briefly and then defeated. Fire comes down from heaven and consumes those who gather against the holy city. The Devil is cast into the lake of fire and brimstone, where the beast and false prophet are already located, and they are tormented day and night forever and ever.

Then John sees a great white throne. Earth and heaven flee from the presence of the one seated on it. The dead, small and great, stand before the throne. Books are opened, and another book, the book of life, is opened. The dead are judged according to their works as recorded in the books. The sea gives up the dead in it, and Death and Hades give up the dead in them. Death and Hades are cast into the lake of fire. This is called the second death. Anyone not found written in the book of life is cast into the lake of fire.

The passage combines resurrection, judgement and the abolition of Hades. Hades is not the final hell in this scene. It gives up the dead and is itself destroyed. The lake of fire is a later destination within the vision. Revelation uses symbols drawn from biblical prophecy, apocalyptic literature and the political experience of Roman imperial power. Its imagery became one of the main sources for later Christian accounts of final punishment.

The New Testament also contains a tradition about Christ's descent to the dead. First Peter says that Christ was put to death in the flesh but made alive in the spirit, and that he went and preached to the spirits in prison. Later in the letter it says that the gospel was preached also to the dead. Ephesians four speaks of Christ descending into the lower parts of the earth before ascending above all heavens. The meaning of these passages was disputed from the beginning. They can be read as a proclamation to the dead, a descent to the underworld, or a reference to Christ's incarnation and exaltation.

The Apostles' Creed later includes the phrase "he descended into hell," though the ancient Greek and Latin forms mean different things. In many Christian traditions, the descent became an account of Christ entering the realm of the dead and breaking its power. The New Testament passages themselves are brief and difficult. They do not offer one agreed narrative of what occurred.

A tension therefore remains between two patterns. One is resurrection of the body at the end of history. The other is the soul's conscious existence after death, whether in paradise, Hades or another intermediate condition. Some Christian writers combined both by placing the soul in an interim state before bodily resurrection. Others emphasized one side more strongly. The later history of heaven and hell grew from attempts to reconcile these strands.

One of the earliest Christian texts to expand the punishments of the dead is the Apocalypse of Peter. It is generally dated to the early second century of the common era, although its exact date and original form are disputed. The work survives in fragments and versions, including Greek fragments and an Ethiopic translation. It presents itself as a revelation given by Jesus to Peter.

The text begins with Jesus teaching the disciples on a mountain. He describes the final coming, the resurrection and the judgement of the world. The disciples then see the righteous and the wicked. The righteous are shown in a place of light and beauty. They have received bodies suited to their condition, and they live in the company of angels and the patriarchs.

The vision then turns to the punishments of the damned. The text gives examples linked to particular sins. Women who have caused children to be killed or have destroyed unborn life are suspended over a place of torment. Men who have been involved in such acts are punished in corresponding ways. Blasphemers hang over fire. Those who have abandoned the way of righteousness are placed in flames. The punishments often mirror the offence through a principle of bodily reversal.

The Apocalypse of Peter describes people who have corrupted marriage, oppressed the poor, lied, betrayed, practised sexual wrongdoing or failed to care for others. Some are hung by the bodily parts associated with their sins. Others stand in mud, fire or darkness. The text is not a neutral catalogue. It is a moral vision in which the body records the truth of a person's conduct.

The righteous are allowed to see the punishments. They respond with praise because divine justice has been displayed. Some churches read the work aloud and others refused to. An early list of accepted books, the Muratorian fragment, notes that some would not have it read in church. The work was influential in the formation of Christian imagination even though it was not included in the New Testament canon.

The Apocalypse of Peter links New Testament themes with older Jewish apocalyptic patterns. It includes resurrection, judgement, differentiated destinations, angels and books of deeds. It also gives the punishments a vivid physical form. Later visions of hell drew on similar ideas, though direct literary lines are difficult to establish in every case. The text's importance lies in the way it shows an early Christian attempt to turn brief scriptural images into a complete tour of the afterlife.

Origen of Alexandria developed one of the most ambitious early Christian accounts of final destiny. He argued that God's purpose was ultimately restorative. Punishment could be corrective rather than endless, and rational beings might eventually return to God after purification. His theory, commonly called apokatastasis, was controversial during his lifetime and after it. Origen did not simply deny judgement. He placed judgement within a long process of healing and restoration.

Augustine of Hippo defended a different position in the late fourth and early fifth centuries. In The City of God, he argued for eternal punishment for the wicked and eternal happiness for the righteous. He rejected interpretations that made the fires of hell temporary. Augustine also treated the resurrection of the body as essential Christian teaching. For him, the soul's post-mortem condition was not the final state. The final judgement would reunite body and soul, after which the destinies of the saved and damned would be everlasting.

The disagreement between Origen and Augustine shows that early Christianity did not begin with one settled theory of hell. Both writers accepted scripture, resurrection and judgement, but they organized the evidence differently. Origen emphasized the goodness and pedagogical purpose of divine punishment. Augustine emphasized the permanence of the final verdict.

The medieval West added another destination: purgatory. The word and the doctrine developed over time rather than appearing fully formed in one biblical passage. Christian writers had long spoken of post-mortem purification, prayers for the dead and temporary punishment. Biblical passages such as First Corinthians three, where a person's work is tested by fire, and Second Maccabees twelve, where prayers and offerings are made for the dead, became important in later arguments.

By the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, Latin theologians increasingly described purgatory as a distinct state or place in which souls undergo purification after death. Gregory the Great helped shape earlier medieval teaching. Scholastic theologians such as Thomas Aquinas gave the doctrine systematic form. The Western church connected it to masses, prayers, almsgiving and indulgences for the dead.

The Eastern Christian traditions developed related ideas about purification and the fate of the dead, but did not adopt every Western definition. The Reformation later rejected or sharply criticized purgatory in many Protestant communities, especially because of its connection with indulgences and ecclesiastical authority. The history is therefore not a straight line from the New Testament to one universal Christian map.

Modern Christian pictures of hell often combine several sources. Sheol and Hades contribute names and underworld imagery. Gehenna contributes fire and judgement. Revelation contributes the lake of fire. Apocalyptic writings contribute angels, books, bodily punishments and tours of the damned. Patristic theology supplies theories of eternal or temporary punishment. Medieval art, preaching and poetry make the scenes visible.

Dante Alighieri's Divine Comedy, completed in the early fourteenth century, became the most influential literary map of hell in European culture. The Inferno is not scripture, though it uses biblical figures, Christian theology, classical mythology and medieval political commentary.

Dante begins his descent guided by Virgil, the Roman poet. They enter the realm of the dead through the gate of Hell. The first region is the vestibule, where the morally indifferent chase a blank banner while insects torment them. They cross the river Acheron with Charon and enter the first circle, Limbo.

Limbo contains virtuous pagans and unbaptized infants. The virtuous pagans do not suffer physical torture, but they lack the vision of God. Dante places famous ancient poets and philosophers there, including Homer, Horace, Ovid and Lucan, as well as Aristotle, Socrates and Plato.

The second circle contains the lustful, blown about by a violent storm. Minos judges the dead and assigns them their place. The third circle contains the gluttonous, lying in vile rain and mud under the guard of Cerberus. The fourth contains the avaricious and the prodigal, who push heavy weights against one another. The fifth contains the wrathful and sullen in the marsh of Styx.

At the city of Dis, the descent enters deeper regions. The sixth circle holds heretics in burning tombs. The seventh contains the violent, divided into groups: those violent against others are immersed in a river of blood; those violent against themselves become trees torn by harpies; those violent against God, nature and art endure a burning desert and falling fire.

The eighth circle is Malebolge, with ten ditches for different forms of fraud. Seducers are driven by demons. Flatterers lie in filth. Simoniacs are fixed headfirst in holes with their feet burning. Diviners walk with their heads turned backward. Barrators are submerged in boiling pitch. Hypocrites wear gilded lead cloaks. Thieves are attacked by serpents and transformed. False counsellors burn in flames. Sowers of discord are mutilated by a demon. Falsifiers suffer disease and corruption.

The ninth circle contains traitors, frozen in the lake of Cocytus. The sinners are divided according to the objects of their betrayal: relatives, political communities, guests and benefactors. At the center, Lucifer is fixed in the ice. He has three faces and beats his wings, which create the freezing wind. In his three mouths he chews Judas, Brutus and Cassius.

Dante's principle is contrapasso, a punishment corresponding to or reversing the sin. The law is poetic and moral rather than a simple biblical list. Dante places classical gods, Roman politicians, medieval opponents and biblical figures within a Christian universe. The result is a richly organized imaginative world, not a transcript of the Bible.

The modern image of hell owes much to Dante's circles, punishments, demons, frozen traitors and three-faced Lucifer. Biblical passages supplied core images, but Dante gave them architecture, ranks and visual order. European paintings, sermons and later literature spread those forms widely.

Scholars can establish several broad stages. In the earliest Hebrew texts, Sheol is a shared realm of the dead, associated with silence, darkness, dust and the end of ordinary activity. Burial practices and ancestral formulas show that the dead remained connected to family memory and land, but the archaeological record cannot determine the nature of Sheol.

During the Second Temple period, Jewish texts increasingly speak of resurrection, judgement and different destinies. Daniel and Second Maccabees connect resurrection with persecution and divine justice. Wisdom of Solomon emphasizes the security of the righteous soul. First Enoch describes separated regions and future judgement. Jewish groups in the first century disagreed over resurrection, the soul and the authority of tradition.

The New Testament preserves several strands at once. Luke describes paradise and an intermediate division between the blessed and the tormented. Paul places the final hope in the transformation of the body. Gehenna draws on the Valley of Hinnom and prophetic condemnation. Revelation distinguishes Hades from the final lake of fire. The New Testament's statements about Christ's descent to the dead remained open to competing interpretations.

The causes of the change remain disputed. Persian contact, Greek language and philosophy, Jewish reflection on martyrdom, and the internal development of biblical ideas all provide plausible parts of the explanation. No surviving source proves that one culture supplied the complete doctrine to another.

Later Christians organized the inherited material in different ways. Origen proposed eventual restoration, Augustine defended eternal punishment, and the medieval West developed purgatory as a state of purification. The Apocalypse of Peter helped shape early Christian visions of bodily punishment. Dante's Inferno transformed scriptural and theological motifs into the most influential literary geography of hell in Europe.

The ideas travelled through Hebrew poetry, Greek translations, Jewish apocalyptic writings, Christian scripture, sermons, theologians, liturgy, manuscripts, paintings and poetry. The history is therefore not a movement from one fixed biblical teaching to another. It is the accumulation and reinterpretation of different accounts of death, justice, resurrection and divine judgement.

The earliest Hebrew texts describe Sheol as the common destination of the dead, while later Jewish and Christian writings develop resurrection, heaven, hell and purgatory in distinct stages.
