Spinoza Reveals Who Invented the Ten Commandments, and It Wasn't God

Baruch Spinoza from Amsterdam's Talmud Torah congregation to the cherem of 1656, his Theological-Political Treatise on Moses, Sinai and the Decalogue, the scandal of a 'book forged in hell', and what biblical scholars after Wellhausen make of his argument.

Full script

In Amsterdam, in the winter of sixteen seventy, a young printer's apprentice worked after the shop had gone quiet.

The room stood somewhere among the narrow streets where presses, warehouses, countinghouses, and dwellings crowded together. The city's printers handled sermons, legal notices, histories, scientific works, and books meant for readers beyond the Dutch Republic. Type was set letter by letter. A page existed first as a pattern of small pieces of metal, reversed in the composing stick, waiting for ink and pressure.

The apprentice had before him a Latin book without an author's name.

Its title page gave a false place of printing. The deception was deliberate. The book had been printed in Amsterdam, but the title page claimed another city. Its author had chosen anonymity, and its publishers had chosen distance on paper. The precautions mattered because the book attacked assumptions held by Jews, Christians, magistrates, and theologians alike.

The apprentice lifted a piece of type and fitted it into the line.

The sentence concerned Moses.

The author argued that Moses had not written all the books later called the books of Moses. The claim was not presented as a passing doubt. It belonged to a larger argument about prophecy, law, miracles, political authority, and the human history of the Bible.

The book was the Theological-Political Treatise. It appeared in sixteen seventy. Its author was Baruch Spinoza, though the title page did not say so.

He had been born Bento de Espinosa in Amsterdam in sixteen thirty two, into a Portuguese Jewish merchant family. By the time the Treatise appeared, he had been cast out from the community in which he had been raised. He lived by grinding lenses and by the support of friendships. He wrote about religion while Europe was still dangerous for writers who treated sacred texts as historical documents.

A printer's apprentice could not know how the book would travel. The first readers would argue over its claims. Some would call it impious. Others would regard it as a defense of freedom to philosophize. Its enemies would give it a name that turned its title into an accusation: a book forged in hell.

The book did not begin with the Ten Commandments. It began with prophecy.

Spinoza asked what prophets possessed when they spoke in the name of God. His answer separated prophetic authority from philosophical understanding. A prophet, in his account, did not receive a clearer grasp of nature's order simply because a revelation had come through the prophet. Prophecy worked through imagination. Its images, voices, and signs were fitted to the prophet's language, temperament, education, and existing opinions.

The claim reached back to the Bible's own scenes.

At Sinai, the Israelites heard a voice and received laws. In Exodus, the people saw thunder and lightning, a thick cloud, and a very loud trumpet. Moses went up the mountain. The Decalogue was presented as the foundation of the covenant between God and Israel.

Spinoza did not treat this scene as a simple record of God dictating a universal code to every human being. He read it through his account of revelation. Divine communication, as he described it, accommodated itself to the people who received it. Israel's law was therefore connected to Israel's political condition, its collective understanding, and its need for a stable common order.

That argument did not mean that Spinoza denied that the text described a voice at Sinai. It meant that the text's language had to be understood within his theory of prophecy. The voice belonged to revelation as experienced and narrated. It did not give the Israelites philosophical knowledge of God's essence.

The distinction was severe.

Spinoza also argued that Moses was not the final author of the Pentateuch in the form known to readers. He examined changes of perspective, references to Moses in the third person, accounts of events after Moses's death, and passages that appeared to belong to a later historical arrangement. In one famous line of reasoning, he drew attention to a remark by Ibn Ezra, a medieval Jewish commentator who had noticed difficulties in the traditional attribution.

Spinoza did not invent the observation that the Pentateuch contained signs of later editing. He placed older Jewish criticism beside Christian and philosophical arguments about Scripture. Thomas Hobbes had made related observations in Leviathan. Richard Simon would later develop a broad account of scribal and editorial activity. The question was not simply whether Moses was a historical figure. It was who had shaped the books that bore his name.

Modern scholarship does not reproduce Spinoza's system in full.

Richard Elliott Friedman and Joel S. Baden, working within modern studies of the Pentateuch, describe a long process of composition from earlier sources, traditions, and editorial work. Their accounts differ from one another and from Spinoza's. Modern historians do not treat the Treatise as a complete solution to authorship. Yet Spinoza recognized problems that later research would examine with manuscripts, language, literary comparison, and historical context.

His argument about the Decalogue also requires care.

The Bible contains more than one presentation of the commandments. Exodus places a version within the Sinai narrative. Deuteronomy presents another version in Moses's later speech to Israel. The differences include the reason given for the Sabbath. Spinoza discussed these books as parts of a historical and political literature rather than as a single document written at one moment by one hand.

The question, then, is not whether Spinoza merely believed or disbelieved.

The question is what he actually argued about prophecy, Moses, Sinai, and the Decalogue; why Amsterdam's Jewish authorities issued a cherem against him in sixteen fifty six; why the Treatise appeared anonymously; and how far modern scholarship agrees with the historical method behind his conclusions.

The record begins with a book, but the book can be followed only through objects, letters, communal documents, biographies, and later editions.

To answer, we must go back to the people and papers that preserve the argument.

The first evidence is the book itself.

The Theological-Political Treatise appeared in Latin in sixteen seventy. The title page gave no author and named a false place of printing. Its anonymity was not a literary game. Spinoza had reason to expect hostility. The book questioned established accounts of prophecy, miracles, biblical authorship, and the authority of religious institutions. It also argued that a state should protect freedom of thought and speech.

The work circulated as a printed object, not as a manuscript passed quietly from hand to hand. Copies survived in libraries and private collections. The title page concealed the author, but readers soon connected the work with Baruch Spinoza.

The connection is also preserved in Spinoza's correspondence.

His letters were written in Latin, Dutch, and other languages according to the circumstances of the exchange. The surviving correspondence includes letters to Henry Oldenburg, secretary of the Royal Society in London, and to other members of the philosophical network around Spinoza. These letters show an author answering objections, clarifying distinctions, and defending the right to investigate nature and Scripture without submitting every conclusion to inherited authority.

A letter is not the same kind of evidence as a printed treatise.

The Treatise presents a public argument arranged for readers. A letter records a particular exchange, often written in response to a question or controversy. Together they show how Spinoza described his purposes, but they do not provide a complete diary of his private thoughts. No surviving letter gives a full account of every event that led to his expulsion from the Amsterdam community.

The communal record is more direct.

On the twenty seventh of July sixteen fifty six, the authorities of the Talmud Torah congregation in Amsterdam pronounced a cherem against the young Bento de Espinosa. The text survives in the congregation's records. It declares that the authorities had learned of the evil opinions and works of Espinosa, investigated them, and found them increasingly serious. It excludes him from the people of Israel and curses him in language intended to make the penalty absolute.

The proclamation says that he was cursed by day and by night, in lying down and in rising up, and that no one should communicate with him, come within four cubits of him, or read anything composed or written by him.

The document does not set out the exact propositions that caused the penalty.

That absence is central. Later accounts describe Spinoza as holding radical views about God, Scripture, and immortality, but the surviving cherem itself does not list a theological indictment in numbered form. It gives the communal decision and its sanctions. It does not tell precisely which words Spinoza had spoken, which books he had read, or which conversation had led to the investigation.

Other records fill the background only unevenly.

The Amsterdam Jewish community had known conflict before. Uriel da Costa, a Portuguese-born thinker who challenged rabbinic authority and the immortality of the soul, had been subjected to communal penalties earlier in the century. His autobiography, Exemplar Humanae Vitae, describes his disputes and humiliations, though it was published after his death and must be read as a shaped account of memory. Da Costa's case became part of the community's remembered history. It also became part of the context in which Spinoza's expulsion was later understood.

The first biographies appeared after Spinoza's death.

Jean Maximilien Lucas wrote an early French life that circulated in manuscript and print. Lucas presented Spinoza as a courageous philosopher opposed to religious persecution. His portrait contains information gathered from people who had known Spinoza, but it also has a clear purpose. It turns a life into an argument about intellectual freedom.

Johannes Colerus, a Lutheran minister in The Hague, wrote another biography. Colerus had lived near Spinoza in the final period of his life and gathered testimony from people who had known him. His account is more hostile in tone than Lucas's, yet it preserves details about Spinoza's rooms, habits, work, and final illness. It also repeats stories that cannot always be tested against earlier evidence.

Neither biography was written while Spinoza was a child in Amsterdam. Neither provides a continuous eyewitness record. Lucas and Colerus wrote after the events, from different positions, with different purposes. Their disagreements show how quickly Spinoza's life became contested territory.

The posthumous publication of Ethics gives another kind of evidence.

Spinoza died in The Hague in sixteen seventy seven. In the same year, his friends arranged the publication of his major philosophical works, including Ethics. The book appeared with his initials and a printer's mark, not with the open authorial presentation that a safer writer might have used. Its geometric structure had been composed over years, revised, and circulated in parts among correspondents.

Ethics presents Spinoza's account of God, nature, mind, body, bondage, and freedom. It is not a commentary on the Bible. It does not retell the Sinai narrative. But it helps explain the philosophical background of the Treatise. Spinoza's God is not described as a ruler who changes plans, issues commands in response to human events, or acts outside the order of nature. His account of necessity and causation stands behind his treatment of miracles and prophecy.

The two books must not be collapsed into one.

The Theological-Political Treatise addresses Scripture, political authority, and religious practice in historical terms. Ethics constructs a philosophical system through definitions, axioms, propositions, demonstrations, and scholia. A claim about Moses in the Treatise belongs to an argument about the formation and interpretation of biblical texts. A claim about God in Ethics belongs to a different architectural structure.

Later readers often joined the works because both carried Spinoza's name and because the Treatise helped explain the religious consequences of Ethics. The historical record shows a relationship, but not identity.

Modern scholars reconstruct that relationship with caution.

Steven Nadler, in Spinoza: A Life, uses the surviving letters, early biographies, communal records, and other documents to distinguish what can be established from later legend. In A Book Forged in Hell, Nadler places the Treatise within the political and religious conflicts of the Dutch Republic and explains why its anonymity mattered.

Jonathan Israel, in Radical Enlightenment, presents Spinoza as a central figure in a broad movement of criticism directed at inherited authority, clerical power, and traditional accounts of nature and revelation. Israel's interpretation gives special weight to networks of printers, merchants, philosophers, and religious dissenters.

Edwin Curley's translation of Spinoza's writings, including volume two of The Collected Works of Spinoza, provides the English wording followed here for the relevant passages of the Treatise. Translation is part of the evidence. Latin sentences carry legal, theological, and philosophical meanings that can shift when rendered into English. A translation makes the argument accessible, but the Latin remains the underlying text.

These sources do not stand at the same distance from Spinoza.

The cherem records an institutional decision made during his lifetime. The letters preserve his own words, though only in selected exchanges. The Treatise presents his public reasoning under a veil of anonymity. Lucas and Colerus report on him later. Ethics was printed after his death. Modern scholars compare all these materials and add knowledge about Amsterdam, Dutch politics, printing, Jewish communal life, and early modern philosophy.

The evidence therefore comes in layers.

One layer is what Spinoza wrote. Another is what others said about him. A third is the interpretation of historians who assess the first two. Keeping those layers separate matters most where the surviving record becomes thin.

Spinoza's name was not always the same.

Within the Portuguese Jewish community of Amsterdam, he was Bento de Espinosa. In Latin philosophical correspondence and later European scholarship, he became Baruch Spinoza. The change of form belongs to the movement of his life from the congregation's world into the wider republic of letters.

The person behind both names entered that world through Amsterdam.

In the autumn of sixteen thirty two, a child was born in Amsterdam to a Portuguese Jewish merchant family.

The child was named Bento de Espinosa. Later records identify him as Baruch Spinoza. His family belonged to the community formed by Jews of Portuguese and Spanish origin whose ancestors had lived under Iberian Christian rule, conversion, exile, and migration. Amsterdam offered a place where Jewish communal life could be rebuilt, though it remained subject to civic regulation and depended on delicate relations with the city's authorities.

The family lived near the Houtgracht, in the part of Amsterdam associated with Jewish residents and merchants. The streets held warehouses, workshops, houses, and canal water moving between the built edges of the city. Commerce connected Amsterdam to Portugal, Spain, the Caribbean, North Africa, and other ports. A merchant family did not live apart from the city's markets. Its business depended on letters, credit, cargo, partners, and trust.

The surviving record does not describe the child's first years in detail.

His father was Miguel de Espinosa, a merchant active in the Portuguese Jewish community. His mother, Hanna Debora, died while Bento was still young. The family endured the deaths of other close relatives as well. Such losses appear in the biographical record, but the record does not preserve Bento's private response. Later writers could describe the family's circumstances; they could not recover the child's thoughts.

Amsterdam's community maintained schools and institutions for worship, charity, discipline, and trade. Bento studied in the congregation's educational system. The Talmud Torah congregation taught Hebrew texts and Jewish law, along with the languages and skills needed by its members. A boy who advanced through this system would encounter the Hebrew Bible in the language of the community, commentaries from earlier Jewish scholars, rabbinic interpretation, and arguments over law and doctrine.

The schoolroom formed one part of Bento's education.

The family business formed another. After his father's death, Bento was involved in commercial affairs with his brother Gabriel. The available documents suggest that he knew the practical world of accounts, contracts, goods, and credit. Later portraits often move quickly from the merchant's house to the philosopher's workbench, but the transition was not instantaneous. Spinoza came from a trading community and remained connected to people whose lives were organized around commerce.

The community also carried memories of disciplinary conflict.

Uriel da Costa had arrived in Amsterdam from the Iberian world and challenged teachings he regarded as human additions to the law of Moses. He rejected the immortality of the soul as taught by rabbinic authorities and resisted communal discipline. After repeated penalties, he submitted to a public reconciliation in sixteen forty seven. His autobiography describes the ceremony: the humiliation, the bodily punishment, and the return to the community. Soon afterward, he died by suicide.

Da Costa's case had taken place before Bento reached adulthood, but it remained close in time and place. The memory of a man punished for theological dissent belonged to the community's recent past. Later historians have treated that memory as part of the atmosphere surrounding Spinoza. The surviving evidence does not show that Bento and Da Costa met, or that Da Costa directly instructed him. It shows that the community knew the case and that later accounts placed the two men in the same genealogy of dissent.

Bento's questions took shape within several worlds at once.

He knew the Hebrew Bible through communal education. He knew the legal and interpretive traditions that surrounded it. He also encountered Christian writings and the philosophical literature circulating in the Dutch Republic. Amsterdam's printers supplied works in Latin, Dutch, Portuguese, and other languages. Jewish merchants traded not only in goods but also in news, letters, and books.

At some point in the sixteen fifties, Bento became connected with Latin-speaking circles outside the congregation.

The name of Franciscus van den Enden appears in this setting. Van den Enden was a former Jesuit, a schoolmaster, a seller of books, and a political radical. He taught Latin in Amsterdam. His students encountered classical authors and philosophical arguments that did not belong to the curriculum of the Talmud Torah congregation. Spinoza's exact relationship with Van den Enden remains difficult to document. Later accounts connect Spinoza with his school, and the connection is broadly accepted, but the surviving record does not preserve a complete syllabus or a dated sequence of lessons.

Latin changed the range of texts available to Bento.

Through Latin, he could read Descartes and other philosophers whose works were being discussed across Europe. He could also enter correspondence with scholars and intellectuals who did not belong to the Portuguese Jewish community. The language became a tool for moving between worlds: Hebrew commentary, Dutch civic life, Portuguese family networks, and European philosophy.

The movement created strain.

Spinoza's later writings would treat the Bible as a historical collection shaped by particular communities and political circumstances. Such a treatment challenged the assumption that the sacred text's authority settled every question about its origin and interpretation. The conflict was not merely between religion and philosophy in the abstract. It involved a living congregation, its teachers, its laws, and the authority of its leaders.

The documents do not give a clean moment when Bento ceased to accept the community's beliefs.

Modern biographers disagree about the pace and content of his intellectual change. Steven Nadler emphasizes the difficulty of separating later testimony from evidence created during Spinoza's youth. Jonathan Israel places Spinoza within Amsterdam's wider culture of religious debate and free inquiry. Both approaches reject the simple image of a single dramatic revelation transforming a faithful boy into a philosopher.

The family business continued while the questions deepened.

In sixteen fifty four, Bento's father died. The death left financial and family responsibilities. Bento and Gabriel became involved in the business, and disputes over inheritance followed. Later legal documents show Bento withdrawing from the firm. The commercial world that had formed his early life did not become his permanent occupation.

He was also moving away from the communal identity expressed by his name.

The record does not state that he publicly announced a new doctrine before the cherem. It does not preserve a transcript of the meetings that preceded the decision. The sequence has to be reconstructed from the communal proclamation, later biographies, and the intellectual position visible in works written afterward.

By the middle of the sixteen fifties, the young merchant's son had become a subject of concern to the congregation.

The concern may have involved spoken opinions, written arguments, conduct, or reports from others. The surviving record uses collective language about evil opinions and works but does not specify them. Modern historians infer from Spinoza's later works that questions about God, Scripture, the soul, and law were already pressing upon him. Those inferences are plausible, but they are not a transcript of the earlier investigation.

The city around him had its own tensions.

The Dutch Republic allowed a degree of religious diversity that was unusual in Europe, yet toleration was limited and conditional. Calvinist institutions held public prominence. Political authority shifted between competing factions. Commercial prosperity coexisted with anxiety about war, faction, and popular unrest. Amsterdam's Jewish community could govern many internal matters, but it did not possess unlimited power. Its disciplinary actions had social and economic consequences because belonging affected family, trade, worship, and burial.

A young man could cross a boundary without leaving the city.

Bento did not need to flee Amsterdam immediately. The decisive act came inside the congregation's system of authority. On a day in July sixteen fifty six, the leaders of the Talmud Torah congregation issued their judgment.

The document still survives.

It is short compared with the life that later grew around it. It names Bento de Espinosa and describes the investigation that preceded the penalty. It does not preserve his answers. It does not tell whether he stood before the congregation, whether he argued, or whether he remained silent. The next record is the language of exclusion.

On the twenty seventh of July sixteen fifty six, the Talmud Torah congregation in Amsterdam pronounced a cherem against Bento de Espinosa.

The surviving proclamation used language of complete separation. It declared that the authorities had examined his actions and opinions, consulted others, and found grounds for a severe penalty. Then it placed him outside the community's protection and fellowship.

The curse extended through the ordinary divisions of time. It invoked day and night, lying down and rising up. It forbade members of the congregation to communicate with him, come within four cubits of him, or read anything written or composed by him. The language was meant to reach speech, movement, social contact, and intellectual exchange.

The document called for no conversation with the condemned man.

It did not identify the exact offenses.

That silence has shaped the whole later history of Spinoza's expulsion. Readers know the penalty with unusual precision, yet they do not know the propositions that prompted it with equal precision. The cherem does not say that Bento denied a particular doctrine in a particular phrase. It does not mention the Ten Commandments, Moses, Sinai, or the authorship of the Pentateuch. It records an institutional judgment rather than a theological trial in full.

Later stories supplied details.

Jean Maximilien Lucas reported that an attempt had been made to stab Spinoza near the synagogue, and that he preserved the torn coat as a reminder of the danger. The story became part of the image of the philosopher marked for death by religious enemies. Colerus also repeated an account of an attack, though details vary between versions.

The evidence for the attempted stabbing is late.

No surviving contemporary communal document confirms it. The cherem does not mention physical violence. Modern biographers, including Steven Nadler, treat the story as possible but uncertain rather than as an established fact. The coat may have existed; the episode may have been remembered accurately or enlarged in retelling. The records cannot settle the matter.

The institutional penalty itself is firmer.

After the cherem, Spinoza no longer belonged to the Amsterdam congregation. The prohibition was not the same as a modern criminal sentence. It was a communal and religious sanction, but its effects reached ordinary life. Family relations could become difficult. Business ties could be interrupted. Access to communal institutions was lost. The person under the cherem stood outside the social structure that had shaped his education.

The name Bento began to recede from the philosophical record.

Baruch Spinoza appeared instead.

He moved among friends who did not depend on the congregation for their intellectual identity. Some were Christians from dissenting circles. Others were merchants, printers, or philosophers connected to the Dutch republic of letters. The surviving letters show a network of exchange, but not a single organized school. Spinoza was not the head of a hidden society. He was a writer whose friendships gave him protection, books, discussion, and access to publication.

The first place associated with his new life was Rijnsburg.

Rijnsburg, near Leiden, was known for a collegiant community. The collegiants gathered for discussion and worship without a priestly hierarchy. Spinoza stayed there during the years after the cherem, and accounts connect him with lens grinding and philosophical correspondence. The small instruments of the optical trade suited a life that required income without dependence on a commercial partnership.

A lens began as a piece of glass that had to be shaped, polished, and examined.

Spinoza's work demanded patience and precision. Later biographers described him at the workbench, though their descriptions were written after his death and may carry the quiet moral symbolism of the philosopher who earned a modest living by his hands. The physical craft is documented broadly; the precise rhythm of his days is not.

Rijnsburg also placed him within a wider network.

Letters passed between Amsterdam, Leiden, London, and other cities. Henry Oldenburg wrote to Spinoza from England. The correspondence touched on philosophy, science, religion, and the political risks of publication. Oldenburg was not merely collecting gossip. He was part of the international exchange through which arguments about nature and Scripture moved.

Spinoza's ideas were already drawing attention before the Treatise appeared.

The early work on Descartes, published in sixteen sixty three, carried his name and showed how he could present another philosopher's system while developing his own position. The book did not yet expose the full religious and political argument of the Treatise. It did show a writer capable of precise exposition and deliberate philosophical architecture.

His social position remained vulnerable.

A person excluded from a Jewish congregation could still live in the Dutch Republic, but public protection depended on local conditions and personal connections. Spinoza avoided presenting himself as a public religious leader. He did not create a new congregation. He did not issue a replacement set of rites. His activity was philosophical, literary, and commercial.

From Rijnsburg he later moved to Voorburg, near The Hague.

The move brought him closer to political and intellectual circles. Johan de Witt, the leading statesman of the Dutch Republic during the period of the Treatise's composition, represented a political order that Spinoza regarded with interest. Spinoza's political writing defended a state's role in securing peace while allowing citizens freedom to think and speak. He did not simply identify philosophy with a particular faction, but the Treatise's publication occurred amid intense political conflict.

Adriaan Koerbagh's fate made the danger plain.

Koerbagh was a friend and radical critic of religious authority. In sixteen sixty eight, he was arrested after his writings were judged offensive by authorities. He died in prison the following year. Spinoza's Treatise appeared only after this event. The prosecution showed that Dutch toleration had limits, especially when criticism seemed capable of disturbing religious and political order.

Spinoza watched the boundaries closely.

The Treatise was printed anonymously in sixteen seventy. Its title page used a false place of publication. The author's identity was soon suspected, and opposition followed. The book's argument for freedom of philosophizing became inseparable from the question of why a writer had needed to conceal his name.

The cherem had been public within the congregation.

The Treatise made Spinoza's challenge public across Europe.

By then he was no longer trying to remain inside the authority that had condemned him. Yet he also did not describe the cherem in the Treatise as a personal grievance. The book argued at the level of Scripture, politics, and reason. Its author transformed the experience of communal exclusion into a general analysis of how institutions claim the right to govern interpretation.

The transition from expelled member to anonymous author was not a single step.

The surviving letters show revisions, delays, caution, and concern about hostile readers. Spinoza had friends who urged publication and friends who warned against it. He knew that a printed accusation of theological error could endanger more than reputation. It could threaten employment, friendship, and legal security.

Later tradition made the moment theatrical.

The documents make it quieter. A proclamation was entered into a communal record. A young man left the social world in which he had been educated. He found work with glass, joined circles of correspondence, and wrote a book whose title page concealed his name.

The cherem remained the clearest public statement made against him.

The Treatise became his answer, though not an answer in the form of a confession or rebuttal. Its opening chapters moved through prophecy, revelation, and the Hebrew Bible. The question of Sinai entered through Spinoza's account of how prophets received and communicated divine messages.

In the Treatise, Spinoza began with prophecy rather than with the date of Moses.

He asked what separated a prophet from an ordinary person. The answer did not rest on superior philosophical understanding. Prophets, he argued, were not necessarily better at reasoning about nature. They received revelation through imagination.

For Spinoza, imagination did not mean deliberate invention.

It meant the human power through which images, voices, signs, dreams, memories, and unusual perceptions became present to the mind. Prophecy worked through that power. A prophet could receive a message vividly and sincerely while lacking the intellectual demonstration of its ultimate cause.

The prophets therefore differed from one another.

Their visions and speeches carried the marks of their languages, temperaments, education, and inherited opinions. An Egyptian prophet would not imagine revelation in exactly the same form as a Hebrew prophet. A prophet accustomed to dreams would receive signs through dreams. Another might hear a voice or see a figure. Spinoza's argument was that the form of revelation followed the constitution of the person receiving it.

God, in Spinoza's account, adapted revelation to the understanding of the prophet.

The language was not that of a lecturer giving a technical explanation to a trained audience. It was language suited to the imagination. A revelation could therefore contain imagery and assumptions belonging to the prophet's world without becoming false as a prophetic communication. The prophet's certainty came from the force of the experience and from the moral effect of the message, not from a philosophical proof of God's nature.

Spinoza drew a firm boundary between prophecy and knowledge.

A prophet might know that a command had been delivered and might call the source God. The prophet did not therefore understand the necessary order of nature. Prophecy did not make a person a philosopher. In Spinoza's system, intellectual knowledge of God belonged to reason, while prophetic knowledge reached people through imagination and was directed toward obedience and conduct.

The distinction affected the Sinai narrative.

Exodus describes the Israelites arriving at Mount Sinai after leaving Egypt. The mountain is associated with thunder, lightning, a thick cloud, and the sound of a trumpet. The people tremble. Moses goes up the mountain. The Lord speaks the words that become the Decalogue.

The text presents the event as a collective revelation.

Spinoza did not simply erase that presentation. He examined what kind of revelation the narrative described and what it could mean within his account of prophecy. The Israelites, as a people, were not presented as receiving a philosophical demonstration. They encountered divine law in a form accessible to their imagination and political needs.

The Decalogue was law.

It was not, in Spinoza's treatment, a timeless philosophical treatise on God's essence. It formed part of the constitution of the Israelite state. The commandments ordered conduct, worship, family relations, property, speech, and communal time. Their authority belonged to the political community established through the covenant.

This point depended on Spinoza's broader distinction between divine law and ceremonial law.

He argued that the universal law of God concerned justice and charity, understood as requirements of conduct that could be known through reason. The particular laws given to the Israelites organized their state and religious constitution. They were divine in the sense that the Israelites understood them as commands from God, but they were also tied to the historical condition of one people.

The scene at Sinai therefore had two levels.

At the level of the text, the Israelites heard and received commandments amid the signs of thunder, cloud, fire, and trumpet. At the level of Spinoza's interpretation, the form of the event belonged to revelation through imagination, and the content of the Decalogue functioned as law for a particular political body.

Spinoza did not say that the Israelites heard nothing.

Nor did he say that Moses simply fabricated a voice and deceived the people. His argument was more exacting. A prophetic voice, even when treated as divine, did not give the hearer philosophical knowledge of God. The revelation was understood through human faculties and shaped for human comprehension.

The biblical narrative itself makes the people afraid.

In Exodus, after the thunder, lightning, trumpet, and smoking mountain, the people stand at a distance. They tell Moses to speak to them rather than God speaking directly, because they fear that direct encounter will kill them. The text then presents Moses as approaching the thick darkness where God was.

Spinoza considered this passage within the difference between Moses and the people.

Moses occupied the role of mediator and lawgiver. The people received the law through a collective event that established their political order. Spinoza's account did not turn Moses into a philosopher with access to a higher metaphysical realm. It made him the central human figure through whom the constitution was given, interpreted, and administered.

The claim was especially important because the traditional attribution of the Pentateuch to Moses carried authority.

If Moses had written the entire Pentateuch, then its unity and authority could be explained through his authorship. Spinoza argued that the books, in the form known to readers, contained evidence of later arrangement. References to Moses in the third person, descriptions of his death, and statements about conditions continuing after his lifetime made complete Mosaic authorship difficult to sustain.

He did not present this as a discovery made from nowhere.

Ibn Ezra had already pointed to passages that created problems for the simple claim that Moses wrote every word. Spinoza treated Ibn Ezra's remarks as evidence that Jewish interpretation had not always been as uniform as later defenders of total Mosaic authorship suggested. Thomas Hobbes had also discussed biblical authorship in Leviathan, arguing that Moses could not have written sections describing events after his death.

The Treatise used these observations to make a larger point.

The Bible had a history. Its books had been transmitted, arranged, and interpreted by communities. Their authority within a political and religious order did not require the assumption that one human author had written the entire collection at one time.

The Sinai narrative belonged to that history.

Spinoza's reading did not depend on proving that no event had occurred at the mountain. It depended on distinguishing the narrative's religious meaning from the historical question of how the text came to exist. The text says that commandments were given. Spinoza asked how the account represented revelation, what kind of knowledge it conveyed, and which political community the law established.

Curley's translation makes the structure of the argument visible.

Spinoza states that prophets were endowed with a vivid imagination rather than a more perfect intellect. He explains that God accommodated revelations to the opinions and understanding of the prophets. In this framework, the same divine purpose could appear through different images and forms because the recipients differed.

The English wording must be handled carefully.

Spinoza's claim is not that every biblical voice was merely a private hallucination in the modern clinical sense. That vocabulary belongs to later categories and does not describe his argument accurately. His point is philosophical and historical: revelation entered human consciousness through imagination, and prophetic certainty did not equal rational demonstration.

Nor does he say that every command in the Bible had universal authority.

The Decalogue, as Spinoza reads it, belongs to the Israelite state. The people were organized through law after the departure from Egypt. Their commandments regulated a historical polity. When that polity ended, the ceremonial and political system no longer possessed the same function. The universal moral demand of justice and charity remained conceptually distinct from the particular laws that had governed Israel.

This reading helped Spinoza explain why biblical laws could be authoritative without being addressed in the same way to every nation.

The Israelites at Sinai did not receive a handbook of abstract metaphysics. They received a constitution in the form of divine command, presented through signs that the people could recognize and fear. Moses stood between the collective experience and the ordered life that followed.

The textual record gives the scene its own sequence.

The people come to the mountain. Boundaries are set around it. The mountain is covered by cloud. Thunder and lightning appear. The trumpet sounds. Moses brings the people out to meet God. They stand below. The voice speaks the commandments. The people withdraw and ask Moses to speak in their place. Moses approaches the darkness.

Spinoza's interpretation does not replace that sequence.

It asks what sort of knowledge the sequence represents. The voice at Sinai is not, in his account, a philosophical lecture delivered in a form that bypasses the human mind. It is revelation as received by a people through imagination, fear, authority, and law.

The later history of the text would complicate the question further.

Exodus presents one version of the Decalogue within the mountain narrative. Deuteronomy presents another in Moses's address to the Israelites before entry into the land. The Sabbath command is framed differently in the two books. The existence of two forms shows that the biblical tradition itself preserved more than one arrangement of the commandments.

Spinoza did not possess modern source criticism, but he read the text closely enough to see tensions in its structure.

Jean Astruc, Julius Wellhausen, and later scholars would develop methods for analyzing repeated divine names, double narratives, vocabulary, legal differences, and editorial seams. Their models differ from Spinoza's, and modern scholarship does not treat any one reconstruction as final. Yet the historical study of the Pentateuch still asks the question Spinoza forced into the open: how did separate materials become the books now read as a continuous account?

At Sinai, his answer was already taking shape.

The authority of the Decalogue belonged to a people, a political constitution, and a mode of revelation adapted to human understanding. Moses remained central to the story, but Moses was not therefore the sole writer of the text that later bore his name.

The voice at Sinai, in Spinoza's reading, was a voice encountered through history, language, imagination, and law.

It was not a philosophical proof delivered from outside those human forms.

The next movement of the argument began with a name that Spinoza treated cautiously: Ibn Ezra.

In the eighth chapter of the Theological-Political Treatise, Spinoza followed Ibn Ezra through a series of remarks about the books attributed to Moses. Ibn Ezra had not simply denied the traditional attribution. He had written in hints, guarded phrases, and partial observations. His readers had to gather the meaning from details that appeared almost incidental.

One detail stood near the end of Deuteronomy. Moses died before Israel crossed the Jordan. Yet the book described his burial and said that no one knew his sepulchre unto this day. A writer speaking in Moses' own time could not naturally have used that phrase. The words pointed toward a later generation, one that already looked back upon Moses as a figure of the past.

Another passage said that Moses had been the meekest man on the face of the earth. That sentence could not easily have come from Moses himself. It described him from outside, as a subject of memory and praise.

The text also referred to kings who would rule Israel. Such a reference made better sense after the rise of the monarchy than during the lifetime of Moses. Elsewhere, the narrative crossed places and customs whose names belonged to later periods. The effect was cumulative. No single phrase settled the matter. Together, the phrases made the traditional picture difficult to preserve in its simplest form.

Ibn Ezra had noticed this pressure. He had not openly declared that Moses wrote none of the Pentateuch. Instead, he had placed certain observations beside one another and allowed the implication to remain just beneath the surface.

Spinoza brought those observations into the open.

His argument did not begin with a modern theory of anonymous authorship. It began with the words on the page. If the text described Moses' death, his burial, and the continued existence of a custom or place unto this day, then at least those portions had been written after Moses. The question became larger. Were these later additions attached to a work originally written by Moses, or did the whole Pentateuch belong to a later process of compilation?

Spinoza examined the books as a continuous account rather than as five separate objects. Exodus moved from Egypt to Sinai, from the covenant to the construction of the sanctuary. Deuteronomy presented Moses' speeches on the edge of the land, then continued beyond his death. The sequence looked like a history whose narrator stood at a distance from the events.

The phrase unto this day mattered because it carried the voice of a later present into an earlier story. A writer used it when an institution, place, or custom remained known in the writer's own time. Spinoza saw the same kind of temporal distance in the account of Moses' tomb. The text did not merely preserve an ancient command. It also recorded how later people remembered the commandgiver.

Then came a second line of evidence. The Ten Commandments did not appear in precisely the same form in Exodus and Deuteronomy.

In Exodus, the Sabbath command connected the seventh day with creation. The reason given was that the deity made heaven and earth, the sea, and all that was in them in six days, and rested on the seventh. In Deuteronomy, the Sabbath command connected the day with the memory of slavery in Egypt. Israel was told to remember that it had been a servant in Egypt and that the deity had brought it out.

The command remained recognizable. The wording and stated reason changed.

Spinoza drew a distinction from that difference. Moses, in the traditional account, had delivered the commandments at Sinai. But the written form preserved in the Pentateuch belonged to later transmission and arrangement. The existence of two versions showed that the text was not a single untouched record copied from one moment.

The difference did not, by itself, prove that the commandments had never been proclaimed. It showed that the written narratives presented them through more than one formulation. Spinoza's interest lay in the history of the text, not in a simple accusation of fraud.

He then returned to Ibn Ezra's most consequential suggestion. If Moses had not written the complete Pentateuch, perhaps Ezra had collected and arranged the materials after the exile.

The proposal did not mean that Ezra had invented every law, story, or speech. It meant that the books had reached their recognizable form through a later editor. The compiler had inherited traditions, documents, laws, genealogies, and memories, and had joined them into a national history.

The thought had predecessors.

Thomas Hobbes had already argued in Leviathan that Moses could not have written all the material attributed to him. Hobbes pointed to the account of Moses' death and to expressions that presupposed a later time. His conclusion was restrained in one respect and radical in another. The Pentateuch could preserve Mosaic material without being wholly written by Moses.

Across the same century, Isaac La Peyrere had questioned accepted assumptions about biblical history and the antiquity of the human race. His work did not reproduce Spinoza's argument, but it belonged to the same European movement of textual and historical criticism. Ancient authority was increasingly examined through language, chronology, and internal evidence rather than accepted only because tradition assigned a name to a book.

The men did not meet in a single room. Their meeting occurred through printed pages, private correspondence, libraries, and the circulation of arguments across the Dutch Republic, France, and England. Hobbes wrote in England. La Peyrere worked in France and the Low Countries. Spinoza read and answered a European conversation whose participants often disagreed about politics, religion, and the limits of reason.

Spinoza's own conclusion was more systematic than Ibn Ezra's hints and more closely tied to the structure of the entire Bible. He argued that the Pentateuch was not an isolated divine deposit but part of a historical literature. Its present form reflected the work of a later compiler, most plausibly Ezra in the account he was examining.

The argument did not depend on proving that every sentence had been written at one time. It depended on showing that the text contained layers of time. Moses appeared in the book as lawgiver and speaker. A later narrator appeared in the words about his death, his tomb, the kings, and customs continuing unto this day. The Pentateuch therefore carried the marks of several historical moments.

A further problem followed. If the books had been compiled after Moses, then the common phrase the law of Moses required care. It could mean the laws associated with Moses, not a volume physically written by his hand. The distinction was small in language and large in consequence.

Spinoza did not erase Moses from the story. He placed Moses in the period described by the narrative and treated him as the source of legislation and political organization. What he rejected was the assumption that the final written collection must have come directly from Moses himself.

The source Spinoza was reading was already a received text. The Hebrew Bible had been copied, transmitted, translated, and interpreted across centuries. His evidence came from the wording and arrangement available to him, not from manuscripts of Moses' lifetime. Modern readers possess additional evidence, but no original Pentateuchal manuscript.

Steven Nadler describes Spinoza's biblical criticism as a central part of his attempt to explain religion historically rather than through inherited claims of authorship. The point was not merely that Moses' name might be wrong on a title page. The point was that a text could acquire authority while passing through ordinary processes of memory, selection, compilation, and political use.

The argument ended this section of the Treatise with a shift in scale. The question was no longer simply whether Moses had written a book. It was whether a written law could be called divine without being a text dictated word for word in the form later generations received.

That question carried Spinoza from authorship into law.

Spinoza's answer rested on a distinction between two kinds of law.

The first belonged to the ancient Hebrew state. It governed courts, sacrifices, festivals, priestly duties, food regulations, military arrangements, inheritance, punishment, and public order. Moses, in the narrative of Exodus and Deuteronomy, did not merely teach private belief. He organized a people.

Spinoza looked at that organization politically.

After the departure from Egypt, the Hebrew tribes were presented as a population without an established state. At Sinai, Moses delivered laws and entered a covenant with the people. The laws distributed authority, defined obligations, and marked the community as distinct from neighboring peoples. The sanctuary gave worship a public center. Priests and Levites received specified roles. Festivals ordered the calendar. The law reached into the structure of collective life.

For Spinoza, those commands were not timeless civil regulations floating above history. They belonged to a particular political body.

A civil law binds through the authority of a state. When that state changes or disappears, the law's political force changes with it. Spinoza therefore argued that the laws of Moses were binding on the Hebrews while their commonwealth stood. They were laws of a nation, not a universal civil code for every society.

This claim did not make the laws meaningless after the fall of the ancient state. The stories and commandments could continue to shape memory, identity, and interpretation. Their political jurisdiction, however, could not simply be transferred unchanged into every later country.

The distinction became sharp when Spinoza discussed ceremony.

Ceremonies, in his account, served political ends. They trained a population in common habits, separated one community from others, and attached obedience to visible actions. Sacrifices, ritual purity, sacred times, and rules of food created a disciplined public order. They gave the state a religious form.

He did not describe ceremonies as empty in the sense of having no historical function. Their function was precisely what made them important. They helped build a people.

The true divine law, by contrast, did not depend on the survival of the Hebrew state. Spinoza identified it with the knowledge and love of God and with justice and charity toward others. Its substance was moral and intellectual rather than ceremonial or national.

The distinction was not a separation between a good religion and a bad religion. It was a separation between different kinds of command.

A rule concerning a festival or a sacrifice required a community organized around that practice. A rule requiring justice could apply wherever human beings lived together. A law of the state addressed citizens as members of one political body. A divine law of justice addressed human conduct more broadly.

Spinoza's use of the word God also required care. His God was not the anthropomorphic ruler who issued decrees like a human monarch. In the Ethics, Spinoza identified God with the one infinite substance of which all things were modes. The Theological-Political Treatise used language shaped by biblical religion, but its philosophical meaning did not simply reproduce the ordinary image of a personal lawgiver.

That is why the claim that Spinoza denied God's part in the commandments needs exact wording.

In Spinoza's account, the written commandments were laws given through Moses for an ancient people. They were preserved, arranged, and transmitted in historical texts. They were not timeless words dictated in the precise literary form later readers encountered.

But Spinoza did not say that God had nothing to do with them in his own sense of God. The laws belonged to a human political history, while the moral truth expressed through justice, charity, and the love of God belonged to the rational order of nature. His God was not removed from the account. God was understood differently.

One position concerns the commandments as political institutions. Another concerns their moral content. A third concerns the form of the written text. Spinoza kept these questions connected but did not collapse them into one.

Moses could therefore be a legislator without being the final author of the Pentateuch. The commandments could be historically delivered through Moses without appearing in one untouched written version. A text could be compiled by later hands and still preserve a teaching that Spinoza regarded as morally significant.

The difference between Exodus and Deuteronomy illustrated the point. The Sabbath command in Exodus gave creation as its reason. Deuteronomy gave liberation from slavery. The formulations served different rhetorical settings. One placed the command within the order of creation. The other placed it within the memory of national suffering and rescue.

The command's moral and political effect did not require identical wording. A later community could preserve, interpret, and adapt a command while claiming continuity with the earlier revelation.

Spinoza's opponents heard danger in this flexibility. If the written form could be historically shaped, then religious authority could no longer rest only on a claim about dictation. If ceremonies were political, then religious institutions could be examined as institutions. If prophecy was understood through the language and imagination of prophets, then revelation could be studied historically.

The Theological-Political Treatise did not ask readers to abandon every religious claim. It argued that Scripture should be read according to its own language, history, and purpose. The Bible taught obedience to justice and charity more clearly than it taught metaphysics. Philosophical truth and religious obedience belonged to different domains.

The political implication was direct. A state needed freedom to investigate nature and speak about religion, provided public peace and justice were preserved. Spinoza opposed clerical power when it sought to control thought or civil government. His defense of interpretation became part of a defense of intellectual liberty.

The argument also explained why Spinoza distinguished the ancient Hebrew state from later Jewish communities. The ceremonies had once formed part of a national constitution. After the ancient state ended, those ceremonies could preserve communal identity, but they did not automatically recreate the old political jurisdiction.

The text therefore moved between ancient law and modern authority. Its discussion of Moses was historical, but its target included the seventeenth-century struggle over who could define religion in public life.

Edwin Curley's translation and edition of the Treatise preserves the force of this structure. Spinoza proceeded from the authority of Scripture to the nature of prophecy, from the history of the Hebrews to the role of ceremonies, and from those subjects to the freedom of thought. The sequence was not accidental. Authorship mattered because authority mattered. Authority mattered because civil peace and intellectual freedom were at stake.

A reader could disagree with Spinoza's philosophy of God and still recognize the historical claim. The Pentateuch as read in the seventeenth century was a literary collection with signs of different periods. The laws within it addressed a people situated in history. The later state of Israelite society could not be separated from the meaning of the laws.

This was the position that made the book dangerous to its opponents. It did not merely question one detail. It changed the place from which the Bible could be read.

The question was no longer whether a command had once been spoken at Sinai. The question was how that command became a written law, how a people used it, and how later institutions claimed power from it.

The book entered public life under pressure.

The Theological-Political Treatise appeared anonymously in sixteen seventy. Its title page named no author and gave a false place of publication. Readers in the Dutch Republic quickly suspected Spinoza. The anonymity protected neither the argument nor its presumed author for long.

Reformed synods condemned the work. Their resolutions described it as irreligious and dangerous, and pressed civil authorities to restrain its circulation. The language of condemnation belonged to a period in which disputes over Scripture were also disputes over public order. A book that challenged clerical authority could be treated as a threat to the religious settlement of the province.

The States of Holland outlawed the Treatise in sixteen seventy four. The prohibition did not end its circulation. It made the book more difficult to print openly, but copies moved through private networks, booksellers, and readers who understood the value of an anonymous title.

Spinoza had already learned what public condemnation could mean.

In Amsterdam, in sixteen fifty six, the Talmud Torah congregation had issued the cherem against Bento de Espinosa. The surviving notice used severe language and cut him off from the Jewish community. It did not set out a full philosophical account of his ideas, and the precise sequence of claims that led to the sentence remains uncertain.

The notice is clear about the result. Bento de Espinosa was expelled, and members of the community were ordered not to maintain contact with him. The young man who had grown up in Amsterdam was separated from the congregation in which his family had worshipped.

The document does not provide a complete transcript of his views. It does not allow a historian to reconstruct every private conversation or every accusation made before the sentence. The public act is known. The exact charges behind it are not.

Spinoza then lived outside the structures that had shaped his early life. He worked with lenses and moved through the Dutch Republic. At Rijnsburg, and later at Voorburg, he wrote, revised, and corresponded. His friends and supporters formed part of a small intellectual network that included Henry Oldenburg, secretary of the Royal Society, and other readers of natural philosophy.

The Treatise emerged from that world of letters.

Its publication coincided with political violence. Johan de Witt, the leading statesman of the Dutch Republic, had been murdered in sixteen seventy two, along with his brother Cornelis. The killings shocked the republic and marked the collapse of the political order associated with Johan de Witt.

A story later attached Spinoza directly to the aftermath. Steven Nadler reports that Spinoza wanted to post a sign near the place of the murder calling the killers the last barbarians. His friends are said to have prevented him from doing so because the act might have exposed him to danger.

The account is attributed, not established by a surviving record of Spinoza carrying the sign. It fits the political anger associated with his defense of republican government, but historians must distinguish the remembered anecdote from documented action.

The danger was real enough without the sign. The States of Holland's ban on the Treatise showed that political authorities regarded the book as more than a private theological essay. Religious criticism, political criticism, and the question of public obedience met in the same pages.

In sixteen seventy six, Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz visited Spinoza. The meeting took place after the Treatise had circulated and while Spinoza was working on the Ethics. Leibniz was interested in philosophy and mathematics, but the encounter also carried political and religious tension. He later distanced himself from Spinoza's positions.

The two men did not leave a complete record of their conversation. Their surviving correspondence and later reports show a relationship shaped by caution. Leibniz recognized Spinoza's intellectual force while rejecting his conclusions about God, substance, and religion.

The visit belonged to the final period of Spinoza's life. He had moved to The Hague, where he lived quietly and continued to write. His health was fragile, and the fine glass dust associated with lens grinding may have affected his lungs, although the exact medical cause of his death cannot be established with certainty.

Spinoza died in sixteen seventy seven at The Hague.

The Ethics had not appeared during his lifetime. After his death, friends arranged the publication of his works. The volume carried the initials B. d. S. and included the Ethics, the Treatise on the Improvement of the Understanding, the Political Treatise, letters, and other writings. The Theological-Political Treatise had already circulated separately, but the posthumous collection gave his philosophy a fuller public form.

The title A Book Forged in Hell was not Spinoza's own. Steven Nadler uses the phrase from hostile responses to the book to describe the intensity of its reception. The expression belongs to the history of opposition, not to Spinoza's description of his work.

The condemnation had several sources. The book challenged traditional biblical authorship. It described prophecy through historical and psychological conditions. It treated ceremonies as political institutions. It defended freedom to philosophize. It denied that religious authorities should control the state.

Each claim could be read as a threat to a different authority. Taken together, they appeared to some contemporaries as an attack on religion itself.

Spinoza's position was more carefully divided. He distinguished philosophical truth from the practical obedience demanded by religion. He argued that Scripture's central teaching concerned justice and charity. He did not make the Treatise a manual for private disbelief. He made it an argument about how a state and its citizens should live amid different beliefs.

That distinction did not reassure the synods or the magistrates who opposed the book. A work could support public morality while rejecting the authority claimed by religious institutions. It could speak of God while denying that Scripture supplied a complete philosophy. It could defend religion's ethical purpose while limiting its political power.

The cherem and the later condemnation belonged to different communities and different decades. The first separated Spinoza from Amsterdam's Talmud Torah congregation in sixteen fifty six. The second targeted a printed book in the Dutch Republic. Neither event can be reduced to a single phrase such as persecution for one isolated idea.

The records show institutions reacting to a body of claims whose implications reached beyond one doctrine. The exact private path from conversation to condemnation remains partly lost. The public path is visible: expulsion, anonymous publication, synodal attack, state prohibition, and posthumous dissemination.

Spinoza's friends ensured that his books survived the official response. They did not turn him into a prophet in the biblical sense. They made his arguments available for later readers, who could accept, revise, or reject them.

Among those readers were scholars who moved the question of biblical authorship into a new historical discipline.

Richard Simon was among the first major scholars to extend the problem of biblical authorship into a sustained study of scribes, transmitters, and editors. In the seventeen century, he argued that the text of the Pentateuch had passed through the work of interpreters and guardians of tradition. He did not simply repeat Spinoza. Simon remained committed to Christian theology, yet his attention to textual transmission helped make authorship a historical question.

The question then moved through different methods.

Jean Astruc, a French physician, observed recurring divine names and patterns in Genesis. In seventeen fifty three, he proposed that Moses had used earlier documents in composing the book. Astruc preserved Mosaic authorship while explaining differences within the text through sources.

His proposal shifted the debate. The issue was no longer only whether Moses had written the Pentateuch. Scholars began asking what written materials might lie behind the final composition.

In the nineteenth century, source criticism developed more elaborate models. Julius Wellhausen presented a documentary account in Prolegomena to the History of Israel, published in eighteen seventy eight. The theory identified major literary strata, commonly designated by letters, and arranged them in a historical sequence.

The classic documentary hypothesis treated the Pentateuch as the result of several sources and later editorial work. It gave particular weight to differences in vocabulary, divine names, law collections, narrative style, and theological emphasis.

Wellhausen's model did not simply prove Spinoza right. It replaced the single compiler associated with Ezra with a more complex history of composition. It also placed the material within changing institutions and political periods.

Modern scholars continue to disagree about the precise sources and dates.

Richard Elliott Friedman argues that the Pentateuch reflects identifiable authors and editors whose work can be related to the history of Israel and Judah. In Who Wrote the Bible?, he presents a developed version of source criticism and assigns substantial material to particular literary traditions. His account supports the broad claim that the Pentateuch has multiple hands, while differing from Spinoza about the number, identity, and sequence of those hands.

Joel S. Baden defends a documentary approach in The Composition of the Pentateuch. He argues that the major sources possess enough continuity and distinctive structure to be read as coherent documents, later combined by editors. Baden's position differs from theories that treat the Pentateuch chiefly as a gradual accumulation of small fragments or layers.

Other scholars dispute the boundaries of the sources or the confidence with which they can be reconstructed. They examine redaction, oral tradition, scribal expansion, and the possibility that a text developed through several kinds of transmission rather than through four complete documents. The debate concerns method as much as conclusion.

The Decalogue itself demonstrates the difficulty.

Exodus chapter twenty contains the familiar sequence of commands concerning other gods, images, the divine name, the Sabbath, parents, killing, adultery, theft, false witness, and coveting. Deuteronomy chapter five repeats the sequence with differences in wording and explanation. The Sabbath command gives the most visible contrast: creation in Exodus, liberation from Egypt in Deuteronomy.

Exodus chapter thirty four presents another set of commands that scholars often call the ritual Decalogue. It includes rules concerning festivals, unleavened bread, firstborn animals, the Sabbath, and sacrificial practice. The passage is introduced in the context of renewed tablets after the breaking of the first tablets.

The existence of these passages complicates any simple picture of one fixed list delivered once and copied without variation. The word Decalogue is itself a later descriptive term for a group of ten statements. The text contains more than one passage organized around a numbered or covenantal set of commands.

Spinoza did not possess the methods or comparative evidence used by modern source critics. He did see that Exodus and Deuteronomy differed, and he recognized that such differences mattered for authorship and transmission.

Modern scholarship agrees with him at the broadest level. The Pentateuch reached its present form through a history of composition. It contains materials from different periods and literary hands. Moses cannot be treated as the sole author of the complete text in the straightforward sense rejected by Spinoza.

Modern scholarship generally does not agree with his suggestion that Ezra alone compiled the Pentateuch. Some scholars assign an important role to postexilic editors, and Ezra remains relevant to discussions of textual authority and the restoration of the community. But the evidence does not establish Ezra as the single compiler of the Pentateuch.

The difference is methodological. Spinoza inferred a compiler from the text's historical distance. Later scholars used comparative manuscripts, linguistic analysis, archaeology, ancient Near Eastern parallels, and theories of source relationships. Their reconstructions remain disputed, but they work with evidence unavailable in seventeenth-century Amsterdam.

The dates are also contested. Scholars disagree about when particular laws, narratives, and editions were composed. Some place important priestly material in the exilic or postexilic period. Others assign greater antiquity to parts of the legal collections or emphasize long processes of oral and written development. No single chronology has ended the debate.

The ritual Decalogue shows why certainty has limits. Its relationship to Exodus chapter twenty is interpreted in different ways. It may preserve an older festival-centered collection, a distinct covenant tradition, or a literary reshaping of earlier materials. The passage cannot be treated as a simple duplicate of the better-known commands.

Spinoza's achievement lies less in the accuracy of every proposed solution than in the kind of question he asked. He read the Bible as a historical collection whose words bore traces of time, place, purpose, and editorial arrangement.

That question survived changes in scholarship. Simon developed the problem of transmission. Astruc looked for documentary sources. Wellhausen built a historical model. Friedman and Baden defend competing forms of modern source criticism. None has restored the Pentateuch to a single moment or produced unanimous agreement about its composition.

The surviving record establishes what Spinoza argued in the Theological-Political Treatise.

He challenged the claim that Moses had written the complete Pentateuch. He drew attention to the account of Moses' death, the references to events continuing unto this day, and the different forms of the commandments in Exodus and Deuteronomy. He used Ibn Ezra's hints, and he considered Ezra a possible later compiler. Hobbes and Isaac La Peyrere had helped create the wider intellectual setting in which such arguments could be made.

Spinoza also argued that the laws of Moses belonged to the ancient Hebrew state. They organized a people, ordered public life, and served political purposes. The divine law he valued most directly concerned knowledge and love of God, justice, and charity. In his account, the written commandments were historical laws given through Moses, not timeless words dictated in the exact form later readers received. His philosophical God remained part of that account, though not as a human king issuing decrees.

Spinoza met Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz in sixteen seventy six, died at The Hague in sixteen seventy seven, and left friends to publish the Ethics and other writings after his death.

The record cannot establish the complete private charges behind the cherem. The notice survives, but it does not preserve every conversation or every belief attributed to him. His inner faith cannot be recovered with certainty from the public documents. His language about God belonged both to philosophy and to controversy, and later readers have drawn different conclusions from it.

The record cannot establish the original moment when the commandments themselves arose. Exodus and Deuteronomy preserve different forms. Exodus chapter thirty four contains another ritual Decalogue. Archaeology and comparative history illuminate the ancient societies in which such laws developed, but they do not provide a transcript from Sinai or identify one individual origin for every command.

Modern scholarship supports Spinoza's broad historical insight. The Pentateuch is a composite work shaped by multiple hands and periods. Scholars disagree about sources, dates, oral traditions, and editorial stages. Most do not accept Ezra as the single author or compiler of the complete collection.
